            <h1>Historical and Dialectical Materialism: From Medieval Society to the Present</h1>
            <div class="byline">
                <span>By <strong>Glenn Junsay Pansensoy</strong></span>
                <span>Published November 1, 2026</span>
                <span><a href="https://poetic-codes.pages.dev" target="_blank" rel="noopener">poetic-codes.pages.dev</a></span>
                <span><a href="https://twitter.com/PoeticGlenn" target="_blank" rel="noopener">@PoeticGlenn</a></span>
                <span><a href="mailto:pansensoyglenn150@gmail.com">pansensoyglenn150@gmail.com</a></span>
            </div>

        <main>
            <p>Today, I want us to look at history not simply as a list of kings, wars, dates, and empires, but as a living process shaped by how people produce, survive, work, trade, and struggle for a better life. This is where historical and dialectical materialism become useful. They invite us to ask deeper questions: Who owns the land? Who controls production? Who works, and who benefits from that work? How do economic systems rise, weaken, and eventually give way to something new?</p>

            <p>If we follow this way of thinking, the story of humanity from medieval times to the present becomes clearer. We begin in a world dominated by feudalism, where land, hierarchy, and tradition organized society. Then we move into the age of exploration and colonialism, when European powers expanded across the globe, extracted wealth, enslaved millions, and connected distant continents into one unequal system. From there, we witness the rise of industrial capitalism, the growth of factories and wage labor, the spread of imperialism, and the emergence of the modern global economy.</p>

            <p>But this is not only a story of powerful rulers and wealthy classes. It is also a story of peasants, workers, enslaved people, colonized communities, women, and ordinary people who resisted, adapted, organized, and fought for dignity and freedom. History, from this perspective, moves through contradictions. Old systems contain the seeds of their own transformation, and new forces emerge from the tensions of the old.</p>

            <p>So in this episode, we will trace that long journey: from medieval feudal society, through colonial expansion and the birth of capitalism, into industrialization, imperialism, decolonization, and the capitalist world we live in today. Our goal is not merely to memorize events, but to understand the material forces and human struggles that shaped them.</p>

            <h2>Historical and Dialectical Materialism: From Medieval Society to the Present</h2>
            <p>Seen through historical materialism, human history since the medieval period is a story of changing ways of producing life, shifting class relations, and conflicts between old institutions and new economic forces. Feudalism gave way to colonial expansion and capitalism; capitalism then became a global system marked by industrialization, imperialism, crisis, and continuing struggles over labor, land, and power.</p>

            <h2>Medieval Europe: Feudalism</h2>
            <p>In much of medieval Europe, the dominant mode of production was <strong>feudalism</strong>. Land was the principal source of wealth, and a hierarchy of monarchs, nobles, and clergy controlled territory while peasants and serfs worked the land in exchange for protection, access to plots, or obligations of labor and rent.</p>

            <p>The feudal order rested on direct control over people and land. Lords extracted surplus from peasants through rents, dues, and unpaid labor, while religion and custom justified hierarchy as natural or divinely ordered. In dialectical terms, the stability of feudal society contained its own tensions: growing trade, towns, money economies, and agricultural improvements gradually strained the rigid obligations of lordship and serfdom.</p>

            <h2>The transition from feudalism</h2>
            <p>Between the late medieval period and the early modern era, commerce expanded across Europe, Asia, the Mediterranean, and eventually the Atlantic world. Towns grew, merchants gained influence, and new technologies and financial practices made production and exchange more mobile than feudal land-based relations could easily contain.</p>

            <p>The Black Death also transformed class relations. Massive population loss weakened serfdom in some regions, increased peasants' bargaining power, and allowed some cultivators to accumulate land or lease larger holdings. In England, these shifts contributed to the decline of serfdom and helped create conditions for agrarian capitalism.</p>

            <p>From a historical-materialist perspective, the transition was not simply the result of new inventions or brave entrepreneurs. It involved a restructuring of property relations: land, labor, and money were increasingly organized around profit, markets, wages, and private accumulation rather than customary obligations and feudal privilege.</p>

            <h2>Colonialism and primitive accumulation</h2>
            <p>The age of colonialism, beginning especially with European overseas expansion after 1492, became central to the rise of capitalism. European powers seized land, extracted precious metals and raw materials, established plantation economies, and forced millions of Africans into the Atlantic slave trade.</p>

            <p>Marx called part of this process <strong>primitive accumulation</strong>: the violent separation of people from their means of subsistence and the concentration of wealth in the hands of emerging capitalist classes. Enclosures in Europe dispossessed many peasants from common land, while conquest and slavery abroad supplied capital, markets, raw materials, and coerced labor.</p>

            <p>Colonialism was therefore not merely an overseas political project. It was an economic system that linked European factories, ports, banks, and states to plantations, mines, and colonized labor systems across the world. This global network helped finance industrial development in Europe while restricting or reshaping economic development in colonized regions.</p>

            <h2>The rise of industrial capitalism</h2>
            <p>By the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, capitalism became increasingly industrial. Machinery, factories, coal, steam power, and wage labor created a new class structure: capitalists owned factories, land, machines, and capital, while workers sold their labor for wages.</p>

            <p>This was a dialectical transformation. Capitalism destroyed many feudal restrictions and created enormous productive capacity, but it also generated new contradictions. Workers became dependent on wages, wealth became highly concentrated, and production for profit produced recurring crises of overproduction, unemployment, and competition.</p>

            <p>The industrial revolution also changed politics. Bourgeois classes challenged monarchies and aristocracies in the name of property, contracts, free markets, and representative government. At the same time, industrial workers organized unions, political movements, and socialist parties to contest exploitation and demand better wages, hours, and working conditions.</p>

            <h2>Imperialism and the global capitalist system</h2>
            <p>In the late nineteenth century, industrial capitalism entered a more openly imperialist phase. Industrial powers competed for colonies, markets, raw materials, ports, and investment opportunities across Africa, Asia, the Pacific, and the Americas.</p>

            <p>For historical materialists, imperialism reflected capitalism's need for expansion: capitalists sought cheaper inputs, new consumers, profitable investments, and strategic control over trade routes. Colonized societies were often forced into export economies—producing sugar, rubber, cotton, minerals, or cash crops—while their local industries were suppressed or subordinated to imperial interests.</p>

            <p>This created a global division of labor: industrialized imperial centers accumulated capital and technological capacity, while colonized and semi-colonized regions supplied labor, land, and raw materials. The result was not a uniform world, but a hierarchical world system shaped by unequal exchange, racial domination, and political control.</p>

            <h2>The twentieth century: Crisis, war, and decolonization</h2>
            <p>The twentieth century intensified capitalism's contradictions. World wars, the Great Depression, fascism, labor struggles, socialist revolutions, and decolonization all reflected conflicts over property, state power, and the distribution of wealth.</p>

            <p>After 1945, many colonies won formal independence, but the economic structures built under colonialism often persisted. New states inherited export dependence, debt, unequal trade relations, and borders drawn by imperial powers. Marxist and anti-colonial thinkers therefore argued that political independence did not automatically end economic domination.</p>

            <p>At the same time, capitalist countries developed welfare states, regulated markets, and mass consumer economies in response to worker movements, depression, and geopolitical competition. These reforms softened some inequalities but did not eliminate the basic capitalist relationship between those who own productive property and those who must sell labor.</p>

            <h2>Capitalism now</h2>
            <p>Today, capitalism operates as a global system of multinational corporations, financial markets, digital platforms, global supply chains, and international institutions. Production is dispersed across countries, while ownership, finance, and technological infrastructure remain concentrated in powerful firms and states.</p>

            <p>Contemporary contradictions include:</p>
            <ul>
                <li><strong>Inequality:</strong> Wealth and ownership remain concentrated even as labor becomes more global and precarious.</li>
                <li><strong>Crisis:</strong> Financial instability, debt, unemployment, and overproduction continue to recur.</li>
                <li><strong>Labor conflict:</strong> Gig work, automation, outsourcing, and weakened unions create new struggles over wages and security.</li>
                <li><strong>Ecological limits:</strong> Capitalism's drive for growth and profit conflicts with the finite resources and stability of the natural world.</li>
                <li><strong>Imperial competition:</strong> Trade wars, sanctions, resource competition, and military alliances continue to reflect struggles over global markets and influence.</li>
            </ul>

            <h2>A dialectical reading of the present</h2>
            <p>Dialectical materialism would not describe the present as a fixed endpoint. It would examine capitalism as a system full of internal tensions: between social production and private ownership, between global cooperation and national competition, between technological possibility and unequal access, and between endless growth and ecological limits.</p>

            <p>From this perspective, historical change occurs when these contradictions become strong enough to transform institutions, class relations, and modes of production. Whether that transformation leads to more democratic and sustainable forms of social organization—or to deeper inequality, conflict, and environmental crisis—remains an open historical question.</p>
