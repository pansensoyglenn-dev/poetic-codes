            <h1>The Ilonggo Journey: From Landless Shores to the Building of Mindanao</h1>
            <div class="byline">
                <span>By <strong>Glenn Junsay Pansensoy</strong></span>
                <span>Published November 1, 2026</span>
                <span><a href="https://poetic-codes.pages.dev" target="_blank" rel="noopener">poetic-codes.pages.dev</a></span>
                <span><a href="https://twitter.com/PoeticGlenn" target="_blank" rel="noopener">@PoeticGlenn</a></span>
                <span><a href="mailto:pansensoyglenn150@gmail.com">pansensoyglenn150@gmail.com</a></span>
            </div>
        </header>

        <main>
            <p>The Ilonggo migration from Iloilo, Negros Occidental, Capiz, Antique, Guimaras, and other parts of Western Visayas was one of the great internal movements that reshaped Mindanao and Palawan. It was not simply a movement of people seeking a better place to live. It was a movement of families searching for land, food, dignity, and a future that seemed impossible in the places where they were born.</p>

            <p>For generations, many Ilonggos lived in a homeland rich in culture, language, food, and history, yet constrained by inequality. Good land belonged to powerful families. Coastal waters became increasingly crowded and depleted. Work was seasonal, wages were low, and opportunities for young people were limited. When stories reached them of vast lands in Mindanao, fertile valleys in Cotabato, fishing grounds in Sarangani Bay, and coastal areas suitable for fishponds, many families decided to take the risk of migration.</p>

            <p>They traveled by boat, truck, and foot. They carried little: clothing, cooking pots, tools, seeds, fishing nets, photographs, and memories of home. But they carried something even more important—the Ilonggo values of patience, discipline, thrift, family cooperation, and hard work. In the decades that followed, these values helped transform frontier areas into rice fields, fishing ports, market towns, and progressive cities.</p>

            <p>The Ilonggo story is therefore a story of survival, achievement, and nation-building. It is also a story that must be told honestly, because the growth of Mindanao came with environmental costs and social tensions. Forests were cleared. Mangroves were converted into fishponds. Indigenous peoples and Muslim communities sometimes lost land and political power. Yet without denying these realities, it is also true that Ilonggo migrants helped build General Santos City, Kidapawan, Tacurong, and many communities across Zamboanga Sibugay and other parts of Mindanao.</p>

            <h2>Why They Left the Visayas</h2>
            <p>The first reason many Ilonggos left home was the land problem. In Negros Occidental, the rise of the sugar industry created enormous wealth, but that wealth was concentrated in the hands of a few haciendero families. Vast sugar plantations covered the plains, while many farm workers had no land of their own. They worked as tenants, laborers, or seasonal <em>sacadas</em>, moving from one plantation to another during harvest time.</p>

            <p>A worker might spend his whole life cutting cane, planting rice, or tending livestock, yet still be unable to buy even a small piece of land. His children often faced the same future. Poverty was not always caused by laziness or lack of effort; it was built into a system in which the best land was controlled by a small elite.</p>

            <p>Iloilo faced a different but related problem. In the nineteenth century, Iloilo was a major center of trade and textile production. Its port connected Panay to Manila, Europe, and other parts of Asia. But as machine-made textiles entered the Philippines and the sugar economy shifted toward Negros, Iloilo’s old industries weakened. Many people lost livelihoods. Land became more expensive, jobs became scarce, and families looked outward for survival.</p>

            <p>Coastal communities faced another pressure: the sea itself was changing. In many fishing villages, more people depended on the same narrow coastal waters. Fishers used finer nets, more boats, and more efficient methods. As population grew and fishing pressure increased, catches declined. A fisherman who once could feed his family near shore might have to travel farther, spend more on fuel, and return with less.</p>

            <p>For families living near the shore, the crisis was double. They had little or no farmland, and the sea could no longer guarantee enough food. When overfishing combined with poverty, landlessness, and lack of opportunity, migration became not a choice but a necessity.</p>

            <h2>The Promise of Mindanao</h2>
            <p>Mindanao was promoted by the government as the “Land of Promise.” It had wide plains, rivers, forests, grasslands, and coastal areas. It appeared to offer what Western Visayas could not: land for farming, space for new communities, and opportunities for families willing to work.</p>

            <p>Government settlement programs encouraged migration, especially during the American colonial period and after the Second World War. Roads, schools, agricultural stations, and land settlement projects opened new areas to migrants. The most famous early settlement effort in the south was led by General Paulino Santos, who brought settlers to the shores of Sarangani Bay in 1939. This settlement became the foundation of what is now General Santos City.</p>

            <p>For Ilonggo families, Mindanao represented hope. A farmer without land in Iloilo or Negros could dream of owning a rice field in Cotabato. A fisherman with empty nets could dream of richer fishing grounds in Sarangani Bay. A laborer tired of working for others could dream of having his own farm, his own house, and his own future.</p>

            <p>But migration was difficult. The journey was long and expensive. Settlers arrived in unfamiliar places with different climates, soils, diseases, languages, and peoples. They had to build houses from wood, bamboo, and cogon grass. They had to clear land, dig wells, plant crops, and protect themselves from floods, droughts, pests, and isolation.</p>

            <p>They also entered lands that were not empty. Mindanao was home to Lumad peoples, Maguindanaons, Maranaos, Tausugs, and other indigenous and Muslim communities. These groups had their own ancestral domains, political systems, cultures, and claims to the land. In some places, settlers and original inhabitants lived peacefully and intermarried. In others, competition for land led to displacement, conflict, and injustice.</p>

            <p>The Ilonggo migration must therefore be understood with compassion but also with honesty. The migrants were often poor people seeking survival. Yet the expansion of settlement also contributed to the loss of forests and the marginalization of communities that had lived in Mindanao long before the migrants arrived.</p>

            <h2>Building General Santos City</h2>
            <p>General Santos City is one of the clearest examples of how migration transformed Mindanao. Before it became a bustling city, it was a coastal frontier near Sarangani Bay. In 1939, General Paulino Santos led the first major group of settlers to the area under the National Land Settlement Administration.</p>

            <p>The early settlers faced a hard beginning. They cleared forests and grasslands, built homes, planted crops, and established communities. They battled mosquitoes, wild animals, difficult soil, and the loneliness of being far from their original hometowns. Yet they persisted.</p>

            <p>As more settlers arrived, General Santos grew. Ilonggos, Cebuanos, Ilocanos, Tagalogs, and other groups contributed to its development. Ilonggos became especially visible in farming, trading, fishing, and local commerce. They brought with them the Hiligaynon language, Ilonggo food traditions, family values, and a strong work ethic.</p>

            <p>The city’s location gave it a major advantage. It faced Sarangani Bay and had access to the Sulu Sea, Moro Gulf, Celebes Sea, and the rich tuna grounds of the western Pacific. Over time, General Santos developed into the “Tuna Capital of the Philippines.” Fish port facilities, cold-storage plants, canneries, ice plants, boat yards, and trading companies grew around the fishing industry.</p>

            <p>The growth of General Santos was not the work of one group alone. It was built by settlers, fishers, traders, workers, indigenous peoples, Muslim communities, and local leaders. But the Ilonggo presence became an important part of its identity. In markets, neighborhoods, churches, schools, and fishing communities, the sound of Hiligaynon became part of the city’s everyday life.</p>

            <h2>Kidapawan and the Cotabato Frontier</h2>
            <p>Kidapawan, located at the foot of Mount Apo, also became a major destination for migrants. The area was once covered with forests and inhabited by indigenous Lumad communities. As roads opened and agricultural lands expanded, settlers from Luzon and the Visayas arrived to farm.</p>

            <p>Ilonggo families helped develop rice fields, cornlands, coconut farms, fruit orchards, and livestock-raising areas. They introduced farming practices from Western Visayas and adapted them to Mindanao’s environment. They built homes, organized neighborhoods, supported schools, and participated in local trade.</p>

            <p>The transformation of Kidapawan and surrounding areas shows both the power and the cost of frontier agriculture. Forests were cut for timber and cleared for farms. Watersheds were affected. Wildlife habitats shrank. Indigenous communities faced pressure as outsiders claimed lands that had once been part of their ancestral territories.</p>

            <p>At the same time, the creation of rice fields and farming communities helped feed growing populations. Rice became a central crop in many settled areas. What was once forest or grassland became productive agricultural land. This transformation gave jobs and food to many families, but it also changed the relationship between people and the land.</p>

            <p>The story of Kidapawan is therefore not only a story of progress. It is also a reminder that development must respect the rights of indigenous peoples, protect watersheds, and preserve the ecological balance that makes agriculture possible in the first place.</p>

            <h2>From Forest to Rice Fields</h2>
            <p>One of the most visible results of Ilonggo migration was the conversion of forested and uncultivated areas into rice fields. In Cotabato, South Cotabato, Sultan Kudarat, and other parts of Mindanao, settlers cleared land, built irrigation canals, planted rice, and created farming communities.</p>

            <p>Rice was more than a crop. It was food security, income, and a symbol of stability. A family that owned or cultivated a rice field had a measure of independence. It could eat from its own harvest, sell surplus grain, and plan for the future.</p>

            <p>Ilonggo farmers were known for their patience and attention to detail. They learned the seasons, watched the weather, and worked long hours under the sun. They planted, weeded, harvested, dried, and milled rice. They raised carabaos, chickens, pigs, and goats. They saved money little by little, often sending children to school so the next generation could have opportunities they never had.</p>

            <p>The transformation of forests into rice fields created prosperity, but it also created environmental problems. Forests protect soil, store water, prevent floods, and support biodiversity. When they are removed too quickly or without planning, rivers become silted, floods become worse, and droughts become more damaging.</p>

            <p>Today, the challenge is to continue producing food while restoring forests, protecting watersheds, and practicing sustainable farming. Agroforestry, organic farming, soil conservation, and watershed protection can help ensure that the rice fields built by earlier generations remain productive for the future.</p>

            <h2>The Ilonggo Way at Sea</h2>
            <p>For many Ilonggo migrants, the coast was not merely a place to live. It was a workplace, a source of food, and a path out of poverty. In Mindanao and Palawan, they combined fishing, fish trading, and coastal farming to build livelihoods in places where land alone could not sustain every family.</p>

            <p>General Santos became a major destination because of its access to rich fishing grounds. The city faced Sarangani Bay and was close to the Sulu Sea, Moro Gulf, and Celebes Sea. These waters became highways for fishing boats, traders, and workers.</p>

            <p>Ilonggo and other Visayan settlers brought fishing knowledge, boat-handling skills, and market experience to Mindanao’s coastal villages. They knew how to read the weather, mend nets, preserve fish, and sell their catch. They also brought the habit of cooperation: neighbors helped each other launch boats, share equipment, and divide the work of fishing and processing.</p>

            <p>Small-scale fishers supplied local markets, dried-fish makers, and traders. Larger operators pursued tuna, which became the foundation of General Santos’ export economy. In between were fish vendors, net makers, boat crews, ice-plant workers, cannery workers, and families who processed fish through salting, drying, and fermentation.</p>

            <p>The fishing industry created a whole chain of livelihoods. A single catch of tuna could support boat owners, crews, port workers, buyers, traders, ice suppliers, mechanics, market vendors, cannery workers, and exporters. Fish did not only feed families; it built an economy.</p>

            <h2>Overfishing and the Shrinking Sea</h2>
            <p>But the success of fishing also created pressure on the sea. As more people settled along the coast and more boats entered the water, fish stocks declined. Small fishers found that they had to travel farther from shore to catch enough fish. Species that were once common near the coast became harder to find.</p>

            <p>In General Santos, many small-scale fishers now travel hours from shore to reach productive fishing grounds. This means higher fuel costs, longer working hours, and greater danger. A fisherman may leave before dawn and return late in the day, only to earn barely enough to feed his family.</p>

            <p>Overfishing affects everyone, but it affects the poor most. Large commercial vessels have capital, bigger boats, advanced equipment, and access to distant fishing grounds. Small fishers depend on nearshore waters, simple boats, and daily catches. When fish stocks decline, the small fisher suffers first.</p>

            <p>Coastal communities also face pollution, habitat destruction, and climate change. Coral reefs are damaged by destructive fishing and warming waters. Mangroves are cut for ponds and settlements. Seagrass beds are affected by pollution and coastal development. When these habitats disappear, fish lose their breeding and feeding grounds.</p>

            <p>The sea that once offered unlimited opportunity now demands careful management. The future of coastal communities depends on protecting fish habitats, regulating fishing, supporting small fishers, and teaching the next generation that the ocean is not a warehouse but a living system.</p>

            <h2>Fishponds in Zamboanga Sibugay</h2>
            <p>Along the bays and river mouths of Mindanao, settlers and entrepreneurs developed brackish-water fishponds for milkfish, shrimp, and mud crabs. Zamboanga Sibugay became especially known for these coastal farms. Its wide mudflats, tidal waters, and mangrove areas made it suitable for aquaculture.</p>

            <p>Milkfish, known locally as <em>bangus</em>, became one of the most important products. Fishponds were stocked with milkfish fry, fed with natural food and supplemental feeds, and harvested for local markets and traders. Mud crabs, often called “king crabs” because of their size and value, became another major product. They were raised in ponds or pens and sold to buyers in Mindanao, the Visayas, Luzon, and even export markets.</p>

            <p>For Ilonggo families, fishponds offered something that landlessness had denied them: a form of production they could manage, expand, and pass on. A pond could feed a household, employ relatives, and create income through harvesting, buying, transporting, and selling fish.</p>

            <p>Fishpond work required skill and patience. Farmers had to control water levels, watch salinity, prevent diseases, feed the fish, and guard against theft. Harvesting was often a community event, with neighbors helping pull nets and sort the catch. The work was hard, but it gave many families a sense of ownership and possibility.</p>

            <p>Mud-crab farming became especially important because crabs command high prices. A good harvest could provide income for school fees, house repairs, boats, nets, and daily needs. Crab farming also created work for gatherers, caretakers, buyers, traders, and transport workers.</p>

            <h2>The Price of Coastal Change</h2>
            <p>But coastal farming also changed the shoreline. Many fishponds were carved out of mangrove forests. Mangroves are among the most valuable ecosystems in the Philippines. They protect coasts from storms and waves, trap sediment, prevent erosion, store carbon, and serve as nurseries for fish, crabs, shrimp, and other marine life.</p>

            <p>When mangroves are cleared for fishponds, these benefits disappear. Coastal communities become more vulnerable to typhoons and storm surges. Fish and crab populations decline because their breeding grounds are destroyed. Water quality suffers as ponds release waste and chemicals.</p>

            <p>In General Santos and nearby Sarangani, the aquaculture boom of the late 1980s and early 1990s led to the conversion of mangrove forests into fishponds and hatcheries. Similar patterns occurred in many coastal areas of the Philippines. The desire for quick income and food production sometimes overcame the need to protect the environment.</p>

            <p>This does not mean that fishponds are always bad. Aquaculture can feed people and create livelihoods. But it must be done responsibly. Ponds should not destroy remaining mangroves. Abandoned or unproductive ponds should be restored. Farmers should use environmentally friendly methods. Communities should be involved in deciding how coastal lands are used.</p>

            <h2>A More Sustainable Future</h2>
            <p>The next stage of the Ilonggo coastal story should not reject fishing or fishponds. Fishing and aquaculture remain essential sources of food and income. But they must be made fairer and more sustainable.</p>

            <p>Mangrove restoration is one important step. Planting mangroves in abandoned ponds, coastal flats, and degraded areas can bring back fish habitats and protect communities from storms. Restoration should involve local residents, fishers, students, local governments, and environmental groups.</p>

            <p>Regulated fishing is another step. Local governments and fisher organizations can protect spawning grounds, control destructive fishing methods, and ensure that small fishers have access to traditional fishing areas. Marine protected areas, seasonal closures, and community-based management can help fish stocks recover.</p>

            <p>Support for small fishers is also necessary. They need access to affordable fuel, safe boats, cold storage, fair prices, credit, insurance, and training. Without support, they will continue to be squeezed between rising costs and declining catches.</p>

            <p>Mud-crab farming offers one example of a better balance. Instead of clearing mangroves for ponds, farmers can raise crabs in pens within healthy mangrove areas. This method can be profitable while preserving the ecosystem that supports crabs, fish, and coastal protection.</p>

            <p>Other sustainable livelihoods include seaweed farming, oyster farming, grouper cages, mangrove-friendly aquasilviculture, and value-added fish processing. These activities can diversify income and reduce pressure on wild fish stocks.</p>

            <h2>Ilonggo Values and the Future</h2>
            <p>The Ilonggo migrants who settled in Mindanao and Palawan did not build their communities through luck. They built them through work. They woke early, planted rice, repaired nets, raised children, saved money, helped neighbors, and endured hardship. They turned unfamiliar places into homes.</p>

            <p>Their values remain important today. Hard work must be paired with justice. Progress must be paired with sustainability. Ownership must be paired with respect for the rights of others. The same determination that helped Ilonggos build new lives in Mindanao can now help build a future where land, sea, and people are protected.</p>

            <p>The Ilonggo diaspora shows how people respond when land, fish, and opportunity are unavailable at home. They crossed seas, cleared frontiers, raised families, and helped turn places such as General Santos, Kidapawan, Tacurong, and communities across Zamboanga Sibugay into progressive agricultural and commercial centers.</p>

            <p>Their history is also a warning. When wealth is concentrated in few hands, when coastal resources are exhausted, and when land distribution is unjust, people will keep moving in search of a future. Migration is not the root problem; inequality is. If people had fair access to land, sustainable fisheries, decent jobs, and opportunities at home, fewer families would be forced to leave.</p>

            <p>The Ilonggos built much of what is now progressive Mindanao. Their rice fields feed cities. Their fishing boats supply markets. Their fishponds produce milkfish and crabs. Their markets, schools, churches, and family networks helped create communities from wilderness.</p>

            <p>But the next chapter must be different. It must ensure that growth is shared, sustainable, and respectful of the indigenous peoples and local communities who were there before the settlers arrived. It must protect the forests that give water to the rice fields, the mangroves that shelter crabs and fish, and the seas that feed coastal families.</p>

            <p>The Ilonggo journey began with hardship, but it became a story of building. Now it must become a story of stewardship—so that the land and sea that once gave migrants hope will continue to give hope to their children and grandchildren.</p>
