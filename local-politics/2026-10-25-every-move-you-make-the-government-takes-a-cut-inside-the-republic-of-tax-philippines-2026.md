        <h1 class="main-title">EVERY MOVE YOU MAKE, THE GOVERNMENT TAKES A CUT</h1>
        <h2 class="welcome">Welcome to the Republic of Tax Philippines — 2026 Edition</h2>

        <p>In this country, from the moment you are born until the day you die, there is a charge.</p>
        <p>You earn? Taxed.</p>
        <p>You spend? Taxed.</p>
        <p>You save? Even the interest is taxed.</p>
        <p>You build a home, buy a car, start a small business, send money to your family, sell land, win a raffle, order a package from abroad, subscribe to Netflix, or boost a Facebook post — there is a fee, a tax, a charge, a deduction, or a compliance requirement waiting somewhere.</p>
        <p>It can feel as if you are not merely a citizen.</p>
        <p class="strong-highlight">You are a walking revenue stream.<br>A source of collections.<br>A permanent contributor to a system that often seems far more efficient at taking from the people than at protecting, serving, and accounting to them.</p>

        <p>This is not an argument that taxes are inherently evil. Taxes build roads. Taxes pay teachers, nurses, soldiers, firefighters, and public servants. Taxes fund hospitals, schools, disaster response, courts, and social protection. A country without taxation cannot function.</p>
        <p><strong>But taxation without visible results is not citizenship.</strong></p>
        <p><strong>It is extraction.</strong></p>
        <p>And when public money disappears into questionable projects, inflated contracts, ghost infrastructure, and unexplained “confidential” spending, the taxpayer is not simply asked to contribute.</p>
        <p>The taxpayer is asked to finance his own betrayal.</p>
        <p>That is the anger behind this article.</p>
        <p>Not anger at paying taxes.</p>
        <p>Anger at the possibility that what we worked so hard for is becoming the luxury, the power, and the private business of the corrupt.</p>

        <h2>The First Cut: Your Salary</h2>
        <p>For most Filipinos, the relationship with the government begins with employment.</p>
        <p>You apply for a job. You pass the interview. You receive an offer. You celebrate because finally, there is income.</p>
        <p>Then payday arrives.</p>
        <p>Before the money reaches your hands, deductions have already been made.</p>
        <p>Income tax. Contributions. Loans. Sometimes union dues. Sometimes insurance. Sometimes mandatory deductions that feel less like protection and more like another monthly bill.</p>
        <p>Under the Philippine income-tax system, individual taxable income is taxed on a graduated scale, with the first PHP250,000 of annual taxable income exempt and rates rising up to 35 percent for the highest bracket.</p>
        <p>The government is not your employer.</p>
        <p>Yet it takes a share of your pay before you can even decide how to use it.</p>
        <p>And this is not merely about the tax itself. It is about what happens after.</p>
        <p>Your salary is supposed to become rice, rent, tuition, medicine, transportation, savings, and maybe — if there is anything left — a small comfort for your family.</p>
        <p>But when the cost of living rises, when wages remain low, when public hospitals are crowded, when classrooms are lacking, and when floodwaters enter homes despite billions spent on flood control, the taxpayer begins to ask a fair question:</p>
        <p class="highlight"><strong>Where did my contribution go?</strong></p>

        <h2>The Second Cut: Everything You Buy</h2>
        <p>You buy coffee, sardines, shampoo, soap, canned goods, rice, cooking oil, school supplies, or medicine.</p>
        <p>At the counter, the price is already higher than what you expected.</p>
        <p>Why?</p>
        <p><strong>Value-added tax.</strong></p>
        <p>The Philippines imposes a standard 12 percent VAT on the sale of goods, properties, and services. In practice, businesses often pass this tax on to consumers. You may not see a separate line item for every product, but the burden is real.</p>
        <p>Your grocery budget is already not enough.</p>
        <p>Yet there is still an invisible shopping companion taking a cut from every item.</p>
        <p>VAT is not unique to the Philippines. Many countries use it. But in a country where minimum wages are low, where food inflation hits poor families hardest, and where public services often fail to match the taxes collected, VAT becomes more than a fiscal tool.</p>
        <p>It becomes a daily reminder that even survival is monetized.</p>
        <p>You buy food to live.</p>
        <p>The government takes a share.</p>
        <p>You buy medicine to heal.</p>
        <p>The government takes a share.</p>
        <p>You buy school supplies so your child can learn.</p>
        <p>The government takes a share.</p>
        <p>And then, when the classroom is overcrowded or the public hospital has no available bed, the same government asks for patience.</p>

        <h2>The Third Cut: The Roof Over Your Head</h2>
        <p>You work for years. You save. You borrow. You sacrifice.</p>
        <p>Finally, you buy a house.</p>
        <p>But owning a home in the Philippines is not simply paying the seller.</p>
        <p>There are documentary stamp taxes. Transfer taxes. Registration fees. Notarial fees. Miscellaneous charges. And once the property is yours, real property tax begins.</p>
        <p>The house may be fully paid.</p>
        <p>But the obligation is a lifetime subscription.</p>
        <p>Every year, the local government sends a notice. Pay, or face penalties, interest, and possible foreclosure.</p>
        <p>This is not to say local governments do not need revenue. They do. Real property tax helps fund local services, roads, drainage, waste collection, and public facilities.</p>
        <p>But the frustration is understandable.</p>
        <p>You paid to acquire the house.</p>
        <p>You paid to transfer it.</p>
        <p>You paid to register it.</p>
        <p>Then you pay again every year simply to keep it.</p>
        <p>And when floods enter subdivisions, when drainage systems fail, when roads deteriorate, and when public services are slow, the homeowner is left asking:</p>
        <p class="highlight">If I am paying for this house every year, why does it still feel like the government is renting space in my life?</p>

        <h2>The Fourth Cut: The Car, the Fuel, and the Road</h2>
        <p>You own a car?</p>
        <p>Excise tax. Registration fees. Motor vehicle user’s charge. Insurance. Emissions testing. Inspection requirements. Penalties for late renewal.</p>
        <p>Every trip costs fuel.</p>
        <p>Every registration costs money.</p>
        <p>But the potholes on the road?</p>
        <p>Free.</p>
        <p>Unlimited.</p>
        <p>Fuel itself carries excise taxes. When fuel prices rise, transportation rises. Delivery costs rise. Food prices rise. Almost everything that moves in the economy rises.</p>
        <p>You do not need to own a car to be affected by fuel taxes.</p>
        <p>The jeepney driver feels it.</p>
        <p>The delivery rider feels it.</p>
        <p>The market vendor feels it.</p>
        <p>The farmer transporting produce feels it.</p>
        <p>The parent bringing a child to school feels it.</p>
        <p>When fuel becomes expensive, the entire chain of daily life becomes expensive.</p>
        <p>And yet, the roads remain uneven, the traffic remains brutal, and the infrastructure sometimes seems designed more for ribbon-cutting ceremonies than for the people who actually use it.</p>

        <h2>The Fifth Cut: Running a Business</h2>
        <p>You run a business?</p>
        <p>Income tax. VAT or percentage tax. Local business taxes. Mayor’s permit. Barangay clearance. Fire safety inspection. Sanitary permit. Environmental compliance. Renewals. Penalties. Inspections.</p>
        <p>You provide the capital.</p>
        <p>You lose the sleep.</p>
        <p>You absorb the losses.</p>
        <p>You pay your workers.</p>
        <p>You deal with suppliers, customers, delayed payments, rising costs, and competition.</p>
        <p>But when it is time to collect, it is as if the government is your silent business partner.</p>
        <p>The small sari-sari store owner, the carinderia operator, the online seller, the farmer, the tricycle driver, the freelancer, the startup founder — all encounter the state not only as a regulator, but as a collector.</p>
        <p>And the burden is not equal.</p>
        <p>Large corporations can hire accountants, lawyers, and tax consultants. They can structure transactions, avail of incentives, and navigate complex rules.</p>
        <p>The small entrepreneur often has no such luxury.</p>
        <p>For them, every permit is a cost.</p>
        <p>Every renewal is a hassle.</p>
        <p>Every penalty is a wound.</p>
        <p>Every tax deadline is another source of anxiety.</p>

        <h2>The Sixth Cut: Selling Online</h2>
        <p>You sell online — on Shopee, Lazada, TikTok Shop, Facebook Marketplace, Instagram, or your own website.</p>
        <p>You no longer need a physical store for the BIR to notice you.</p>
        <p>Online income is still income.</p>
        <p>Platform earnings, commissions, product sales, affiliate income, and brand deals can create tax obligations. Depending on the taxpayer’s situation, this may involve income tax, VAT or percentage tax, withholding tax, registration, invoicing, and record-keeping requirements.</p>
        <p>Even online, the government is very present in your income.</p>
        <p>This is not inherently unjust. If someone earns a living online, they should contribute fairly. Digital businesses should not be allowed to hide simply because they operate through a screen.</p>
        <p>But fairness requires proportion.</p>
        <p>It requires clear rules.</p>
        <p>It requires support for micro-sellers who are not experts in taxation.</p>
        <p>And it requires the same energy in pursuing large-scale tax evasion, smuggling, underdeclaration, and corrupt procurement that is used in monitoring ordinary online sellers.</p>
        <p>Because it is one thing to ask a small seller to comply.</p>
        <p>It is another thing to let billions in public funds vanish through questionable projects while the seller is threatened with penalties for a missed deadline.</p>

        <h2>The Seventh Cut: Content Creation</h2>
        <p>You are a content creator?</p>
        <p>Your monetization is taxable.</p>
        <p>Facebook Stars. Ad revenue. Brand deals. Affiliate commissions. Sponsored posts. YouTube income. TikTok rewards. Livestream donations. Product placements.</p>
        <p>You create the content.</p>
        <p>You edit it.</p>
        <p>You research it.</p>
        <p>You stay awake hoping it reaches people.</p>
        <p>You deal with algorithms, trolls, copyright claims, demonetization, and inconsistent payouts.</p>
        <p>But the moment the payout arrives, the BIR makes a guest appearance.</p>
        <p>Even memes can become a taxable profession.</p>
        <p>Now, even laughter has a receipt.</p>
        <p>Again — content creators should pay taxes on real income. That is fair. Many creators are self-employed professionals, not hobbyists.</p>
        <p>But the public deserves consistency.</p>
        <p>If the government can determine how much a content creator earned on Facebook, then the people should be even more able to know where PHP100 million in a government project went.</p>
        <p>If digital platforms can report earnings, then government procurement systems should be able to show budgets, contractors, project locations, completion status, and audit findings.</p>
        <p>Transparency should not be a privilege reserved for the Bureau of Internal Revenue.</p>
        <p>It should be a right of every citizen.</p>

        <h2>The Eighth Cut: Freelancers and Remote Workers</h2>
        <p>You are a freelancer, virtual assistant, graphic designer, writer, programmer, online consultant, translator, or remote employee?</p>
        <p>Income tax.</p>
        <p>Just because your client is foreign does not mean you are invisible.</p>
        <p>Even working from home, in your pajamas, with a laptop and an unstable internet connection, you still have compliance obligations.</p>
        <p>You may be earning in dollars, but you are living in the Philippines.</p>
        <p>You may be paid through PayPal, Wise, Payoneer, bank transfer, or crypto — but the income can still be taxable.</p>
        <p>The freelancer pays for equipment.</p>
        <p>The freelancer pays for electricity.</p>
        <p>The freelancer pays for internet.</p>
        <p>The freelancer pays for software.</p>
        <p>The freelancer absorbs the risk of delayed payments, canceled projects, and clients who disappear.</p>
        <p>And yet, when tax season arrives, the freelancer must organize receipts, compute income, file returns, and hope the rules are clear enough to avoid penalties.</p>
        <p>The digital economy has changed.</p>
        <p>The burden of compliance has not always become simpler.</p>

        <h2>The Ninth Cut: Digital Comfort</h2>
        <p>Netflix. Spotify. YouTube Premium. Canva. Adobe. ChatGPT. Cloud storage. Online games. Digital subscriptions.</p>
        <p>Twelve percent VAT.</p>
        <p>Even the movie you watch to temporarily forget the problems of the Philippines — the government takes a share.</p>
        <p>Even the song you play while commuting.</p>
        <p>Even the editing tool you use to create content.</p>
        <p>Even the software you need to work.</p>
        <p>The Philippines has moved to apply VAT to foreign digital service providers supplying consumers in the country.</p>
        <p>This may be fiscally logical. Digital services are part of the modern economy. Foreign platforms should not be permanently exempt while local businesses carry the tax burden.</p>
        <p>But again, the ordinary consumer feels it directly.</p>
        <p>Every subscription becomes more expensive.</p>
        <p>Every small comfort becomes taxable.</p>
        <p>Every escape becomes a transaction.</p>
        <p>You scroll to relax.</p>
        <p>Taxed.</p>
        <p>You stream to cope.</p>
        <p>Taxed.</p>
        <p>You use the internet to complain about the government.</p>
        <p>Also taxed.</p>

        <h2>The Tenth Cut: Buying From Abroad</h2>
        <p>You order from abroad?</p>
        <p>Customs duties. Import VAT. Processing charges. Courier fees. Storage fees. Sometimes brokerage fees.</p>
        <p>You are excited for your parcel — until it arrives with a surprise billing episode.</p>
        <p>Sometimes, customs is more stressful than checkout.</p>
        <p>You bought a phone accessory, a book, a gift for a family member, a replacement part, or a small item not available locally.</p>
        <p>Then the package is held.</p>
        <p>Then you receive a notice.</p>
        <p>Then you compute the charges.</p>
        <p>Then you wonder whether the item is still worth claiming.</p>
        <p>Importation rules exist for legitimate reasons: revenue collection, consumer protection, security, and preventing smuggling. But the experience of many consumers is often confusing, inconsistent, and expensive.</p>
        <p>And when the process feels opaque, people do not merely complain about the cost.</p>
        <p>They lose trust.</p>

        <h2>The Eleventh Cut: Sin, Health, and Hypocrisy</h2>
        <p>You drink alcohol or smoke?</p>
        <p>Sin tax.</p>
        <p>The government says these products are bad for your health.</p>
        <p>But while it discourages you, it also profits from you.</p>
        <p>Sin taxes are meant to reduce consumption and fund health programs. That logic can be defended. Tobacco and alcohol cause illness, hospitalization, and social costs.</p>
        <p>But the hypocrisy becomes difficult to ignore when public hospitals remain underfunded, when health workers are overworked, and when patients still have to buy their own medicines, supplies, or equipment because the public system cannot provide enough.</p>
        <p>You tax the vice.</p>
        <p>But do you truly fund the cure?</p>
        <p>You collect from the smoker.</p>
        <p>But do you provide accessible smoking-cessation support?</p>
        <p>You collect from the drinker.</p>
        <p>But do you adequately fund mental-health services, rehabilitation, and community health programs?</p>
        <p>A sin tax is only morally defensible when the revenue visibly strengthens public health — not when it becomes another revenue stream while the health system struggles.</p>

        <h2>The Twelfth Cut: The OFW’s Sacrifice</h2>
        <p>You are an OFW sending money home?</p>
        <p>Remittance fees. Exchange-rate costs. Possible charges on service fees.</p>
        <p>There may be no general tax on the remittance itself — but that does not mean nothing is deducted from the money you worked so hard for abroad.</p>
        <p>The OFW leaves family behind.</p>
        <p>The OFW endures loneliness, discrimination, unsafe conditions, long hours, and homesickness.</p>
        <p>The OFW sends money for tuition, medicine, food, debt, house repair, and emergencies.</p>
        <p>Then fees and exchange-rate spreads take a portion before the money reaches the family.</p>
        <p>The OFW is often called a modern-day hero.</p>
        <p>But heroes should not have to fight fees just to send love home.</p>
        <p>And when the OFW finally returns, what awaits?</p>
        <p>Sometimes, a country with few opportunities.</p>
        <p>Sometimes, a government that asks for contributions but struggles to provide jobs, healthcare, housing, and dignity.</p>
        <p>The OFW does not merely send remittances.</p>
        <p>The OFW subsidizes families, communities, and sometimes the entire economy.</p>
        <p>That sacrifice deserves more than slogans.</p>
        <p>It deserves protection, accountability, and real opportunity at home.</p>

        <h2>The Thirteenth Cut: Selling Property</h2>
        <p>You sell land or a house?</p>
        <p>Capital gains tax. Documentary stamp tax. Possibly withholding tax. Transfer fees. Registration costs.</p>
        <p>You bought it.</p>
        <p>You paid for it.</p>
        <p>You maintained it.</p>
        <p>And when you finally sell it, the government still wants its closing scene.</p>
        <p>Capital gains tax on the sale of real property is generally imposed at a flat 6 percent of the gross selling price or fair market value, whichever is higher.</p>
        <p>This is not merely a technical detail.</p>
        <p>For many families, land is inheritance.</p>
        <p>Land is security.</p>
        <p>Land is the result of decades of work.</p>
        <p>Land is what parents leave behind for children.</p>
        <p>So when the family finally sells a small lot to pay for medical treatment, education, debt, or relocation, the government takes another share.</p>
        <p>The family may understand taxation.</p>
        <p>But it will also remember the timing.</p>
        <p>It will remember that the state arrives not only when you earn, but also when you are forced to let go of something precious.</p>

        <h2>The Fourteenth Cut: Even Luck Is Taxed</h2>
        <p>You win the lotto?</p>
        <p>Final withholding tax.</p>
        <p>Finally, luck smiles on you.</p>
        <p>But before you can even hold your prize, there is a congratulations — and a deduction.</p>
        <p>You bought the ticket with taxed income.</p>
        <p>You paid VAT or other charges embedded in consumption.</p>
        <p>You dreamed of escaping debt.</p>
        <p>Then the prize arrives with a tax.</p>
        <p>One can accept that lottery winnings are taxable.</p>
        <p>But the symbolism is brutal.</p>
        <p>Even hope has a withholding tax.</p>
        <p>Even the fantasy of a better life comes with a deduction.</p>

        <h2>The Fifteenth Cut: Electricity and the Internet</h2>
        <p>Electricity?</p>
        <p>VAT. Generation charges. Transmission charges. Distribution charges. System loss. Universal charges. Taxes and other pass-on costs.</p>
        <p>Sometimes, you no longer know whether you are paying for electricity — or tuition just to understand your billing statement.</p>
        <p>The bill arrives.</p>
        <p>You study it.</p>
        <p>You see charges you did not directly choose.</p>
        <p>You see adjustments, charges, and line items that feel designed to confuse rather than inform.</p>
        <p>Then comes the internet.</p>
        <p>Cellphone load. Data. Broadband. Wi-Fi.</p>
        <p>VAT.</p>
        <p>Every scroll. Every post. Every video call. Every data subscription.</p>
        <p>Even the internet you use to complain about the government is taxed.</p>
        <p>And that is perhaps the sharpest irony of all.</p>
        <p>The citizen pays to connect.</p>
        <p>The citizen pays to speak.</p>
        <p>The citizen pays to organize.</p>
        <p>The citizen pays to expose corruption.</p>
        <p>And the state collects from the very platform used to question the state.</p>

        <h2>The Sixteenth Cut: Savings</h2>
        <p>Bank savings?</p>
        <p>Twenty percent final withholding tax on ordinary deposit interest.</p>
        <p>You save carefully for an emergency.</p>
        <p>You avoid unnecessary spending.</p>
        <p>You postpone small pleasures.</p>
        <p>You build a little cushion for illness, job loss, tuition, or disaster.</p>
        <p>But when your savings earn interest, there is a deduction.</p>
        <p>Even your financial discipline gets a commission.</p>
        <p>The message can feel cruel:</p>
        <p>If you spend, you are taxed.</p>
        <p>If you save, your earnings are taxed.</p>
        <p>If you invest, there may be taxes.</p>
        <p>If you inherit, there may be estate tax.</p>
        <p>If you give, there may be donor’s tax.</p>
        <p>If you die, there is still estate tax.</p>
        <p>It is as if the system has anticipated every stage of human life — and placed a collection point at each one.</p>

        <h2>Birth, Generosity, and Death</h2>
        <p>Donor’s tax applies to donations above the exempt threshold. Under current rules, donor’s tax is generally 6 percent on total net gifts above PHP250,000 within a calendar year.</p>
        <p>Estate tax is generally 6 percent of the net estate.</p>
        <p>So even generosity has a computation.</p>
        <p>Even death has a final bill.</p>
        <p>A parent may spend a lifetime building a small property, saving for children, and hoping to leave something behind.</p>
        <p>Then the heirs must deal with estate tax before they can fully settle the inheritance.</p>
        <p>The dead cannot protest.</p>
        <p>The grieving family must comply.</p>
        <p>And the state collects one last time.</p>
        <p>This is not an argument for abolishing estate tax. Estate taxes can prevent untaxed wealth from passing endlessly across generations.</p>
        <p>But the moral question remains:</p>
        <p class="highlight">Why is it so easy to tax a grieving family —<br>and so difficult to recover billions lost to corruption?</p>

        <h2>And After All of This, Where Is the Money?</h2>
        <p>Every year, trillions are collected.</p>
        <p>Every year, trillions are spent.</p>
        <p>Yet the national government still borrows.</p>
        <p>The 2026 fiscal program anticipates a full-year national government deficit of approximately PHP1.66 trillion. As of August 2026, the year-to-date deficit had already reached PHP1.05 trillion, according to the Bureau of the Treasury.</p>
        <p>Borrowing is not automatically evil.</p>
        <p>Governments borrow to fund infrastructure, respond to crises, and manage timing gaps between revenue and spending.</p>
        <p>What is evil is borrowing while public funds are wasted.</p>
        <p>What is evil is borrowing while projects fail.</p>
        <p>What is evil is borrowing while corruption remains unpunished.</p>
        <p>What is evil is asking future generations to pay debt for services they never received.</p>
        <p>Because debt is not just a number.</p>
        <p>Debt is future taxes.</p>
        <p>Debt is fewer resources for hospitals, schools, pensions, and disaster response.</p>
        <p>Debt is a bill passed to children who had no vote in the decisions that created it.</p>

        <h2>Confidential Funds While Hospitals Wait</h2>
        <p>The 2026 budget reportedly contains PHP11.8 billion in confidential and intelligence funds.</p>
        <p>Meanwhile, public hospitals lack equipment.</p>
        <p>Schools lack classrooms.</p>
        <p>Workers cannot survive on their wages.</p>
        <p>Farmers struggle with costs, middlemen, and climate shocks.</p>
        <p>Fisherfolk face declining catch and rising fuel prices.</p>
        <p>Commuters endure broken transport systems.</p>
        <p>Families live in flood-prone areas with inadequate drainage.</p>
        <p>And one of the greatest insults of all?</p>
        <p>Nearly PHP1.9 trillion was allocated for flood control from 2011 to 2025.</p>
        <p>That does not automatically mean all of it was stolen.</p>
        <p>But with the scale of anomalies now being investigated, the people deserve answers.</p>
        <p>How much truly protected communities?</p>
        <p>How much was lost to questionable projects?</p>
        <p>How much was wasted through poor planning?</p>
        <p>How much was inflated through procurement abuse?</p>
        <p>How much was lost to ghost projects?</p>
        <p>And how much remains unaccounted for?</p>

        <h2>PHP1.9 Trillion — Again, PHP1.9 Trillion</h2>
        <p>From 2011 to 2025, the Department of Public Works and Highways’ flood management program received approximately PHP1.939 trillion in allocations, according to Senator Panfilo Lacson.</p>
        <p>That is not a small amount.</p>
        <p>That is not a rounding error.</p>
        <p>That is a sum so large it should have transformed the country’s relationship with water.</p>
        <p>It should have meant stronger dikes.</p>
        <p>It should have meant functioning drainage systems.</p>
        <p>It should have meant rivers dredged and maintained.</p>
        <p>It should have meant warning systems, evacuation routes, resilient housing, and local capacity.</p>
        <p>Instead, some communities flood year after year.</p>
        <p>Some roads are newly built, then immediately destroyed.</p>
        <p>Some projects were funded, but people are asking whether they were ever truly completed.</p>
        <p>Reports have described alleged ghost flood-control projects, repeated allocations for the same locations, non-existent works, and manipulation across levels of government.</p>
        <p>Senator Lacson has cited estimates that fake flood-control projects may have caused economic losses of about PHP118.5 billion since 2023, with the Department of Finance linking the damage to roughly 266,000 lost jobs.</p>
        <p>If even a fraction of these allegations is proven, this is not merely mismanagement.</p>
        <p class="strong-highlight">It is theft from people who pay taxes while their homes drown.</p>

        <h2>If a Taxpayer Is Late, There Is a Penalty</h2>
        <p>If a taxpayer is even one day late, there is a penalty.</p>
        <p>Interest. Surcharge. Compromise penalty. Enforcement action.</p>
        <p>But a project worth hundreds of millions that remains unfinished?</p>
        <p>Sometimes, they simply ask for another budget.</p>
        <p>Why is it so fast to collect from the people, but so slow to hold accountable those who waste public money?</p>
        <p>For the ordinary employee, withholding tax is automatic.</p>
        <p>For the small business owner, receipts, permits, registration, and tax filing are required.</p>
        <p>For the content creator, even platform earnings and brand deals can be examined.</p>
        <p>For the freelancer, every invoice may become part of a compliance record.</p>
        <p>But when the issue involves billions in questionable projects, suddenly it takes years.</p>
        <p>Hearings.</p>
        <p>Committee reports.</p>
        <p>Technical working groups.</p>
        <p>Preliminary investigations.</p>
        <p>Appeals.</p>
        <p>Motions.</p>
        <p>Reinvestigations.</p>
        <p>Different administrations.</p>
        <p>Before accountability moves forward.</p>
        <p>The taxpayer cannot say, “I will pay later because I am still studying my income.”</p>
        <p>The contractor, the politician, the fixer, and the procurement operator often seem to have all the time in the world.</p>

        <h2>The Asymmetry of Accountability</h2>
        <p>I am not saying every wealthy person evades taxes.</p>
        <p>I am not saying every government official is corrupt.</p>
        <p>I am not saying every public project is fraudulent.</p>
        <p>My point is simpler:</p>
        <p>Why does it seem easier to watch the income of ordinary Filipinos than to pursue the complex transactions used to hide illegally acquired wealth?</p>
        <p>Why can the state identify a content creator’s Facebook payout, but struggle to publish a complete public ledger of infrastructure contracts?</p>
        <p>Why can a small seller be required to issue receipts, but a multi-billion-peso project can lack accessible, citizen-friendly documentation?</p>
        <p>Why can an employee’s salary be automatically deducted, but a politician’s confidential spending can remain shielded from full public scrutiny?</p>
        <p>Because accountability requires political will.</p>
        <p>And political will is often weakest precisely where the money is largest.</p>

        <h2>What the People Need</h2>
        <p>Because in a country full of politicians who know how to make promises, we need people who know how to audit.</p>
        <p>People who can scrutinize budgets.</p>
        <p>People who can uncover anomalies.</p>
        <p>People who can read procurement documents.</p>
        <p>People who can trace contractors.</p>
        <p>People who can follow the money.</p>
        <p>People who will ask the most basic question:</p>
        <p class="highlight"><strong>Where did the people’s money go?</strong></p>
        <p>Your mother’s taxes deserve to be protected.</p>
        <p>Your father’s overtime pay.</p>
        <p>Your sibling’s minimum wage.</p>
        <p>The market vendor’s daily earnings.</p>
        <p>The jeepney driver’s boundary.</p>
        <p>The farmer’s harvest.</p>
        <p>The fisherfolk’s catch.</p>
        <p>The OFW’s remittance.</p>
        <p>The content creator’s late-night editing.</p>
        <p>The freelancer’s dollar payment.</p>
        <p>The small business owner’s sacrifice.</p>
        <p>That money is not a campaign fund.</p>
        <p>That is not a family inheritance.</p>
        <p>That is not the personal piggy bank of politicians who act as if they own the government.</p>
        <p class="strong-highlight">That is the people’s money.</p>

        <h2>Who Did You Vote For?</h2>
        <p>And you?</p>
        <p>Who did you vote for?</p>
        <p>People who know how to read a budget?</p>
        <p>Or people with a PhD in burning public funds?</p>
        <p>Real public servants?</p>
        <p>Or political dynasties that treat government — from Malacañang down to the barangay — like a family corporation?</p>
        <p>People with plans to fix the system?</p>
        <p>Or experts at placing their faces on tarpaulins for projects paid for by our taxes?</p>
        <p>Every election is not merely a choice of personalities.</p>
        <p>It is a choice of fiscal culture.</p>
        <p>Do we choose leaders who see public office as service?</p>
        <p>Or leaders who see public office as access?</p>
        <p>Do we choose leaders who publish, explain, and submit to audit?</p>
        <p>Or leaders who hide behind “confidential,” “classified,” and “delicate”?</p>
        <p>Do we choose leaders who build systems?</p>
        <p>Or leaders who build loyalty networks?</p>
        <p>The budget is a moral document.</p>
        <p>It tells you what a government truly values.</p>
        <p>Not its speeches.</p>
        <p>Not its slogans.</p>
        <p>Not its press releases.</p>
        <p>Its budget.</p>

        <h2>Is This a Nation — or a Subscription Service?</h2>
        <p>Deductions are monthly.</p>
        <p>Payments are annual.</p>
        <p>The obligation is lifetime.</p>
        <p>But the service sometimes feels like a free trial that has already expired.</p>
        <p>You pay for security, yet fear crime.</p>
        <p>You pay for health, yet queue for hours in public hospitals.</p>
        <p>You pay for education, yet classrooms remain insufficient.</p>
        <p>You pay for infrastructure, yet roads crack and floods return.</p>
        <p>You pay for governance, yet corruption scandals repeat.</p>
        <p>A subscription service at least gives you a receipt.</p>
        <p>A subscription service at least tells you what you are paying for.</p>
        <p>A subscription service can be canceled.</p>
        <p>But citizenship cannot be canceled.</p>
        <p>And that is why the demand for accountability is not optional.</p>

        <h2>The Greatest Irony</h2>
        <p>Here is the greatest irony:</p>
        <p>Corruption is not legal, taxable income that can simply be declared to make it clean.</p>
        <p>But for the corrupt, it can become a massive business.</p>
        <p>Public office becomes a marketplace.</p>
        <p>Budgets become inventory.</p>
        <p>Projects become profit centers.</p>
        <p>Contracts become commissions.</p>
        <p>Disaster funds become opportunities.</p>
        <p>The poor become justification.</p>
        <p>The taxpayer becomes the financier.</p>
        <p class="strong-highlight">In the Philippines, you are not just a taxpayer.<br>You are a full-time sponsor of a government that keeps asking for money — but is so difficult to ask for a receipt.</p>

        <h2>What I Want</h2>
        <p>Here is what I want:</p>
        <p>Every government project should have a QR code.</p>
        <p>A code the public can scan to see the budget, the contractor, the cost breakdown, the completion status, the inspection reports, and the audit findings.</p>
        <p>A code that shows who approved it.</p>
        <p>A code that shows who implemented it.</p>
        <p>A code that shows who received payment.</p>
        <p>A code that shows whether the project is real.</p>
        <p>A code that shows whether the community actually benefits.</p>
        <p>If the BIR can determine how much a content creator earned on Facebook, then the people should be even more able to know where PHP100 million in a government project went.</p>
        <p>If platforms can report digital income, then government systems can publish procurement data.</p>
        <p>If ordinary citizens must submit receipts, then public officials must submit to full audit.</p>
        <p>If late taxpayers face penalties, then officials responsible for ghost projects must face prosecution.</p>
        <p>If the poor must pay VAT on food, medicine, load, and transportation, then the powerful must not be allowed to hide public money behind technical language.</p>
        <p class="highlight">Transparency is not a favor.<br>It is a duty.</p>

        <h2>I Am Not Angry at Taxes</h2>
        <p>I am not angry at taxes.</p>
        <p>I am angry at the possibility that what we worked hard for is becoming the luxury, the power, and the business of the corrupt.</p>
        <p>I am angry when a worker’s overtime is deducted automatically, while questionable contractors receive billions.</p>
        <p>I am angry when a mother pays VAT on rice, soap, and medicine, while flood-control funds disappear into questionable projects.</p>
        <p>I am angry when a content creator must declare Facebook Stars, while officials refuse to explain confidential funds.</p>
        <p>I am angry when an OFW pays remittance fees, while the country cannot provide enough reasons for them to stay home.</p>
        <p>I am angry when a family pays estate tax while grieving, while those who plunder public funds may die wealthy and unpunished.</p>
        <p>The problem is not that we pay for a government.</p>
        <p>The problem is when we keep paying, yet we still have to beg for the very services we ourselves funded.</p>
        <p>Because taxes are not a donation to the politician who won the election.</p>
        <p>Taxes are not tribute.</p>
        <p>Taxes are not loyalty payments.</p>
        <p>Taxes are not campaign contributions.</p>
        <p>Taxes are not personal funds.</p>
        <p>Taxes are the people’s collective investment in a country that owes them roads, schools, hospitals, justice, safety, dignity, and truth.</p>
        <p>And until the government can show the people where every peso goes —</p>
        <p>until it can punish those who steal —</p>
        <p>until it can protect the taxpayer as fiercely as it collects from the taxpayer —</p>
        <p>the question will remain:</p>
        <p class="strong-highlight">Is this a nation?<br>Or a subscription service?</p>
        <p>Because in the Republic of Tax Philippines, the people are not asking for exemption.</p>
        <p>They are asking for honesty.</p>
        <p>They are asking for receipts.</p>
        <p>They are asking for accountability.</p>
        <p>They are asking for the simple dignity of knowing that their sweat, their sacrifice, and their taxes are being used for the country —</p>
        <p>not for the corrupt.</p>
        <p><strong>Because that is our money.</strong></p>
        <p><strong>Not theirs.</strong></p>
