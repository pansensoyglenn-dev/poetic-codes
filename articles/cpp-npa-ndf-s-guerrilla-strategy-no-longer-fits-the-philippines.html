CPP-NPA-NDF's Guerrilla Strategy No Longer Fits the Philippines

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · Local Politics · November 6, 2026

Why the CPP-NPA-NDF's rural guerrilla-camp strategy is a relic that no longer fits the Philippines' security.

Written by Glenn Junsay Pansensoy  ·  Local Politics  ·  10 min read

The CPP-NPA-NDF’s strategy of establishing rural guerrilla camps and pursuing a protracted people’s war has become a relic of a different historical moment. It was designed for a country where the state had limited reach, rural poverty was largely untouched by government institutions, and an armed movement could plausibly expand from isolated villages into a national revolutionary force. That Philippines no longer exists.

Today, the strategy persists not because it offers a credible path to social transformation, but because the organizations behind it have struggled to imagine a future beyond armed struggle. The result is a tragic mismatch: young people are still being asked to leave school, families, and legal political life for camps in the countryside, while the country’s most urgent security challenges lie elsewhere—in the West Philippine Sea, in cyber space, in disaster resilience, and in the protection of an archipelagic nation whose sovereignty is increasingly tested from outside.

The time has come to say plainly that the CPP-NPA-NDF’s guerrilla-camp strategy is irrelevant to the current situation. Continuing it does not advance the interests of workers, farmers, indigenous peoples, students, or the Filipino nation. It merely prolongs a war whose original premises have collapsed.

The Logic Behind the Old Strategy

The CPP-NPA-NDF framework draws heavily from the Maoist idea of “protracted people’s war.” In simplified terms, the strategy assumes that revolution begins in the countryside, where state authority is weakest and popular discontent is strongest. Guerrilla units establish bases among rural communities, conduct political education, recruit fighters, build local organs of power, and gradually expand into larger guerrilla fronts.

The theory holds that these fronts will eventually link together, allowing the revolutionary movement to encircle the cities and seize national power. Rural camps are therefore not merely military shelters; they are meant to be the seedbeds of a parallel state. They are supposed to demonstrate that the movement can govern, administer justice, collect taxes, mobilize communities, and defend territory.

For decades, this model shaped the NPA’s operational imagination. Guerrilla fronts became the basic unit of revolutionary work. Cadres were assigned to remote barangays. Recruits were trained in the mountains. Armed propaganda units moved from village to village, seeking to build support through a combination of organizing, service delivery, intimidation, and armed action.

The strategy had a certain internal coherence. It recognized that the Philippine state historically neglected many rural areas. It understood that landlessness, poverty, abusive landlords, corrupt officials, and weak public services could generate resentment. It also understood that armed groups could exploit these grievances when legitimate channels for change appeared closed.

But recognizing a grievance is not the same as possessing a viable solution. The CPP-NPA-NDF mistook the persistence of rural poverty for proof that armed revolution remained viable. It mistook the existence of injustice for evidence that guerrilla camps could eventually overthrow the state. Most importantly, it failed to update its strategy as the Philippines changed.

The Philippines Has Changed

The Philippines of the twenty-first century is not the Philippines of the 1970s or even the 1990s. The state’s presence has expanded, even if unevenly. Roads, mobile networks, schools, health stations, local government units, social welfare programs, and electoral politics reach farther into rural communities than they once did. This does not mean governance is adequate or just. It does mean that the old claim that the countryside is a political vacuum awaiting revolutionary administration is far less persuasive.

The NPA itself has changed. At its height, it was described as a force with tens of thousands of fighters and a presence across much of the archipelago. Today, it is a much smaller and more fragmented remnant. Government agencies report that the remaining guerrilla fronts are weakened, inactive, or subject to focused dismantling operations. Whatever one thinks of official claims, the broader trend is difficult to deny: the NPA no longer possesses the nationwide reach, mass base, or military momentum required to sustain the revolutionary strategy it professes.

This matters because guerrilla warfare depends on more than weapons. It depends on safe terrain, community protection, recruitment, logistics, intelligence, and the ability to regenerate forces after losses. A small armed group hiding in remote areas may survive for years, but survival is not victory. It may launch ambushes or harass patrols, but harassment is not a program for national transformation.

The guerrilla camp, once imagined as the embryo of a new society, has instead become a symbol of strategic exhaustion. It represents a movement that can no longer win but refuses to stop fighting.

Camps Have Become a Liability

In practical terms, maintaining camps in remote areas is now more dangerous than productive. Modern surveillance, drones, better intelligence networks, improved roads, and coordinated military-police operations make it harder for armed groups to operate with impunity. A camp that once might have been concealed by distance and difficult terrain can now be located, monitored, and targeted.

The consequences fall hardest on the newest and youngest members. Senior cadres may understand the risks and possess the ideological conviction to accept them. But many recruits are students, out-of-school youth, activists, or young people searching for meaning and belonging. They are asked to believe that joining the armed struggle is the highest form of patriotism or social commitment.

What they often encounter instead is hardship, fear, isolation, and the constant possibility of death. They may be trained to use firearms, taught to view fellow citizens as class enemies, and placed in situations where they can be injured, captured, or killed. Their families may not know where they are. Their education is interrupted. Their futures are narrowed before they have had the chance to build them.

This is not liberation. It is sacrifice without a realistic strategic return.

A movement that genuinely cared about young people would ask how to help them become doctors, engineers, teachers, farmers’ advocates, journalists, lawyers, entrepreneurs, scientists, and community leaders. It would help them organize legally, vote, run for office, expose corruption, defend human rights, and push for reform. Instead, the guerrilla-camp model asks them to disappear into the hills for a cause whose military prospects have diminished beyond recognition.

The Country Faces Different Threats

The most compelling reason to abandon the guerrilla-camp strategy is not simply that it has failed. It is that the Philippines now faces threats that demand a completely different national focus.

The country is an archipelago of more than 7,600 islands, with vast maritime territories, critical sea lanes, fisheries, energy interests, and exposed coastal communities. Its security can no longer be understood primarily as a land-based conflict between the government and rural insurgents. The central challenge is external: protecting sovereignty and sovereign rights in the West Philippine Sea, deterring coercion, monitoring foreign vessels, securing communications, and building a credible defense posture across sea, air, land, cyber, and space domains.

The Armed Forces of the Philippines has increasingly recognized this shift. Defense planning now emphasizes archipelagic defense, maritime domain awareness, external deterrence, and the modernization of naval and air capabilities. The government has invested in ships, aircraft, radar, bases, logistics, cyber defense, and cooperation with allies. These are not distractions from internal peace; they are responses to the reality that the country’s most consequential security risks no longer come mainly from small guerrilla units in the mountains.

This shift has major implications. Every peso, soldier, intelligence asset, and policy attention devoted to chasing remnants of an insurgency is a resource unavailable for maritime defense, disaster response, border protection, and national resilience. The longer the CPP-NPA-NDF insists on armed struggle, the longer it forces the country to divide its security attention between yesterday’s war and tomorrow’s threats.

There is also a deeper irony. The CPP-NPA-NDF claims to oppose imperialism and foreign domination. Yet its continued armed struggle weakens the country precisely at a time when foreign pressure and maritime coercion are most visible. A nation preoccupied with internal conflict has less capacity to defend its waters, protect its fishermen, patrol its exclusive economic zone, and respond to external intimidation.

If patriotism means strengthening the nation’s ability to stand on its own, then clinging to a fading internal insurgency is not patriotic. It is strategically self-defeating.

Social Justice Requires Politics, Not Camps

None of this means the grievances that once fueled the insurgency have disappeared. Poverty remains real. Land reform remains incomplete. Workers still face insecurity. Indigenous communities still struggle against displacement and exploitation. Corruption remains a national disease. Rural communities still deserve better roads, hospitals, schools, irrigation, credit, and protection from abusive actors.

But these problems require political solutions, not guerrilla camps.

A student who wants to fight landlessness can study agrarian law, join a farmers’ cooperative, document abuses, support local candidates, or work in rural development. A young person angered by corruption can become an investigative journalist, a public-interest lawyer, a budget transparency advocate, or an honest civil servant. A community organizer can build cooperatives, defend workers’ rights, organize voters, and push for local ordinances. These paths are slower and less dramatic than armed struggle, but they are more constructive and more likely to produce lasting change.

Armed struggle offers certainty of sacrifice but uncertainty of results. Legal political struggle offers uncertainty and frustration, but also the possibility of building institutions, changing laws, winning elections, and improving lives without destroying them.

The CPP-NPA-NDF often frames legal activism as insufficient and reform as a trap. That argument may have had more force when democratic space was brutally restricted. But it is a poor excuse today. The country’s democratic institutions are imperfect, vulnerable, and frequently abused. Yet they remain real arenas of struggle. To abandon them entirely in favor of clandestine camps is not revolutionary clarity; it is political surrender to a failed method.

The Moral Cost of Persistence

There is a moral question that the CPP-NPA-NDF cannot evade: who pays for the persistence of this strategy?

Not the theorists who write long documents about people’s war from relative safety. Not the spokespersons who issue statements from abroad or from urban offices. Not the veteran cadres who have already spent decades in the movement and possess the experience to navigate its risks.

The people who pay are the young recruits. They are the ones sent to remote camps. They are the ones exposed to encounters, landmines, surveillance, hunger, disease, and psychological trauma. They are the ones who may die before they have finished their studies, started a career, raised a family, or discovered what they might have become.

A movement that claims to represent the future cannot keep sacrificing the future generation to preserve its own past.

It is not enough to say that the cause is noble. Causes do not justify endless war when the method has lost strategic meaning. It is not enough to say that the state is unjust. Injustice does not make every form of resistance wise. And it is not enough to say that young people are free to choose. Recruitment into clandestine armed organizations often occurs through emotional pressure, ideological isolation, romanticized narratives, and a distorted picture of the risks involved.

The ethical test is simple: would a movement honestly tell an eighteen-year-old student that joining a rural guerrilla camp offers a realistic path to national liberation—or would it admit that the student is being asked to sustain an institution’s survival?

A Better Path Forward

The CPP-NPA-NDF should retire the guerrilla-camp strategy and confront the present honestly. That does not require abandoning concern for the poor or surrendering the right to criticize the state. It requires choosing methods suited to the country’s actual condition.

It means investing in legal organizing, community services, cooperative economics, local politics, human rights work, and public education. It means helping young people develop skills rather than sending them into hiding. It means accepting that sovereignty, development, and social justice are national projects that require broad participation, not a monopoly claimed by an armed vanguard.

It also means recognizing that the Philippines needs its young people in classrooms, laboratories, farms, hospitals, coastal communities, government offices, and civic organizations. The nation needs them building resilience against typhoons, defending democratic institutions, improving food security, protecting the environment, and strengthening the country’s capacity to respond to external threats.

The old revolutionary dream promised that camps in the countryside would become the foundation of a new Philippines. In reality, they have become monuments to a strategy that time has left behind. The country does not need more young people buried in remote hills. It needs them alive, educated, empowered, and engaged.

Otherwise we are just sending our young students in the countryside to death for a cause that doesn’t fit the current reality.
