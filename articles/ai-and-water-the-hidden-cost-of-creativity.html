AI and Water: The Hidden Cost of Creativity

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · AI · November 1, 2026

AI's data centers consume vast water and energy, threatening communities and creativity.

Written by Glenn Junsay Pansensoy  ·  AI  ·  13 min read

Artificial intelligence has become one of the most talked-about technologies of the modern world. It can write essays, generate images, compose music, edit videos, translate languages, answer questions, and even help people design new products. To many people, AI feels almost magical: a person types a few words into a screen, and within seconds, a detailed image, song, or article appears.

However, behind this apparent magic is a massive physical system. AI does not simply exist in phones, laptops, or the internet. It runs in enormous data centers filled with powerful computers that process huge amounts of information. These data centers consume vast quantities of electricity and require large volumes of water to stay cool. As AI becomes more popular, its demand for energy and water continues to grow.

This creates an important contradiction. AI is often praised as a tool for creativity and progress, yet it can also be destructive when its environmental costs are ignored. In places where water is already scarce, data centers may compete with families, farmers, and ecosystems for limited water supplies. The question is no longer only whether AI can create impressive things. The more urgent question is whether society is willing to sacrifice water, energy, and environmental stability for convenience.

What Generative AI Really Is

Generative AI refers to systems that can create new content rather than simply analyze existing information. These systems are trained on enormous collections of text, images, audio, video, and other data. Through this training, they learn patterns in language, color, sound, style, and structure.

When a user gives a prompt, such as "create a painting of a sunset over a rice field," the AI uses what it has learned to generate an image. When someone asks it to write a poem, summarize an article, or produce a song, it follows a similar process. The output may seem original, but it is built from patterns found in massive amounts of human-made material.

This is why AI can be useful for creative work. It can help artists brainstorm ideas, writers overcome creative blocks, designers test visual concepts, and musicians experiment with sounds. It can also save time by handling repetitive tasks such as editing, formatting, translating, or generating drafts.

But AI's usefulness should not hide its cost. Every request sent to a generative AI system requires computing power. That computing power happens in data centers, which are industrial facilities containing thousands of servers. These servers work continuously, generating heat as they process information.

Data Centers and the Water Problem

Data centers are the physical backbone of AI. They store, process, and transmit digital information. Without them, AI chatbots, image generators, video tools, cloud storage, and many online services would not function.

Servers produce heat when they operate. If they become too hot, they can slow down, malfunction, or be damaged. To prevent this, data centers use cooling systems. Some use air cooling, but many large facilities use water-based cooling because water can absorb and transfer heat efficiently.

Water-based cooling can consume enormous amounts of water. In some cases, water is evaporated during the cooling process, meaning it does not simply return to the local water system. Instead, it leaves as vapor. This makes the water consumption especially significant because it is not always easy to recover or reuse.

The amount of water needed can reach millions of liters. The exact amount depends on the size of the data center, the type of cooling system, the local climate, and how intensively the servers are used. During hot seasons, cooling demands rise even further. This means that AI use may place the greatest pressure on water supplies precisely when communities need water most.

Why Water Scarcity Makes This Dangerous

Water is not equally available everywhere. Some regions have abundant rainfall and strong water infrastructure, while others suffer from drought, declining groundwater, polluted sources, or limited access to clean water.

The danger of AI's water use becomes clearer when data centers are built in water-stressed areas. Companies may choose locations because land is affordable, electricity is cheap, or internet connections are strong. However, these business advantages do not always match the environmental needs of the local community.

When a data center consumes large volumes of water, it may reduce the water available for households, schools, hospitals, farms, and small businesses. In agricultural communities, reduced water access can affect crops, livestock, food production, and farmers' incomes. In urban areas, it can contribute to higher water bills, supply restrictions, and conflicts over who gets priority during shortages.

This is especially concerning in countries and regions that already experience seasonal droughts or rapid population growth. A data center may appear clean and quiet from the outside, but its water demand can have serious consequences for the people living nearby.

The Impact on Communities

The effects of AI's water consumption are not abstract. They can affect real people in real places.

Farmers may struggle to irrigate their fields if local water supplies become less reliable. Families may face stricter rules about when they can use water for washing, cooking, or cleaning. Communities that depend on rivers, lakes, or groundwater may see those sources decline over time.

There is also an issue of fairness. The benefits of AI are often enjoyed by people around the world who use apps, websites, and digital tools. Meanwhile, the environmental burden may fall on the communities located near data centers. These communities may receive few direct benefits while bearing the costs of water depletion, noise, energy infrastructure, and environmental disruption.

This raises an environmental justice concern. Should a global technology company be allowed to use large amounts of local water if the surrounding community does not have enough for its own needs? Should residents have a stronger voice before data centers are built? These questions are becoming more important as AI expands.

The Energy Problem Behind AI

Water is not the only environmental issue. AI also requires enormous amounts of electricity. Training large AI models can take weeks or months and requires thousands of powerful processors working together. After training, running the AI for millions of users also consumes continuous energy.

Much of the world's electricity still comes from fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and natural gas. When data centers rely on electricity produced from these sources, AI contributes to greenhouse gas emissions. These emissions worsen climate change, which in turn increases droughts, heat waves, and water shortages.

This creates a harmful cycle. AI consumes water and energy. The production of energy can worsen climate change. Climate change makes water scarcity more severe. Then, as temperatures rise, data centers need even more cooling, which can require even more water.

The problem is therefore not limited to one data center or one community. It is part of a larger environmental system. If AI grows faster than clean energy and sustainable water management can support, its environmental damage may become much worse.

AI and Creativity: Helpful or Harmful?

AI can genuinely support creativity. It can help a student visualize an idea, assist a small business in designing a logo, or allow a filmmaker to test different visual styles before production. It can also make creative tools more accessible to people who cannot afford expensive software, professional designers, or large production teams.

However, AI can also harm creative communities. Many artists, writers, musicians, and designers worry that their work has been used to train AI systems without permission, credit, or fair payment. AI can imitate artistic styles, sometimes producing work that competes directly with the original creators.

This can reduce opportunities for human artists. If companies can generate logos, illustrations, music, or written content instantly and cheaply, they may hire fewer creative professionals. Young artists may find it harder to build careers, while experienced artists may lose income and recognition.

There is also a deeper concern: creativity is not only about producing a final product. Human art often carries personal experience, emotion, culture, struggle, and intention. An AI system can imitate the appearance of creativity, but it does not live through the experiences that inspire human expression.

When AI-generated content floods the internet, people may begin to value quantity more than quality. Creative work may become something that is produced quickly, consumed quickly, and forgotten quickly. This can weaken appreciation for the time, skill, and effort that human creativity requires.

The Illusion of Effortless Creation

One reason AI's environmental cost is often ignored is that using it feels effortless. A person may generate dozens of images in an afternoon without thinking about the servers, electricity, and water involved.

But "effortless" for the user does not mean effortless for the planet. Every image, video, or long response requires computing resources. When millions of people make similar requests every day, the combined impact becomes enormous.

This is similar to other environmental issues. A single plastic bottle may seem harmless, but billions of bottles create a major pollution problem. A single AI prompt may seem insignificant, but billions of prompts require massive infrastructure, electricity, and cooling.

This does not mean people should never use AI. It means AI should be used thoughtfully. Generating hundreds of variations for fun, creating unnecessary videos, or relying on AI for every small task may have more environmental impact than many users realize.

Responsible AI Use

Individuals can make more responsible choices when using AI. One approach is to use AI only when it provides real value. For example, AI may be helpful for brainstorming, learning, editing, or solving a difficult problem. But generating endless images for entertainment or producing large amounts of content that will never be used may be wasteful.

People can also support companies that are transparent about their environmental impact. AI companies should disclose how much water and electricity their data centers use, where their facilities are located, and what steps they are taking to reduce harm.

Governments can also play a role. They can require environmental impact assessments before large data centers are approved. They can set rules on water use, especially in drought-prone areas. They can encourage companies to use renewable energy, efficient cooling technologies, and water recycling systems.

Companies themselves must take responsibility. They can design more efficient AI models, improve hardware efficiency, use renewable electricity, and invest in cooling systems that reduce or eliminate freshwater consumption. They should also consult local communities before building facilities in water-sensitive regions.

Can AI Become More Sustainable?

AI does not have to be environmentally destructive. There are ways to reduce its impact.

One solution is improving efficiency. Researchers can develop smaller, more efficient models that require less computing power while still performing well. Companies can avoid training unnecessarily large models and focus on systems that meet real needs.

Another solution is better cooling technology. Some data centers use closed-loop cooling systems, which recycle water instead of evaporating it. Others use air cooling, liquid cooling, or waste-heat recovery systems. These technologies can reduce freshwater consumption.

Renewable energy is also essential. Data centers powered by solar, wind, geothermal, or other clean sources can reduce their contribution to climate change. However, renewable energy must be paired with responsible water management, because even clean energy infrastructure can have environmental impacts.

Finally, transparency is crucial. People cannot make informed choices if companies hide their water and energy use. Public reporting, independent audits, and clear environmental standards can help hold AI companies accountable.

The Ethical Question

The debate about AI is often framed as a question of innovation versus restriction. Some people argue that AI should be allowed to grow freely because it may bring economic growth, scientific discoveries, and creative opportunities. Others argue that its risks are too serious to ignore.

But this is not simply a choice between progress and protection. True progress should not come at the expense of basic resources such as clean water. A technology that helps people create digital art while contributing to water shortages in real communities is not fully beneficial.

Ethical AI development means considering who benefits and who pays the cost. It means asking whether a new data center is necessary, whether it is located responsibly, whether it uses water sustainably, and whether the community has consented to its presence.

It also means recognizing that creativity has value beyond convenience. Human artists, writers, and musicians contribute culture, identity, and meaning to society. AI should not exploit their work or replace them without fair systems of consent, credit, and compensation.

The Future of AI and Water

AI will likely continue to grow. More people will use AI tools for work, school, entertainment, communication, and business. More companies will build data centers to meet this demand.

The future depends on the choices made now. If AI companies prioritize rapid expansion without environmental safeguards, water scarcity and energy consumption could become much more severe. But if governments, companies, and users demand sustainable practices, AI can develop in a way that reduces harm.

The goal should not be to stop technological progress completely. Instead, the goal should be to make progress responsible. AI should serve people without taking away the resources they need to live. It should support creativity without exploiting artists or damaging the environment.

Conclusion

AI is neither purely good nor purely bad. It can be a powerful tool for learning, communication, and creativity. But it can also be destructive when its hidden costs are ignored. The water required to cool AI data centers, the electricity needed to power them, and the pressure placed on local communities show that AI has real-world consequences.

The convenience of generating an image or writing a paragraph in seconds can make people forget that AI depends on physical resources. Millions of liters of water, vast amounts of electricity, and large industrial facilities stand behind the digital world.

Society must ask difficult questions before AI expands further. Where is the water coming from? Who is affected by its use? Is the energy clean? Are artists protected? Are communities consulted? Are companies transparent about their environmental impact?

AI should not be allowed to grow simply because it can. It should grow because it can do so responsibly. The future of artificial intelligence should not be built by draining rivers, depleting groundwater, or putting profit above people. Before celebrating what AI can create, humanity must confront what it may destroy.
