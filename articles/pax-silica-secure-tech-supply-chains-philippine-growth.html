Pax Silica: Secure Tech Supply Chains & Philippine Growth

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · Economy · November 14, 2026

Pax Silica is a U.S.-led international initiative launched in December 2025 to strengthen the supply chains behind semiconductors, artificial intelligence, and other advanced technologies. Its name refers to silica—the raw material used to make silicon for computer chips—and signals its ambition to create a more reliable foundation for the global digital economy.

The initiative arrives at a moment when the world’s most important industries increasingly depend on a narrow set of countries, companies, minerals, and manufacturing facilities. Pax Silica seeks to ensure that participating countries are not left dependent on fragile supply chains or vulnerable to political and economic pressure.

For the Philippines, Pax Silica represents more than a diplomatic agreement. The proposed Economic Security Zone in New Clark City, Tarlac, is the most visible expression of that ambition.

What Pax Silica Is

Pax Silica is a coalition of governments committed to expanding cooperation on the materials, technologies, and infrastructure required for artificial intelligence and advanced manufacturing. It brings together countries that recognize that economic strength in the twenty-first century depends not only on access to markets, but also on secure access to chips, critical minerals, energy, logistics networks, and industrial capacity.

The initiative is not a binding treaty. Instead, it establishes a framework for cooperation among governments that share an interest in building resilient supply chains, expanding technological capacity, and reducing the risks created by excessive concentration in global production.

The term “coercive dependency” is central to Pax Silica’s purpose. It refers to a situation in which a country, industry, or company becomes so reliant on a limited supplier or group of suppliers that it can be pressured through export restrictions, price manipulation, political leverage, or the threat of disrupted access.

Pax Silica’s answer is diversification. Rather than relying on one country or one region for essential inputs, participating governments seek to build a broader network of trusted partners.

Why Supply Chains Matter

Global supply chains are often described as invisible, but their effects are visible everywhere. A shortage of semiconductor chips can slow automobile production, delay the construction of data centers, raise the price of consumer electronics, and disrupt industries that depend on automation and digital systems.

The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated how quickly disruptions can spread through interconnected economies. The experience showed that efficiency alone is not enough; resilience also matters.

Pax Silica responds to this reality by encouraging countries to build redundancy into their supply chains. Redundancy does not mean abandoning trade or rejecting foreign investment.

For developing economies, this approach can be especially valuable. These investments can create jobs, transfer skills, and build local industrial capacity.

The Importance of Silicon

Silicon is one of the most abundant elements on Earth, but turning it into the highly purified material used in semiconductor manufacturing requires extraordinary precision.

The process involves mining and refining raw materials, producing high-purity silicon, manufacturing wafers, designing circuits, fabricating chips, and assembling and testing finished products.

This is why the semiconductor industry is difficult to replicate. A country cannot simply decide to become a major chip producer overnight.

Pax Silica recognizes that no single country can control every stage of this process. The goal is not autarky—economic self-sufficiency at any cost—but strategic interdependence.

Semiconductors and Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence has made semiconductors even more strategically important. Advanced AI systems require powerful processors, high-bandwidth memory, specialized networking equipment, and enormous amounts of electricity.

The rapid growth of AI has therefore created a new industrial race.

The Philippines has existing strengths that can support this transition. It has a large English-speaking workforce, experience in electronics manufacturing and business-process outsourcing, growing digital infrastructure, and a strategic location in Southeast Asia.

However, opportunity alone is not enough. The Philippines must develop the technical skills, industrial ecosystems, and infrastructure needed to attract and retain advanced manufacturing.

The Philippine Participation

The Philippines joined Pax Silica in April 2026, becoming one of only two Southeast Asian members alongside Singapore. This decision placed the country within a coalition that includes major economies and technology powers, including the United States, Japan, Australia, India, Israel, South Korea, and the United Kingdom.

Membership does not guarantee investment or automatic industrial development. But it signals to global companies that the Philippines is part of a network committed to supply-chain resilience, technological cooperation, and long-term industrial development.

For Philippine policymakers, the challenge is to convert membership into concrete outcomes. This means attracting credible investors, training workers, improving permitting processes, expanding power capacity, strengthening logistics, and ensuring that local firms can participate in supply chains rather than serving only as passive providers of land and labor.

The country’s participation also reflects a broader strategic calculation. The Philippines sits along vital maritime routes and has a growing role in regional trade and security.

New Clark City and the Economic Security Zone

The centerpiece of the Philippine involvement in Pax Silica is a proposed Economic Security Zone in New Clark City, Tarlac. The zone is expected to cover roughly 1,620 hectares and is envisioned as an “AI-native industrial acceleration hub.”

Rather than focusing solely on traditional manufacturing, the proposed zone aims to bring together semiconductor assembly, testing and packaging, advanced manufacturing, data infrastructure, research, logistics, and related industries.

New Clark City has several advantages. It is located within Central Luzon, near Clark Freeport Zone and major transport corridors.

The project also builds on the Luzon Economic Corridor, a Philippines–United States–Japan initiative focused on infrastructure and connectivity. The corridor links Subic Bay, Clark, Manila, and Batangas through rail, ports, energy infrastructure, and other investments.

Technology Transfer and Skills Development

Pax Silica should not be viewed simply as a program for attracting foreign factories or hosting foreign companies. Its real value lies in whether Filipino workers, students, engineers, technicians, and local firms can learn from the advanced industries it brings into the country.

When foreign companies establish semiconductor assembly, testing, packaging, data-center, or advanced-manufacturing operations in the Philippines, they bring more than capital. If Filipinos are trained to operate, maintain, improve, and eventually innovate within these systems, the country gains more than jobs—it gains industrial capability.

This is the essence of technology transfer. A Filipino technician who learns to operate precision equipment, an engineer who learns advanced manufacturing processes, or a local supplier that learns to meet international quality standards becomes part of a growing national knowledge base.

However, technology transfer will not happen automatically. It requires deliberate investment in education and training.

This requires stronger education in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics. It also requires practical training in electronics, precision manufacturing, industrial automation, electrical systems, software, data analysis, cybersecurity, logistics, and equipment maintenance.

Universities should strengthen research and engineering programs, while technical-vocational schools should provide accessible pathways into high-demand industrial careers.

Teachers and trainers also need support. They must have access to updated knowledge, modern facilities, industry exposure, and opportunities to learn from companies operating at the frontier of technology. A country cannot teach advanced manufacturing if its educators have never seen or worked with advanced manufacturing systems.

The highest form of technology transfer occurs when a country moves from using foreign technology to improving and creating technology. This does not happen overnight, but it becomes possible when a country develops a critical mass of skilled workers, engineers, researchers, and entrepreneurs.

If the Philippines succeeds, Pax Silica can become more than an investment program. It can become a national learning platform—one that helps Filipinos acquire the skills and confidence to compete in the global technology economy.

Moving Beyond Low-Value Assembly

For decades, the Philippines has participated in global electronics production largely through assembly, testing, and packaging. These activities are important, but they represent only part of the value chain.

Pax Silica offers the Philippines an opportunity to move up this value chain. The country may not immediately become a leading semiconductor fabricator, but it can build capabilities in areas where it already has advantages. These include testing, packaging, electronics manufacturing, software services, engineering support, logistics, and data infrastructure.

The goal should not be to imitate every country’s industrial strategy. Instead, the Philippines should identify the segments where it can realistically compete and build a long-term plan around them. A successful industrial strategy would combine foreign investment with domestic capability-building.

Infrastructure Is Essential

No industrial strategy can succeed without infrastructure. Advanced manufacturing requires stable electricity, reliable water supply, high-speed telecommunications, efficient transport, and modern industrial facilities.

The Philippines must therefore treat infrastructure as a national priority. This includes expanding generation capacity, improving transmission and distribution networks, upgrading ports and roads, expanding rail connections, and ensuring that industrial zones have access to the utilities they need.

Public-private partnerships can play an important role. Government can provide planning, land-use coordination, permitting reform, and strategic infrastructure, while private investors can bring capital, technology, and operational expertise.

Energy policy is particularly important. AI and semiconductor manufacturing are energy-intensive. If the Philippines wants to attract these industries, it must provide affordable, reliable, and increasingly clean electricity.

Foreign Investment and National Capability

Foreign investment is often criticized as a source of dependency, but this criticism misses an important point: investment can be a tool for development when it is managed strategically.

The key is not to reject foreign capital, but to ensure that it contributes to national development.

The Philippines should welcome investors that create real value in the country. This means companies that train Filipino workers, source from local suppliers where possible, build lasting facilities, and contribute to the development of industrial ecosystems.

A mature development strategy recognizes that global integration and national capability are not opposites. Countries become stronger by participating in global markets while steadily building the skills, institutions, and industries needed to capture more value from that participation.

The Lesson of Vietnam and China

Vietnam and China offer powerful examples of how pragmatic engagement with the global economy can produce extraordinary results. Both countries faced poverty, conflict, and limited industrial capacity in their recent histories.

Vietnam’s transformation is especially relevant for the Philippines. Once one of the poorest countries in Asia, Vietnam pursued economic reforms that opened its economy to foreign investment and global trade. It attracted manufacturers seeking alternatives to higher-cost production centers, developed industrial zones, invested in infrastructure, and built a workforce capable of supporting export manufacturing.

Today, Vietnam is a major producer of electronics, textiles, furniture, footwear, and other goods. Its success did not come from ideological isolation.

China’s experience is even more dramatic. After decades of economic isolation, China adopted reforms that encouraged foreign investment, export manufacturing, infrastructure development, and technological learning.

China also invested heavily in education, research, and domestic industry. Over time, it moved from low-cost manufacturing toward more advanced production in areas such as telecommunications equipment, electric vehicles, batteries, solar panels, and digital infrastructure. Its rise shows that a country can participate in global markets while pursuing a deliberate strategy of industrial upgrading.

The lesson is not that every country should copy China or Vietnam exactly. But the broader lesson is clear: countries that engage strategically with the global economy, invest in infrastructure and skills, and build domestic capability tend to achieve better outcomes than those that remain trapped in ideological slogans.

Why Anti-Imperialism Rhetoric Is No Longer Enough

Some critics describe Pax Silica as an instrument of “U.S. imperialism.” This language may carry emotional force, but it offers little practical guidance for a country seeking development. The world is not divided neatly into imperial powers and passive victims.

The Philippines is a sovereign country. It has the right to enter agreements, attract investment, and pursue partnerships that serve its national interests.

More importantly, anti-imperialist rhetoric does not build semiconductor plants, train engineers, expand power grids, or create jobs. It does not improve ports, roads, schools, or data centers. It does not help a Filipino technician learn advanced manufacturing skills or enable a local supplier to enter a global value chain.

The old ideological framework assumes that foreign investment and international cooperation are inherently harmful. But Vietnam and China show that this assumption is wrong.

The real test of any economic initiative is practical: Does it create productive employment? Does it develop skills? Does it build infrastructure? Does it strengthen the country’s ability to compete in the global economy?

Pax Silica should be judged by these standards. If it delivers investment, technology, jobs, and capability, it deserves support. If it fails to deliver, it should be improved—not dismissed because it involves cooperation with the United States.

The Philippine Development Imperative

The Philippines faces a persistent challenge: it has a young, talented population, but too many Filipinos still lack access to high-quality jobs, advanced skills, and productive industries. Many workers remain in informal employment, low-productivity sectors, or jobs that offer limited opportunities for advancement.

Industrialization is therefore not a luxury. It is a necessity. A country cannot build widespread prosperity on consumption, remittances, and low-value services alone.

The semiconductor and AI economy offers a path toward this goal. These industries require a wide range of capabilities, from basic manufacturing to advanced engineering.

The Philippines should not aim merely to host foreign factories. It should aim to become a country where Filipino engineers design systems, Filipino technicians operate advanced equipment, Filipino firms supply components and services, and Filipino workers earn wages that reflect higher productivity.

Building a Competitive Workforce

A competitive workforce is the foundation of any technology-led development strategy. The Philippines has a young population, but demographic advantage becomes economic advantage only when people are educated, trained, and connected to productive opportunities.

The country should expand access to technical education in areas such as electronics, mechatronics, industrial automation, semiconductor technology, data-center operations, electrical engineering, software development, and logistics. Training should not be limited to universities. Technical-vocational education can provide practical pathways into well-paying industrial jobs, and partnerships between schools, companies, and government agencies can ensure that programs match employer needs.

Workers also need opportunities to upgrade their skills over time. The Philippines should also encourage women to enter technical and industrial fields, since the technology economy cannot reach its potential if half the population is underrepresented in engineering, manufacturing, and digital infrastructure.

Regional Development Beyond Metro Manila

Pax Silica’s focus on New Clark City and Central Luzon could help spread development beyond Metro Manila. For too long, economic opportunity has been concentrated in the capital region, while many provinces have struggled to attract investment and create high-quality jobs.

A successful industrial zone in Tarlac could create demand for workers, suppliers, housing, transport, education, healthcare, and services across the surrounding region.

Local governments will play an important role. They must ensure that infrastructure, housing, transport, and public services keep pace with industrial growth. They should also work with employers and training institutions to prepare residents for opportunities in the technology economy. The goal should be inclusive growth—connecting local communities to supply chains, employment, training, and long-term economic opportunity, rather than creating isolated enclaves for foreign companies.

A Practical Path Forward

The Philippines should approach Pax Silica with confidence, clarity, and strategic discipline. The country should welcome investment, but it should also negotiate agreements that produce real benefits for Filipino workers and businesses.

Several priorities stand out:

  • Build reliable electricity, water, transport, and digital infrastructure in and around New Clark City.
  • Expand technical education and industry-linked training in semiconductor, AI, and advanced-manufacturing skills.
  • Encourage foreign investors to train local workers, develop local suppliers, and transfer practical technical knowledge.
  • Streamline permitting and business processes without weakening environmental, labor, or public-interest standards.
  • Support Filipino firms seeking to enter supply chains as component suppliers, service providers, logistics partners, and technology companies.
  • Ensure that industrial development creates opportunities for communities in Central Luzon, not only for large corporations.
  • Maintain a long-term industrial strategy that survives changes in administration and political leadership.

These steps will determine whether Pax Silica becomes a transformative development initiative or remains a promising announcement.

A Strategic Economic Vision

Pax Silica reflects a wider shift in global economic policy. Countries increasingly view chips, minerals, energy, and industrial capacity as strategic assets.

For the Philippines, participation in Pax Silica offers a chance to move beyond dependence on low-value activities and become a more capable participant in the global technology economy. It offers an opportunity to attract investment, develop skills, expand infrastructure, and create a more productive industrial base.

The country should reject the idea that foreign cooperation is inherently harmful. Vietnam and China demonstrate that strategic engagement with the global economy can produce growth, employment, and industrial transformation.

The future will belong to countries that can build, produce, train, connect, and innovate. Pax Silica gives the Philippines an opportunity to become one of those countries. The task now is to turn opportunity into achievement.

📌 Key takeaway: Pax Silica is a framework for resilient supply chains, technology transfer, and industrial upgrading. For the Philippines, success depends on infrastructure, skills, and strategic follow-through.
