Vietnam's Cooperative Model and the Philippine Challenge

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · Economy · November 15, 2026

Vietnam’s economic transformation offers an important lesson for the Philippines: a poor, largely agrarian society cannot leap directly into a fully communist society. It must first develop productive forces, modern institutions, industrial capacity, technical skills, and a disciplined work culture. Vietnam’s Communist Party recognized this reality during its Đổi Mới (Renovation) reforms. In 1986, it shifted from a centralized, bureaucratic, and subsidized planned economy toward a socialist-oriented market economy that prioritized commodity production, private and collective enterprises, and foreign investment.

This did not mean Vietnam abandoned its socialist goals. Instead, it accepted that communism cannot be created by decree alone. A society must pass through stages of economic development, accumulate capital, build industries, educate its people, and learn how to compete in global markets. Vietnam used markets, exports, private enterprise, and foreign investment as instruments to strengthen the productive base of the country, while the state maintained a long-term development direction.

For the Philippines, the lesson is not that Filipinos must imitate Vietnam’s political system. Rather, it is that national prosperity requires more than slogans, elections, foreign aid, or individual ambition. It requires cooperation, discipline, productive work, honest institutions, and a shared willingness to sacrifice short-term personal gain for the long-term good of the community and nation.

The Kibbutz Inspiration

Vietnam’s cooperative experience also reflects an interest in collective economic models from other countries, including Israel. The Israeli kibbutz, first established in 1909, was not simply a farm. It was a social and economic community built around collective ownership of land and productive assets, shared labor, mutual responsibility, democratic decision-making, and a commitment to rebuilding a nation through practical work.

In a kibbutz, members contributed their labor and shared the benefits of production. Decisions were made collectively, and the community provided education, housing, healthcare, childcare, and other basic services. The model was demanding because it required trust. Members had to believe that the community would protect them and that their own labor would benefit everyone, not merely a privileged few.

Vietnam did not copy the kibbutz exactly. Its history, population size, colonial experience, war trauma, and socialist ideology were different. However, Vietnam learned from the central idea behind the kibbutz: ordinary people can organize themselves into productive communities when they combine self-reliance with cooperation. Vietnamese policymakers and agricultural reformers later studied Israel’s collective-economy models directly, including how cooperatives could organize farmers, improve production, and connect rural communities to modern markets.

This is important because the kibbutz was never merely a romantic idea. It was a practical institution for survival, agricultural production, and nation-building. It showed that collective ownership can work when members are committed, leadership is accountable, production is efficient, and the community adapts to changing economic conditions.

From Collective Farming to Market Discipline

Vietnam’s early collective-farming system faced serious problems. Like many centrally planned economies, it suffered from weak incentives, bureaucratic control, low productivity, and limited freedom for farmers to make economic decisions. Farmers often had little reason to work harder if the rewards of their labor did not clearly return to them or their families.

The Đổi Mới reforms changed this. Vietnam gradually allowed households greater control over production, liberalized prices, reduced internal trade restrictions, opened the economy to foreign investment, and encouraged exports. The result was not the destruction of cooperation, but its transformation. Cooperatives were expected to become more autonomous, business-oriented, and responsive to members and markets.

Today, Vietnamese cooperatives are increasingly viewed as bridges between small farmers and larger economic forces. They help members purchase inputs at lower cost, access machinery and technology, share technical knowledge, standardize production, obtain certifications, and negotiate with processors and exporters. In this sense, the cooperative is not opposed to the market; it is a tool that allows small producers to participate in the market more effectively.

This is the key insight for the Philippines. A cooperative fails when it becomes a vehicle for political patronage, debt without production, or leaders who treat common resources as personal property. It succeeds when it operates like a disciplined enterprise: it produces goods people want, maintains quality, controls costs, repays loans, adopts technology, and shares profits fairly among active members.

Why Vietnam Moved Ahead

Vietnam’s advantage was not simply that its people were naturally more honest or hardworking than Filipinos. Such generalizations are unfair and historically simplistic. Every nation contains honest and dishonest people, industrious and unproductive workers, visionary leaders and corrupt officials. The more useful question is: what institutions, incentives, and cultural expectations allow honesty, discipline, and cooperation to become widespread?

Vietnam benefited from a strong sense of national purpose after decades of war, division, poverty, and isolation. Its people had experienced the cost of weakness and dependence. When the country opened its economy, many citizens understood that national survival required production, exports, education, and technological upgrading. The government also pursued economic diplomacy and trade agreements that connected Vietnamese firms to global markets.

Vietnam’s state played an active role, but it did not try to replace every private decision with bureaucracy. It created conditions for investment, infrastructure, education, export promotion, and industrial development. It allowed market forces to reward efficiency while maintaining a strategic role in guiding national development.

The Philippines, by contrast, has often struggled to sustain a coherent long-term development strategy. Political leadership changes frequently, policies may shift with each administration, and powerful interests can influence decisions for short-term advantage. Corruption, weak enforcement, poor infrastructure, and fragmented local politics can prevent cooperatives, farmers, and small enterprises from growing into competitive national industries.

Yet the Philippines should not despair. Its people possess talent, creativity, resilience, and a strong tradition of mutual help. Filipino communities already practice cooperation in many forms: bayanihan, religious associations, hometown associations, credit cooperatives, labor groups, farmers’ organizations, and community savings groups. The challenge is to turn these habits of mutual assistance into modern, transparent, and productive economic institutions.

The Filipino Question: Can We Sacrifice for the Common Good?

The central question is whether Filipinos can achieve collective economic prosperity. This requires more than forming cooperatives on paper. It requires a change in mindset.

Will every Filipino sacrifice personal selfish interest for the common good? Not completely, and not all at once. Human beings are naturally concerned with their families, income, security, and future. A successful cooperative does not deny self-interest; it channels it. Members gain more when the cooperative succeeds, so they have a reason to contribute labor, capital, loyalty, and discipline.

But this only works when members trust one another. If leaders steal, members withhold labor. If members refuse to repay loans, the cooperative weakens. If powerful families capture the organization, ordinary members lose faith. If politicians use cooperatives as vote-buying machines, the institution becomes corrupt. Collective prosperity therefore depends on shared rules, equal accountability, and moral courage.

Filipinos must learn to ask not only, “What can I take from this organization?” but also, “What can I contribute to it?” A member who receives credit must repay it. A farmer who benefits from shared equipment must maintain it. A leader elected by members must serve them rather than enrich himself. A community that receives assistance must use it productively rather than treat it as a permanent entitlement.

This is not a demand for impossible selflessness. It is a demand for responsible citizenship. People can pursue their own welfare while also recognizing that their welfare is connected to the welfare of others. A fisher benefits when the coastal cooperative protects fishing grounds. A farmer benefits when the cooperative maintains quality standards. A worker benefits when the enterprise grows. A community benefits when its members refuse to tolerate corruption.

Will Cooperatives Thrive in the Philippines?

Yes, cooperatives can thrive in the Philippines—but only under certain conditions. The country already has a legal and institutional foundation. The 1987 Constitution recognizes cooperatives as instruments of social justice, equity, and economic development. Republic Act No. 9520 declares that the state shall foster cooperatives as practical vehicles for self-reliance, people power, economic development, and social justice.

The Cooperative Development Authority (CDA) is the lead government agency for promoting the sustained growth and development of Philippine cooperatives. Its mandate is to help cooperatives become broad-based instruments of social justice, equity, and balanced national progress.

Philippine law also recognizes the core principles that make cooperatives different from ordinary corporations. These include voluntary and open membership, democratic member control, member economic participation, autonomy, education and training, cooperation among cooperatives, and concern for the community. In primary cooperatives, members generally have one vote each, regardless of how much capital they have contributed.

These principles are powerful because they prevent a cooperative from becoming the private empire of a few wealthy investors. At the same time, they create responsibilities. Democratic control means members must participate. Economic participation means members must contribute capital and share risks. Autonomy means cooperatives must not become dependent on politicians or government subsidies. Education means leaders and members must continuously learn how to manage a business.

A cooperative can thrive when it has:

  • Honest and competent leaders selected through transparent elections.
  • Clear bylaws, regular meetings, and accessible financial records.
  • Members who understand their rights and obligations.
  • Productive enterprises, such as farming, fishing, processing, transport, retail, housing, or savings and credit.
  • Access to markets, technology, training, and affordable credit.
  • Partnerships with universities, local governments, private firms, and other cooperatives.
  • Strict rules against nepotism, political interference, and misuse of funds.
  • A long-term vision rather than dependence on short-term political favors.

The Problem of Corruption

Corruption is perhaps the greatest enemy of cooperative prosperity. When public funds disappear, when contracts are awarded through connections rather than merit, when officials demand commissions, and when members tolerate wrongdoing because they benefit from it, trust collapses. Without trust, cooperation becomes impossible.

Corruption is not only a problem of politicians. It can exist inside cooperatives when officers falsify records, lend money to relatives, misuse equipment, manipulate elections, or distribute benefits according to friendship rather than contribution. It can also exist among members who expect favors without responsibility, refuse to follow rules, or treat the cooperative as a source of easy money.

The Philippines cannot build collective prosperity if corruption is accepted as normal. Citizens must demand transparency from government, but they must also practice transparency within their own organizations. A cooperative that demands honesty from national leaders while tolerating dishonesty among its own officers will not succeed.

The solution is not merely punishment after corruption occurs. Prevention is more important. Cooperatives need independent audits, clear procurement rules, public financial reports, term limits, conflict-of-interest policies, member education, and strong internal controls. Members must be trained to read financial statements, question unusual expenses, and hold leaders accountable.

A Practical Path for the Philippines

The Philippines should build a cooperative economy that is neither purely capitalist nor purely state-controlled. It should combine the efficiency of markets with the solidarity of cooperation. Farmers, fishers, workers, consumers, and small entrepreneurs should be organized into legally registered, professionally managed cooperatives that can compete in local, national, and international markets.

For example, a rice cooperative in Central Luzon could help members buy seeds, fertilizer, and machinery at lower cost. It could operate drying facilities, storage warehouses, and milling equipment. It could standardize quality, obtain certifications, develop a common brand, and negotiate directly with institutional buyers. Instead of selling palay individually at low prices, farmers could retain more value through collective processing and marketing.

A fishing cooperative could manage cold storage, ice production, boats, fuel purchasing, and direct sales to hotels, supermarkets, and exporters. A workers’ cooperative could provide transport, construction, or technical services. A consumers’ cooperative could reduce household expenses through bulk purchasing. A credit cooperative could finance productive enterprises rather than merely providing emergency loans.

These examples show that cooperation is not anti-business. It is a different way of doing business—one in which the people who produce, work, buy, and use the services also own and govern the enterprise.

Education and National Discipline

The Philippines must invest heavily in cooperative education. Many cooperatives fail because members do not understand accounting, governance, credit, marketing, or business planning. Leaders may be sincere but unprepared. Members may be willing to cooperate but lack the knowledge needed to manage a modern enterprise.

Schools, universities, local governments, churches, and civil-society organizations should teach financial literacy, entrepreneurship, cooperative governance, environmental responsibility, and civic ethics. Young Filipinos should learn that success is not only individual wealth; it is also the ability to build institutions that uplift families and communities.

National discipline also matters. Filipinos must follow laws, pay taxes, protect public property, respect contracts, and refuse to participate in bribery. They must reject the idea that connections matter more than competence. A society cannot prosper when talent is ignored and loyalty is rewarded above merit.

A Shared National Project

Vietnam’s success was not an accident. It came from a clear recognition that a country must develop its productive capacity before it can achieve higher forms of social equality. It came from a willingness to learn from other countries, including Israel’s kibbutz tradition, while adapting lessons to Vietnamese realities. It came from the decision to combine collective organization with market discipline, exports, technology, and practical reforms.

The Philippines can learn from this without surrendering its own identity. Filipinos do not need to become Vietnamese. They need to become better Filipinos: more disciplined, more honest, more productive, and more committed to one another.

Collective economic prosperity is possible when citizens understand that no family, barangay, province, or nation can progress while everyone acts only for himself. The cooperative model offers a path between selfish individualism and coercive state control. It allows people to retain initiative and ownership while pooling their strength for common goals.

If Filipinos can build honest cooperatives, reject corruption, invest in education, support local production, and practice genuine solidarity, then the country can create a more just and prosperous economy. The question is not whether Filipinos are capable. They clearly are. The question is whether they are willing to choose responsibility, sacrifice, and cooperation over short-term selfish gain.

Vietnam’s example proves that a poor agricultural society can transform itself when it develops patiently, learns from the world, and organizes its people for productive work. The Philippines can do the same—if its people decide that the common good is not an empty phrase, but a national duty.
