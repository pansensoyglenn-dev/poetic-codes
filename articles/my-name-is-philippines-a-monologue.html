My Name Is Philippines: A Monologue

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · Culture · November 23, 2026

They look at me and see only what they want to see.

My neighbors, the ones who have watched me since I was young, the ones who knew me before I had a name for myself—they look at me now and call me a whore. They say it without shame. They say it casually, the way people speak of weather or the price of rice. They say it in doorways, in market stalls, behind the thin walls of houses where nothing stays private for long. They say I let myself be used. They say I am paid for what I give. They say it as though the word can hold the whole of my life inside it.

But a word is a small thing. It cannot hold a lifetime.

Once, they called me beautiful. Once, they said I was the most striking woman in the whole district, the one people noticed, the one whose name traveled on its own. I was young then. I did not understand that beauty could be a burden. I did not know that being seen could become a kind of prison. I did not know that a thing admired is a thing marked, and a thing marked is a thing hunted.

I do not know if it was a blessing or a curse. Maybe it does not matter. Maybe everything that comes to you unasked carries both inside it, the way a fruit carries sweetness and rot in the same skin.

Come. Sit beside me. Have a cigarette. Let the smoke do what smoke does—give the hands something to hold while the mouth says what it has been avoiding.

So many came to me.

They came with different faces but the same hunger. Some spoke softly. Some spoke loudly. Some said nothing at all and simply waited, as though patience were a kind of right. They desired me. They were drawn to me. They looked at me as though I were a thing that had been placed there for them.

I was young. I did not know the difference between being wanted and being valued. I thought that if someone desired me, it meant I mattered. I did not yet understand that desire is not the same as respect. That a man can want you desperately and still see nothing in you but the shape of his own need.

I welcomed them. I did not turn anyone away. I gave what I had, which was more than I knew. I trusted. I believed that if I was open, if I was kind, if I forgave the small cruelties, the larger ones would never come.

They came anyway.

Three foreigners took what they wanted from my body. They called it rape.

The first came with a cross in one hand and a sword in the other. He spoke of God and salvation, but his hands knew only taking. He stayed for centuries. He built churches on my land and taught me to kneel. He gave me a name I did not choose and a faith I did not ask for. When he left, he left behind bells that still ring in towers I did not build, and a language that still sits in my mouth alongside my own.

The second came speaking of liberty and democracy. He said he was setting me free, but he built bases on my soil and called it protection. He taught me his language and told me it was a gift. He stayed long after the war was over, and even now, his shadow falls across my islands. His ships still pass through my waters. His soldiers still walk on my ground. He calls it alliance. He calls it partnership. But a partner does not build a house in your yard and then tell you it is for your own good.

The third came in the name of empire. He was brief, but brutal. He came fast, took what he wanted, and left before I could even understand what had happened. He did not stay long, but what he took, he took completely. He left scars that still ache when the weather changes. He left memories I cannot put down.

And then there was the fourth.

He did not come with a cross or a flag. He did not come with soldiers or gunboats. He came with contracts. He came with loans. He came with men in barongs and men in suits, with engineers and surveyors and people who talked about partnership and mutual benefit and win-win cooperation, as though those words meant anything to someone like me.

He smiled a lot.

That was the first thing I noticed. He smiled more than the others did. The others did not bother pretending. The first one came with a Bible in one hand and a sword in the other, and he did not pretend he was anything but a conqueror. The second one came talking about freedom and democracy, but he built his bases on my soil and called it protection, and he stayed long after the war was over. The third one came fast and brutal, took what he wanted, and left before I could even understand what had happened.

But this one, this fourth one, he smiled.

He brought gifts. Chocolates. Gadgets. Cheap things made in his country that filled our stores and our homes. He built roads. He built bridges. He built buildings with his name on them, tall glass things that caught the sun and made the poor feel small just looking at them. He said he was helping. He said he was investing. He said we were partners now, friends even, and that the old days of colonization were over and this was something new.

But I have been alive a long time. I know what new looks like when it is wearing an old face.

His eyes kept going to the water.

Every time he visited, every time he came to inspect this or that project, every time he stood on a stage and gave a speech about cooperation and shared future, his eyes drifted to the sea. He looked at my islands and did not see land. He saw position. He saw strategy. He saw a map with lines drawn on it that did not belong to me.

He looked at my reefs and saw not coral but claim.

He looked at my seas and saw not home but passage—channels and straits and deep water where ships could pass and submarines could hide and fleets could move. He looked at the water between my islands and his, that stretch of sea we call our own, and he saw not a boundary but an opportunity.

And beneath my soil, he saw other things.

Nickel. Copper. Gold. Chromite. Rare earths. The things his factories needed, the things his industries hungered for, the things that make the machines and the batteries and the weapons that keep his country running. He looked at my mountains and did not see mountains. He saw ore. He looked at my rivers and did not see rivers. He saw the water needed to pull that ore out and wash it clean. He looked at my people and did not see people. He saw labor. He saw hands. He saw the cost of extraction written in a currency he understood.

He did not call it invasion.

He called it investment.

He did not call it occupation.

He called it development.

He built ports I did not need, in places I did not ask for, connecting points on a map that served his routes and not mine. He built railways that went where he wanted them to go. He gave loans with terms written in language I could barely read, with interest that grew in the dark like mold, and when I could not pay, he did not get angry. He just offered more. Another loan. Another project. Another smile.

He said it was help.

He said it was friendship.

But friendship, I have learned, does not keep a ledger. Friendship does not count what it gives and wait for repayment with interest. Friendship does not look at your house and calculate what it is worth in pieces.

He is still here.

That is what frightens me. The others came and went. They took and left. But this one, he intends to stay. He sits at my table now. He eats from my plate. He tells me how grateful I should be for the roads and the bridges and the buildings, as though I did not pay for them myself, with money I borrowed and will spend years repaying. He tells me the world is changing. He tells me the old powers are fading and a new order is rising, and that I would be wise to choose the winning side.

He says it gently.

That is the worst part. He says it gently. He does not threaten. He does not raise his voice. He just explains, patiently, as though I am a child who does not understand how the world works, that resistance is foolish, that the tide is turning, that I should be realistic, that I should be grateful, that I should be careful not to end up on the wrong side of history.

But I know what he is.

I know what he wants.

He wants the islands. He wants the reefs. He wants the shoals and the banks and the rocks that stick out of the water, the ones we have fished from for generations, the ones our grandfathers knew by name. He wants the channels and the straits and the deep water. He wants the minerals beneath my mountains and the fish in my seas and the strategic position of my shores. He wants all of it, and he is patient, and patience is a kind of violence when you are the one being waited upon.

He does not need to fire a shot.

He just needs to wait.

He needs to lend and build and smile and wait until I am so deep in debt, so dependent on his money and his markets and his goods, that saying no becomes impossible. He needs to make himself so necessary that removing him would hurt more than keeping him. He needs to make me grateful for the very chains he is wrapping around me.

And the worst part is, some of my own children help him.

Some of them take his money. Some of them sign his contracts. Some of them stand beside him at ceremonies and smile for the cameras and call it progress. They say he is a friend. They say he is an ally. They say the old powers are worse, and maybe they are, maybe they are, but a knife is a knife whether it comes from the left or the right.

Of the three who came before him, and the one who came after, I cannot say which hurt most. Each took something different. Each left something behind. And I am the one who has to live with all of it.

I am tired.

I am tired of being passed from hand to hand. I am tired of being told what I am worth by people who see only what they can take. I am tired of being beautiful in a way that invites plunder. I am tired of being strategic in a way that makes my body a map for other people's ambitions. I am tired of smiling back at men who smile at me while their hands are already reaching for what is mine.

But I am still here.

And I am watching him.

After the fourth came, things changed again.

Not all at once. Not with a single blow. He killed me the way water wears down stone: patiently, without drama, until one day you look at yourself and realize you are no longer the shape you were.

He gave us things. Chocolates. Cigarettes. Money. Small comforts that became large necessities. He made our lives easier, and that was the trap. Ease is seductive. Once you have known it, poverty feels personal. It feels like punishment. It feels like proof that you have failed.

Through him, we learned more than English. We learned his language, his rhythms, his ways of speaking. We learned to read the fine print on contracts written in a script that was not ours. We learned to nod when we did not understand. We learned to smile when we were afraid.

When I took him into my home, things changed.

We had food. We had new clothes. We had things we had never owned before. For a while, it felt like rescue. For a while, I believed that this was what salvation looked like: a man with money, a house with things in it, children who no longer went hungry.

I thought I loved him.

Maybe I did. Maybe love and dependency look the same when you have never been given a choice between them. Maybe what I felt was not love at all, but the desperate gratitude of someone who has been given a lifeline and is afraid to let go.

He was killing me slowly.

People told me to leave him. They said I was a fool. They said I should have known better. They said it as though knowledge were the same as power, as though seeing a trap clearly meant you could walk out of it.

But traps do not work that way. A trap is not a trap because you cannot see it. A trap is a trap because seeing it does not help. Because the way out is narrower than the way in. Because by the time you understand, you are already caught.

It took years before I could hear them.

When I finally did, when I finally gathered what was left of me and drove him out, I thought the hardest part was over. I thought that once the poison was out, the body would begin to heal.

I was wrong.

Starting over is not a single act. It is a thousand small acts, repeated daily, each one harder than the last. It is waking up and choosing not to call him. It is looking at an empty cupboard and not reaching for the phone. It is telling your children, again, that things will be better, when you are not sure you believe it yourself.

We are buried in debt now.

The numbers grow while we sleep. They feed on delay. They feed on interest. They feed on the simple fact that we cannot stop needing. It is as though we sold something we did not know we had, and now the bill has come due, and there is nothing left to pay it with but ourselves.

Sometimes, when the weight becomes too much, I still think of him.

I hate that I admit this. But shame is a luxury. When you are drowning, you do not think about how it looks to grab at anything that floats. You just grab.

I am like a dog that has bitten its master and then comes back, tail low, asking to be fed. I know what it looks like. I know what people say. But people who have never been hungry do not get to judge the hungry for what they do to survive.

My neighbors talk.

Some pity me. Some despise me. Some simply watch, which is its own kind of cruelty. They say a beautiful woman like me has become a whore. They say it as though beauty and whoredom are the only two things a woman can be. As though there is no middle ground. As though a woman cannot be both beautiful and broken, both desired and discarded, both a mother and a victim.

And yet I stand.

Not because I am strong. I am not strong. I stand because my children need me to. Because if I fall, there is no one else to catch them. Because a mother does not get the luxury of collapse.

We tried to build something.

Some of my children went abroad. Japan. Hong Kong. Saudi Arabia. America. Canada. Europe. They crossed water. They learned new languages. They sent money when they could. They built lives in places that did not know their names.

Some stayed.

But staying did not mean staying close. Some of my children are near me in body and far from me in every other way. They live in my house, eat from my table, and still find ways to take from me. They see what I have saved and think it belongs to them. They do not ask. They assume. And when I object, they look at me as though I am the one who has failed them.

Among those who try to help, there are just as many who take.

They exploit the little I have set aside. They see my savings and think: mine. They do not see the years it took to gather. They do not see the nights I went without so they could have. They see only what is in front of them, and they reach.

Now we can barely rise.

Poverty is not just the absence of money. It is the absence of room. It is the narrowing of choices until every path leads back to the same place. We became accustomed to ease, and now ease has become a debt of its own. We learned to need things we cannot afford to keep, and now we cannot stop needing them.

I wanted to stop.

I wanted to stop selling myself. I wanted to stop letting strangers into my body and my home. I wanted to stop being the thing people reached for when they wanted something and forgot when they had it.

But the debt is too large. And the children still under my roof need feeding. And the children abroad, well—perhaps they will come back. Perhaps they will not. Perhaps they have already forgotten the woman who gave them everything.

It does not matter if my beauty is gone. It does not matter if my body is ruined. What matters is that my children know I loved them. That I would have given anything. That I did give everything, even the parts of me that were never mine to give.

When I look in the mirror, I still see something there.

Not beauty, exactly. Not anymore. But something. A trace. A refusal to disappear completely. And beneath it, always, my children's faces. I see them in the lines around my eyes. I see them in the set of my mouth. I see them in the way I hold myself, as though I am still waiting for someone to come home.

Tears fall before I know it.

My children are talented. They succeed wherever they go. They are capable in all things, for good or for harm. They have gifts I never had. They have opportunities I never dreamed of. And yet.

Of all my children, only a few truly care.

Even those who care are still unripe. Their concern is thin. Their loyalty is conditional. They love me when it costs nothing. They leave when it costs too much.

What breaks me most is knowing we were never really a family.

Almost all of my children are at war with one another. A few want to help, but even they cannot seem to do it without pulling someone else down. They compete for scraps. They resent what others have. They mistake ambition for virtue and cruelty for strength.

I ask myself if I failed.

From what seed did they come, these children of mine who are educated and still cruel? Who have diplomas and titles and still find pleasure in their siblings' pain? I thought education would make them better. I thought knowledge would soften them. I was wrong. Knowledge without compassion is just a sharper tool for hurting people.

They were the ones I believed would save us.

Instead, they watch each other starve and feel nothing.

They are not siblings. They are strangers who share a bloodline and nothing else.

What I never expected was that my own children would be the ones to finish me.

They profited from my ruin. They took the money I earned and called it theirs. They bought things with it, showed them off, wore their success like a costume. They forgot where it came from. Or worse—they remembered, and did not care.

Is wealth really worth this much?

Are titles and possessions really worth the cost of a soul? I have seen what money does. It does not fill the empty place. It only makes the empty place louder. It gives you things to hold, but nothing that holds you.

Sometimes I look in the mirror and do not know the woman there.

Christmas is coming.

I hope they remember me. In a few weeks, a new year begins. I am afraid. They are already talking about driving me out again. They will use the hunger of their own siblings as a weapon. They will call it necessity. They will call it pragmatism. They will call it anything but cruelty.

I have been in this corner so long, I no longer know if there is anywhere else to go.

I wish someone would defend me.

I wish someone would fight for me.

I want to shout: I am your mother. All I need is your love.

Not money. Not gifts. Not promises. Just love. The kind that stays. The kind that costs something. The kind that does not disappear when it becomes inconvenient.

Forgive me. I have talked too long.

My makeup is ruined.

Thank you for listening. It means more than you know.

We have been sitting here a long time, and I never told you my name.

Forgive me.

My name is Philippines!
