Curry in the Philippines: A Story of Seas, Spices, and Local Creativity

From ancient maritime trade routes to kulma in Sulu and kare-kare in Luzon — how a mobile culinary idea became unmistakably Filipino.

When Filipinos hear the word curry, many people immediately think of India. Others may think of Thailand, Indonesia, or Malaysia—countries whose fragrant, coconut-rich, spice-laden dishes have become familiar across the world. But the Philippines has its own curry story, one shaped by ancient sea routes, regional trade, migration, war, colonialism, religion, and local creativity. Filipino curry did not arrive fully formed from one place, nor did it remain unchanged after it reached the islands. It became something distinctly Filipino because generations of cooks adapted foreign ideas to local ingredients, tastes, and occasions.

Long before Filipino curry became familiar in homes and restaurants, the Philippine islands were already connected to a wider maritime world. Boats carried goods across the seas. People carried languages, beliefs, skills, and cooking traditions. Among these traditions were techniques for combining spices, aromatics, coconut, meat, seafood, and rice. The story of curry in the Philippines is therefore not simply the story of an imported dish. It is the story of how a mobile culinary idea became rooted in a specific archipelago.

A Country Shaped by the Sea

The Philippines has always been shaped by the sea. It is an archipelago made up of thousands of islands, and many early communities lived near rivers, bays, coastlines, and trading ports. The sea was not a barrier; it was a highway. People traveled by boat between islands and exchanged rice, ceramics, textiles, metals, forest products, dried fish, salt, and spices. Coastal settlements became meeting points where local products met foreign goods and where different peoples encountered one another.

These communities were also connected to Borneo, Java, Sumatra, China, and South Asia. Maritime Southeast Asia was never isolated. For centuries, traders moved through the South China Sea, the Sulu Sea, the Celebes Sea, and the Indian Ocean. Their routes linked the Philippine islands to larger networks of commerce and cultural exchange. A trader from Borneo might bring cloth and spices. A Chinese merchant might bring ceramics and metal goods. A Muslim missionary or merchant might bring religious ideas, Arabic words, and new ways of preparing food.

Trade was more than the exchange of objects. It also allowed people to share words, beliefs, technologies, farming practices, and ways of preparing food. A cooking tradition could travel with a merchant. A spice could arrive with a sailor. A recipe could move through a marriage, a migrant family, a religious community, or a political alliance. After reaching a new place, the recipe could change according to the ingredients available there. This is why food history is rarely simple. Dishes do not travel as fixed objects; they travel as ideas that people reinterpret.

Archaeological evidence from Southeast Asia shows how old these connections are. Research on ancient grinding stones at Óc Eo in southern Vietnam found traces of turmeric, ginger, galangal, clove, nutmeg, cinnamon, and other spices associated with curry-making. Scholars suggest that South Asian traders and migrants helped introduce curry-like culinary traditions to Southeast Asia through Indian Ocean trade networks beginning around 2,000 years ago. The important point is not that the Philippines copied Vietnam or India directly. Rather, it shows that spice-based cooking was already moving across maritime Asia long before modern national borders existed.

The Meaning of “Curry”

The word curry is a broad term. Today, it describes many different dishes from South Asia and Southeast Asia: Indian korma, Thai green curry, Indonesian gulai, Malaysian rendang, and many others. But the word itself does not describe one single dish. It is a European colonial label that was applied to a wide range of spiced stews, sauces, and rice accompaniments from southern Asia. Because of this, using the word “curry” too narrowly can hide the diversity of the foods it describes.

Filipino cooks already had their own ingredients and cooking traditions before the modern idea of curry became familiar. Coconut was abundant across the islands. Ginger, garlic, onions, chili peppers, and other aromatics gave depth to food. Fish, shellfish, chicken, pork, root crops, and rice were prepared in different ways according to regional customs. Cooks boiled, grilled, smoked, fermented, roasted, and stewed their food. In some regions, turmeric and other spices also became part of local cooking.

These ingredients were not necessarily combined in one standardized dish called curry. Instead, they created a strong foundation for new recipes. When outside influences arrived, Filipino cooks already had the knowledge to make those flavors their own. Coconut milk could replace dairy. Local aromatics could replace unfamiliar spices. Fish sauce, shrimp paste, calamansi, and native vegetables could give a dish a taste that belonged to the Philippines.

This is one of the most important lessons of Filipino curry: adaptation is not a loss of authenticity. It is how cuisine survives and develops. A dish becomes Filipino not because it remains unchanged from its place of origin, but because Filipino cooks make meaningful choices about how to prepare it, season it, serve it, and share it.

Indian Influence Through Maritime Networks

Indian influence reached the Philippines through the wider maritime networks of Asia. For centuries, Indian merchants, religious traditions, political ideas, languages, and artistic influences spread throughout parts of Southeast Asia. Hindu and Buddhist ideas traveled through trade routes, as did Sanskrit-derived vocabulary, writing systems, textile styles, and culinary practices. But this influence did not always travel directly from India to the Philippines.

It often passed through several communities and trading centers. A spice may have traveled from India to a Southeast Asian port. From there, it could have been carried by Malay, Javanese, Bornean, or other traders to Philippine shores. The same was true of cooking methods. By the time an ingredient or recipe reached the islands, it may already have been changed by many cultures.

This makes the history of curry more accurate when imagined as a network rather than a straight line. Indian spices and cooking ideas moved through Sri Lanka, the Malay world, Java, Sumatra, Borneo, and the Sulu Sea. Along the way, they encountered local ingredients and local preferences. In Southeast Asia, cooks commonly blended Indian-derived spices with galangal, lemongrass, makrut lime, coconut milk, and other regional ingredients. The result was not a copy of Indian food but a family of related cuisines.

The Philippines, located farther east than mainland Southeast Asia, received some of these influences in a more layered and indirect way. Still, the evidence of connection is visible in language, trade history, and especially in the food of Mindanao and the Sulu Archipelago. Filipino cuisine includes dishes influenced by Indian and Malay traditions, including kulma, which is related to korma, and kare-kare, whose possible connections to Indian sepoys have become part of popular food history.

The Southern Philippines: A Culinary Crossroads

The Southern Philippines played an especially important role in this history. Mindanao and the Sulu Archipelago maintained close connections with Borneo and the Malay-Indonesian world. These connections influenced trade, religion, language, clothing, political traditions, and cuisine. Islam spread through much of maritime Southeast Asia through trade and missionary activity, and Muslim trading communities helped connect the Sulu region to broader Indian Ocean networks.

In southern communities, cooks prepared richly seasoned dishes using coconut, turmeric, chili, ginger, garlic, meat, and seafood. Some preparations resemble foods from other parts of maritime Southeast Asia. Others developed into distinctive local traditions. The cuisine of Mindanao, Sulu, Tawi-Tawi, and parts of Palawan uses spices such as turmeric, coriander, lemongrass, galangal, cumin, cinnamon, native lime leaves, and chili—ingredients less common in many other parts of the Philippines.

This does not mean that southern Filipino food is simply Malay or Indian food. It means that the region belongs to a shared maritime world. The Sulu Sea connected Tausug, Sama-Bajau, Yakan, Maranao, Maguindanaon, and other communities to Borneo, Malaysia, Indonesia, and the wider Muslim trading world. Food moved with people, and people moved with food.

Dishes such as kulma show how southern Philippine cuisine reflects long contact with the wider Muslim and Malay world. These dishes should not automatically be described as Indian curry. They are better understood as regional foods shaped by movement, exchange, and local knowledge. Kulma is Filipino because it developed in Filipino communities, uses Filipino ingredients and preferences, and carries the memories and celebrations of the people who cook it.

Kulma: A Filipino Curry With Deep Roots

Kulma is one of the clearest examples of Filipino curry’s layered history. It is especially associated with the Tausug people of the Sulu Archipelago and with communities in Mindanao, including Zamboanga. The dish is often made with beef slowly cooked in coconut milk, peanuts or peanut butter, tomatoes, and a blend of spices. Its name resembles korma, a South Asian dish, and many accounts connect kulma to the broader history of Indian and Muslim trade in Southeast Asia.

Some accounts trace kulma to korma traditions from northern India and Pakistan, suggesting that Arab and Muslim traders may have helped carry related dishes through Sulu before they evolved into the Tausug version known today. Other descriptions emphasize that kulma developed through Tausug contact with traders from Southeast Asia, India, and the Middle East, with local cooks adapting spices such as turmeric, cloves, cinnamon, and cardamom to local tastes. Whatever the exact route, the broader pattern is clear: kulma is a product of connection, not isolation.

What makes kulma distinct is its Filipino character. It often uses coconut milk rather than yogurt, a common feature of South Asian korma. It may include peanuts, giving it a rich, nutty body that can remind diners of kare-kare. It is commonly served with rice and appears at important gatherings, including Muslim weddings and festivals such as Eid’l Adha. In this sense, kulma is not merely a curry; it is a celebratory dish, a marker of hospitality, and a connection to community identity.

Kulma also shows why it is misleading to divide food into “foreign” and “Filipino” too rigidly. The dish has roots connected to South Asia and the Muslim maritime world, but its present form belongs to Tausug and Mindanaoan culinary traditions. It reflects the creativity of cooks who took an idea, adapted it to available ingredients, and made it part of their own celebrations.

Tiyula Itum and the Diversity of Southern Cooking

Kulma is not the only southern Filipino dish that complicates the idea of curry. Tiyula itum, a Tausug specialty, is a dark beef soup or stew colored and flavored by burnt coconut meat. It is rich, smoky, and deeply savory. Although it may not look like the yellow or orange curries many people imagine, it belongs to the same broader world of coconut-based, spice-influenced cooking.

Tiyula itum demonstrates an important point: Filipino curry traditions are not defined only by turmeric or curry powder. They include different approaches to richness, heat, aroma, and depth. In Tausug, Sama-Bajau, and Yakan communities, celebratory meals may include kulma and tiyula itum, showing how curry-like dishes exist alongside other complex coconut and meat dishes.

The Maranao culinary tradition offers another example. Maranao rendang may rely less on some imported spices and more on toasted coconut, ground rice, and sakurab, an aromatic plant native to parts of Mindanao. This shows that even when a dish shares a name with food from Indonesia or Malaysia, its Filipino version can be distinct. Local ingredients reshape the dish. Local preferences determine its texture and heat. Local history gives it meaning.

Kare-Kare: A Luzon Story of Improvisation

While kulma represents the southern curry tradition, kare-kare is perhaps the most famous curry-adjacent dish in Luzon. It is a thick stew, often made with oxtail, peanut sauce, annatto, vegetables such as eggplant, string beans, banana blossom, and pechay, and served with bagoong alamang or shrimp paste. Its color and richness often lead people to call it the Philippine version of curry.

One widely told explanation connects kare-kare to the British occupation of Manila from 1762 to 1764 during the Seven Years’ War. British forces included Indian sepoys, soldiers recruited from Madras. According to this account, after the British withdrew, some sepoys remained in the Philippines, settled in areas including Cainta in Rizal, married locally, and adapted their cooking to the ingredients available around Manila. Without familiar Indian spices, they reportedly used annatto for color and peanuts for richness, creating a dish that eventually became kare-kare.

The story is compelling because it explains both the dish’s appearance and its name. The term kari or karehan has been linked to curry-selling food stalls, which some accounts connect to the later development of the word carinderia, now used for small Filipino eateries. Whether every detail of this origin story can be proven is another matter. Food history often mixes documented events with family memory, local legend, and later interpretation. But the kare-kare story captures something real: curry in the Philippines was also shaped by empire, war, migration, and improvisation.

Kare-kare is not simply Indian curry made with Filipino ingredients. It has become a Filipino dish with its own logic. Peanut sauce gives it a distinct body. Annatto gives it a warm orange color. Bagoong provides a salty, fermented contrast that is very Filipino. The vegetables and cuts of meat reflect local markets and family preferences. In other words, even if kare-kare began with an encounter involving Indian cooks or sepoys, it became Filipino through continued local use.

Curry Powder and the Filipino Home Kitchen

In many Filipino households, especially in Luzon and the Visayas, “chicken curry” often means chicken stewed with garlic, onions, ginger, curry powder, fish sauce, potatoes, carrots, and coconut milk or coconut cream. This version is familiar, comforting, and practical. It reflects how Filipino home cooks use convenient modern ingredients while retaining older cooking habits.

Filipino chicken curry is sometimes described as a variant of ginataang manok, or chicken cooked in coconut milk, with curry powder added. That description is useful because it shows how Filipino cooks do not simply borrow a foreign recipe. They insert a new ingredient into an existing culinary framework. Coconut milk, ginger, garlic, onion, and fish sauce are already familiar. Curry powder becomes another layer of flavor rather than the foundation of the entire dish.

This kind of adaptation is common in Filipino cooking. Filipino cuisine often absorbs outside elements and makes them feel natural. Soy sauce came through Chinese influence but became central to dishes such as adobo and pancit. Spanish colonial cooking influenced many stews and celebrations, yet Filipinos transformed them with local ingredients. In the same way, curry powder became part of some Filipino kitchens without erasing the older traditions around it.

The result is a dish that can vary greatly from family to family. Some cooks make it soupy. Others make it thick. Some use coconut cream for richness. Others keep it lighter. Some add chili for heat; others prefer a mild, family-friendly version. These variations are not mistakes. They are evidence of a living cuisine.

Why Filipino Curry Is Regional

One reason curry in the Philippines is difficult to define is that the country itself is regionally diverse. Luzon, the Visayas, and Mindanao have different histories, climates, products, and cultural connections. A dish that feels natural in Sulu may be unfamiliar in Ilocos. A home-style chicken curry in Metro Manila may differ from a festive beef kulma in Zamboanga. This regional diversity is not a weakness; it is the heart of Filipino food.

In Luzon, curry is often milder and more likely to use commercial curry powder. In Mindanao and Sulu, cooks may use fresher and more complex spice combinations, reflecting centuries of contact with the Malay world. In the Visayas, coconut-based dishes are common, and local seafood, pork, and chicken can appear in ginataan-style preparations that overlap with curry-like cooking.

This regional variation also reflects geography. Mindanao and Sulu are closer to Borneo, Malaysia, and Indonesia than Luzon is. Their trade routes and cultural ties naturally produced stronger culinary connections to the Malay-Indonesian world. Luzon, especially Manila, had a different history shaped by Spanish colonial rule, Chinese trade, and later British occupation. Each region absorbed different influences at different times.

Therefore, asking “Where did Filipino curry come from?” cannot produce one answer. It produces several answers: from ancient Indian Ocean trade, from Malay and Bornean connections, from Muslim trading communities, from Indian sepoys during the British occupation, from local coconut-based cooking, and from the creativity of Filipino families.

Coconut, Rice, and the Filipino Palate

Three ingredients help explain why curry adapted so well to the Philippines: coconut, rice, and aromatic seasonings.

Coconut is central to much Filipino cooking. Coconut milk gives dishes body, sweetness, and richness. It can soften the sharpness of spices and create a comforting sauce for meat, seafood, or vegetables. In curry-like dishes, coconut milk performs a role similar to cream or yogurt in other cuisines, but it brings a flavor that is unmistakably tropical and familiar in the Philippines.

Rice is the natural companion. Curry, kulma, kare-kare, ginataan, and similar dishes are designed to be eaten with rice. The sauce is not separate from the meal; it is meant to coat the grains. This makes Filipino curry dishes practical and satisfying. A small amount of rich sauce can flavor a large serving of rice, making the dish suitable for family meals and communal eating.

Aromatics complete the picture. Garlic, onion, ginger, lemongrass, native lime, chili, and fish sauce give Filipino dishes their recognizable depth. Even when curry powder or imported spices are used, these local aromatics keep the dish grounded in Filipino taste. The Filipino palate often values balance: savory, slightly sweet, aromatic, and sometimes sour or salty. Curry-like dishes succeed when they fit this balance.

Migration, Religion, and Memory

Food also carries memory. For Muslim Filipino communities, dishes such as kulma are not everyday foods alone. They appear during weddings, religious celebrations, family reunions, and festivals. Preparing them can be an act of hospitality and cultural continuity. A pot of kulma may represent generosity, status, family pride, and connection to ancestors.

For migrants and overseas Filipino workers, curry-like dishes can become a way of carrying home abroad. A Filipino cook in another country may use whatever spices are available, but the act of cooking chicken curry or beef kulma can still evoke home. The recipe may change, but the emotional purpose remains.

This is why food history matters. Curry is not only about flavor. It is about movement, survival, belonging, and identity. When a Filipino family cooks kulma for Eid, when a carinderia serves kare-kare with rice, or when a household makes chicken curry on a rainy evening, they are participating in a long history of cultural exchange.

Beyond the Word “Curry”

The word curry is useful, but it can also be limiting. It can make people assume that all spiced dishes come from one tradition or that Filipino versions are merely imitations. In reality, Filipino curry belongs to a broad family of Southeast Asian foods shaped by trade, migration, and local adaptation.

A better way to understand Filipino curry is to see it as part of a continuum. At one end are dishes closely connected to South Asian korma, such as kulma. At another are dishes adapted through colonial encounters, such as kare-kare. Elsewhere are everyday coconut stews and chicken curries that use curry powder as one ingredient among many. All of these belong to Filipino cuisine because Filipino communities made them their own.

The Philippines did not receive curry passively. Filipino cooks selected, substituted, simplified, enriched, and reinvented. They used coconut instead of dairy, annatto instead of turmeric when necessary, peanuts for richness, fish sauce for savoriness, and local vegetables for texture. They served these dishes with rice and made them part of family life.

A Living Tradition

Filipino curry is still evolving. Restaurants in Manila and Mindanao now present kulma in both traditional and modern forms. Chefs may slow-cook beef for many hours, pair it with local grains such as adlai, or highlight ingredients such as sakurab and tiyula itum reductions. Home cooks continue to adjust recipes according to budget, availability, and preference. Overseas Filipinos recreate familiar dishes with ingredients from new markets.

This ongoing evolution is exactly what has always happened. Curry reached the Philippines through movement, and it continues to move through migration, tourism, media, and everyday family cooking. Its history is not finished. Every time a cook changes a spice level, substitutes an ingredient, or serves a dish at a new celebration, the tradition continues.

The story of curry in the Philippines reminds us that cuisine is never fixed. It is made by people: traders crossing seas, families sharing recipes, communities celebrating together, and cooks improvising with what they have. Filipino curry is not a copy of India, Thailand, Malaysia, or Indonesia. It is a Filipino expression of a much larger maritime world.

Conclusion

So, where did curry in the Philippines come from? It came from many places and many journeys. It came through ancient Indian Ocean trade, through Malay and Bornean connections, through Muslim communities in Mindanao and Sulu, through Indian sepoys and the British occupation of Manila, and through the everyday creativity of Filipino cooks. It came through coconut groves, coastal ports, market stalls, weddings, festivals, and family kitchens.

But most importantly, it became Filipino because Filipinos made it their own. Kulma carries the history of the Sulu Archipelago. Kare-kare carries the memory of colonial encounter and culinary improvisation. Chicken curry carries the comfort of the Filipino home. Together, they show that Filipino cuisine is not isolated or simple. It is connected, adaptive, regional, and deeply creative.

Curry in the Philippines is therefore best understood not as a single imported dish, but as a living tradition: a story of spices crossing the seas, meeting local ingredients, and becoming part of Filipino identity.
