Why Maoism No Longer Works in the Philippines

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · Local Politics · November 18, 2026

The Communist Party of the Philippines, the New People’s Army, and the National Democratic Front have spent nearly six decades pursuing a revolution modeled on Maoist armed struggle. Yet the Philippines they seek to transform no longer resembles the country in which that strategy was first conceived. The countryside is more connected, the economy is more diversified, political participation is broader, and the state—despite its serious failures—has developed institutions and capacities that make a rural-based, protracted war increasingly difficult to sustain.

This does not mean that poverty, inequality, landlessness, corruption, political violence, and state abuse have disappeared. They remain real and urgent. It means, rather, that the CPP-NPA-NDF must confront an uncomfortable question: if its Maoist strategy has not produced national victory after decades of sacrifice, why should it continue asking young Filipinos to risk their lives for it?

A movement that claims to speak for the oppressed must be capable of honest self-criticism. If it cannot demonstrate a credible path to victory, it has a moral obligation to reassess its ideology, strategy, and recruitment practices. Otherwise, it risks becoming less a revolutionary force for liberation and more an armed organization preserving its own institutional survival.

A Doctrine Born in Another Time

Maoism, as understood by the CPP, rests on several core ideas: that revolutionary change requires armed struggle; that the countryside can surround and eventually overcome the cities; that peasants are a decisive revolutionary force; and that a protracted people’s war can gradually build base areas, expand political influence, and ultimately defeat the existing state.

These ideas emerged from China’s particular historical experience. Mao Zedong’s revolution developed in a vast, predominantly agrarian country marked by foreign invasion, civil war, weak central authority, and enormous rural deprivation. In that context, guerrilla warfare, rural base areas, and peasant mobilization were not merely abstract theories. They were practical responses to specific conditions.

When Jose Maria Sison and other founders established the CPP in 1968, they adapted this framework to the Philippines. The country then had a large rural population, deep inequalities in land ownership, weak state presence in remote areas, and an authoritarian political system that would later deteriorate further under martial law. Armed struggle appeared to many young activists as a rational response to a society they believed could not be changed through ordinary politics.

The CPP’s founding generation was not simply motivated by abstract ideology. Many were responding to genuine injustices: landlords exploiting tenants, rural poverty, political exclusion, corruption, foreign influence, and state repression. The movement’s appeal was strongest when it could present itself as the only serious alternative to a deeply unequal order.

But historical context changes. A strategy appropriate to one era may become obsolete in another. The Philippines of the 1960s and 1970s is not the Philippines of today. The CPP-NPA-NDF cannot continue treating Maoist doctrine as if it were immune from history.

The Philippines Has Changed

The most obvious change is demographic and geographic. The Philippines is no longer a predominantly rural, agrarian society in the way it was when the CPP was founded. Millions of Filipinos now live in cities, towns, and peri-urban communities. The economy has expanded beyond agriculture into services, manufacturing, business process outsourcing, construction, retail, tourism, logistics, and digital work.

This transformation matters because Maoist strategy depends heavily on the countryside. It assumes that rural base areas can be established, protected, and expanded; that peasants can be organized into a revolutionary force; and that guerrilla units can operate in terrain where the state has limited reach.

Today, the Philippine state has greater administrative, military, communications, and intelligence capacity than it did decades ago. Roads, mobile networks, satellite communications, drones, digital databases, local government structures, and improved military mobility have altered the balance between insurgent movements and the state. Remote areas are no longer as isolated as they once were.

This does not make state power invincible. It does mean that the old Maoist assumption—that revolutionaries can indefinitely build liberated zones in the countryside while the cities remain passive—has become harder to sustain.

The Philippine economy has also changed in ways that complicate the Maoist narrative. While rural poverty remains severe in many areas, the country’s economic life is no longer centered solely on landlord-tenant relations or agricultural production. Many Filipinos depend on wages, small businesses, overseas employment, remittances, informal work, transport, services, and digital platforms.

A revolutionary movement that speaks mainly in the language of agrarian revolution may fail to connect with workers in cities, gig workers, call-center employees, migrant workers, small entrepreneurs, professionals, students, and urban poor communities. These groups experience exploitation and insecurity, but not always in the same form imagined by classical Maoist theory.

The CPP-NPA-NDF therefore faces a strategic contradiction. Its ideology was built for a rural, agrarian, semi-feudal society. The Philippines has become more urban, more globally connected, more service-oriented, and more digitally mediated. If the movement cannot translate its analysis into a credible program for contemporary Filipino life, its revolutionary claim weakens.

The Problem of Perpetual War

Maoist theory describes armed struggle as protracted. It expects revolution to take years, decades, or even generations. This may have made sense in a historical moment when revolutionaries believed they were participating in a worldwide wave of anti-colonial and socialist movements.

But “protracted” can become a dangerous excuse. If victory is always somewhere in the future, leaders can avoid accountability for repeated failures. Every setback can be explained as a temporary tactical reversal. Every loss can be reframed as a necessary sacrifice. Every generation of recruits can be told that victory is near, even when the strategic balance has not fundamentally changed.

After nearly six decades, the CPP-NPA-NDF must ask whether it is pursuing a revolution or maintaining a war. A revolution aims to transform society. A war can continue indefinitely if an organization has weapons, territory, recruits, financing, and a narrative that justifies continued fighting.

The evidence suggests that the NPA has contracted significantly from its historical peak. Government figures and independent conflict-monitoring organizations have described a decline in armed encounters, weakened guerrilla fronts, loss of key leaders, and reduced territorial influence. These claims should always be examined critically, because the state has an obvious interest in portraying the insurgency as defeated.

Still, the broader pattern is difficult to ignore. The NPA no longer appears capable of launching a nationwide strategic offensive. It has not established a durable alternative government across large parts of the country. It has not mobilized a mass armed uprising capable of overthrowing the Philippine state. Its areas of influence have become more localized, fragmented, and vulnerable.

If the movement’s own theory predicted a progression from strategic defensive to strategic stalemate to strategic offensive, then decades without a decisive breakthrough should prompt serious reflection. Instead of asking whether the strategy is failing, the movement risks repeating the same cycle: recruit, fight, suffer losses, rebuild, and repeat.

The Cost Falls on the Young

The people most affected by this failure are often the youngest and most idealistic. Students, community organizers, rural youth, activists, and children of poor families may be drawn to revolutionary politics because they see injustice around them and want meaningful change.

These recruits deserve honesty. They deserve to know the risks of armed struggle, the likelihood of death or imprisonment, the possibility of prolonged separation from family, and the real prospects of victory. They deserve to know whether the organization has a realistic plan, not merely slogans about imperialism, feudalism, bureaucrat capitalism, and people’s war.

It is deeply troubling when intelligent, capable young people are encouraged to leave school, abandon professional opportunities, or enter armed conflict on the promise that their sacrifice will bring liberation. If the movement cannot show a credible route to victory, then sending them into combat is not empowerment. It is exploitation.

A revolutionary organization should not treat recruits as expendable symbols of commitment. It should not confuse willingness to die with political wisdom. Courage is admirable, but courage without strategy can become tragedy.

The CPP-NPA-NDF must ask whether it is developing young Filipinos into leaders capable of building a better society, or whether it is using them to sustain an armed structure that has outlived its strategic usefulness. The difference is fundamental.

Revolution Requires Results

A serious revolutionary movement must be judged by results. Has it improved the lives of the people it claims to represent? Has it expanded democratic participation? Has it protected communities from violence? Has it built durable institutions? Has it created a credible alternative to the existing political order?

The CPP-NPA-NDF has contributed to political consciousness, rural organizing, and resistance to authoritarianism at certain points in Philippine history. It would be dishonest to deny that the movement has influenced generations of activists and shaped national political discourse.

But influence is not the same as victory. Moral outrage is not the same as a viable program. The ability to criticize the state is not the same as the ability to replace it with something better.

The movement’s continuing armed struggle has also imposed costs on communities caught in conflict. Villages can become militarized. Families can be divided. Civilians can be caught between government forces and guerrilla units. Local development can be disrupted. Fear, displacement, and trauma can persist long after encounters end.

These consequences cannot be dismissed as unavoidable collateral damage. If a movement claims to fight for the people, it must be accountable to the people who bear the consequences of its war.

A genuine people’s movement would prioritize the safety, dignity, and future of communities. It would ask whether armed action improves their lives or endangers them. It would listen when communities say they want peace, education, healthcare, livelihood, land security, and freedom from fear.

The Need for Ideological Honesty

Ideology should help people understand reality. It should not become a prison that prevents them from seeing reality.

Maoism gave the CPP a coherent worldview: imperialism, feudalism, and bureaucrat capitalism; class struggle; people’s war; the leadership of the working class; the strategic role of the peasantry; and the eventual transition to socialism. This framework once gave activists a sense of purpose and direction.

But ideology becomes dangerous when it is treated as unquestionable truth. If every failure is blamed on traitors, infiltrators, reformism, revisionism, or insufficient revolutionary commitment, then the movement never learns. If every criticism is treated as enemy propaganda, then internal debate disappears. If every setback is interpreted as proof that members must sacrifice more, then the organization becomes trapped in a cycle of self-justification.

The CPP-NPA-NDF needs ideological honesty. It must distinguish between enduring principles—justice, equality, dignity, national sovereignty, workers’ rights, and democratic participation—and outdated strategic formulas that no longer fit Philippine conditions.

It must ask whether the country still has a large revolutionary peasant base. It must ask whether armed struggle still enjoys broad popular support. It must ask whether its military actions strengthen or weaken its political legitimacy. It must ask whether its cadres are building institutions or merely surviving underground.

Most importantly, it must ask whether its theory still explains the Philippines as it actually exists.

The Limits of Foreign Symbols

In recent years, some materials associated with legal-front organizations and online militant networks have increasingly used symbols drawn from foreign conflicts. Palestinian flags, keffiyehs, and slogans of resistance have appeared in posts, memes, rallies, and social-media campaigns.

Solidarity with oppressed peoples is not inherently wrong. Filipinos have every right to oppose injustice anywhere in the world. They may condemn civilian deaths, displacement, occupation, collective punishment, and violations of international humanitarian law.

But there is a difference between principled solidarity and political imitation. When foreign symbols become a substitute for local analysis, activism risks becoming performative. A Filipino activist may wear a keffiyeh or carry a Palestinian flag while having little serious understanding of the history, politics, and human complexity of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict.

The Israeli-Palestinian conflict cannot be reduced to a simple story of pure victims and pure villains. Jews have deep historical, religious, and cultural connections to the land of Israel. The Jewish people endured centuries of diaspora, persecution, pogroms, and ultimately the Holocaust. Modern Israel emerged in 1948 as the realization of a national movement that sought security and self-determination for a people repeatedly denied both.

At the same time, the establishment of Israel occurred in a land where Palestinians also lived, had deep roots, and possessed their own national aspirations. The creation of the state was accompanied by war, displacement, and enduring conflict. Palestinians have suffered occupation, statelessness, violence, restrictions on movement, and the loss of homes and livelihoods.

A responsible position must recognize both realities. Jews have a legitimate historical connection to Israel, and Palestinians have legitimate rights, grievances, and claims to self-determination. Any movement that denies either truth is not pursuing justice. It is pursuing propaganda.

For Filipino activists, the deeper concern is strategic. Importing the language and imagery of a distant conflict can distract from the urgent struggles in the Philippines: land rights, labor protections, corruption, disaster preparedness, healthcare, education, housing, and political accountability.

It can also make local organizations vulnerable to manipulation. Foreign conflicts can become emotional shorthand for radical identity. Instead of studying Philippine society, activists may adopt slogans that make them feel morally pure but do little to improve Filipino lives.

A mature movement does not need to borrow another people’s war to prove its seriousness. It needs to understand its own country.

The Danger of Propaganda Over Analysis

Social media has made political messaging faster, louder, and more visually powerful. A flag, a slogan, a meme, or a photograph can mobilize emotion quickly. But emotion is not analysis.

When political organizations rely on simplistic imagery, they may encourage supporters to see the world as a battle between heroes and villains. This can be useful for recruitment, but it is poor preparation for governing, negotiating, building institutions, or solving complex problems.

The Philippines needs activists who can distinguish between solidarity and sloganism. It needs people who can condemn violence against civilians without denying the historical rights and security concerns of either Israelis or Palestinians. It needs people who can oppose authoritarianism without romanticizing armed struggle. It needs people who can criticize capitalism without pretending that every social problem has a single revolutionary solution.

The CPP-NPA-NDF should be especially cautious. If its legal fronts become associated with foreign propaganda, simplistic narratives, or inflammatory symbolism, they may weaken their credibility among Filipinos who want justice but do not want to be recruited into another country’s conflict.

Political maturity means being able to say: “We support human rights, but we will not reduce a tragic and complicated conflict to a meme.” It means being able to say: “We oppose oppression, but we will not use other people’s suffering to recruit Filipino youth into a failing war.”

A Better Path for the Philippine Left

The Philippine left does not need to abandon its concern for the poor, workers, peasants, Indigenous peoples, women, and marginalized communities. It needs to abandon the belief that Maoist armed struggle is the only legitimate form of political action.

There are many ways to struggle for justice without asking young people to die in the mountains.

Community organizing can build local power. Labor unions can protect workers. Peasant organizations can fight for land rights and rural development. Legal advocacy can challenge abusive policies. Investigative journalism can expose corruption. Electoral politics can give citizens a voice in government. Cooperatives can build economic alternatives. Public education can strengthen democratic participation. Local governance can deliver services directly to communities.

None of these methods is perfect. Elections can be manipulated. Courts can be slow. Unions can be co-opted. Civil society can be fragmented. But these methods do not require young people to take up arms against the state.

They also create possibilities for reform that armed struggle cannot easily provide. A community organizer who helps farmers secure land tenure, a union leader who wins better wages, a lawyer who defends political prisoners, or a local official who improves public services may achieve concrete improvements that a guerrilla fighter cannot.

This is not reformism for its own sake. It is realism. It recognizes that people live in the present, not in a distant revolutionary future. They need healthcare now. They need jobs now. They need safety now. They need education now. They need protection from violence now.

A movement that cannot improve present conditions while claiming to promise future liberation has lost touch with the people it claims to serve.

What the CPP-NPA-NDF Must Ask Itself

The CPP-NPA-NDF should conduct an honest internal assessment. It should ask whether its founding assumptions still hold. It should ask whether its strategy has produced the results it promised. It should ask whether its recruits understand the real risks and real prospects of the struggle.

It should ask whether the NPA still has mass support in the countryside. It should ask whether its guerrilla fronts are growing or shrinking. It should ask whether communities see the movement as protector or burden. It should ask whether armed actions advance political goals or merely sustain military activity.

It should ask whether its leaders are accountable to the people. It should ask whether internal criticism is permitted. It should ask whether dissent is met with dialogue or suspicion. It should ask whether the organization has become more committed to its own survival than to the liberation of the Filipino people.

These are difficult questions. But difficult questions are the price of political seriousness.

A movement that cannot question itself cannot renew itself. A movement that cannot admit failure cannot correct failure. A movement that cannot distinguish between sacrifice and waste cannot lead anyone toward a better future.

Peace Is Not Surrender

Critics may argue that calls for reassessment are calls for surrender. They are not.

Peace is not surrender when it prevents further deaths. Dialogue is not betrayal when it opens possibilities for justice. Political participation is not passivity when it allows communities to shape the decisions that affect their lives.

The Philippine government must also do its part. It cannot demand that rebels lay down arms while ignoring land conflict, extrajudicial violence, corruption, poverty, and political exclusion. A credible peace process must address the roots of conflict, not merely offer amnesty and reintegration.

But the burden of reassessment is not only on the state. It is also on the CPP-NPA-NDF. A movement that claims moral superiority must hold itself to a higher standard. It cannot demand accountability from the government while refusing accountability for its own failures.

Peace should not mean the return of the old order. It should mean a political settlement that expands rights, protects communities, addresses land and labor injustice, and creates space for democratic participation.

That is not surrender. That is responsibility.

The Future Cannot Wait

The Philippines faces serious challenges: poverty, inequality, political corruption, climate vulnerability, disaster risk, unemployment, underemployment, weak public services, and regional insecurity. These problems will not be solved by slogans alone. They will not be solved by foreign symbols. They will not be solved by a protracted war that has failed to deliver national victory.

They will be solved by Filipinos who organize, vote, build institutions, defend human rights, create livelihoods, improve communities, and demand accountable government.

The CPP-NPA-NDF has a choice. It can continue treating Maoism as an unquestionable doctrine and continue asking new generations to sacrifice themselves for a war with no clear end. Or it can recognize that the Philippines has changed, admit that its strategy has not achieved its promised victory, and begin imagining a politics of justice that does not require more graves.

The first path preserves the organization. The second path may preserve the people.

If the movement truly believes in the Filipino people, it must choose the second path. It must stop treating ideology as an excuse for indefinite war. It must stop sending intelligent, hopeful young Filipinos into a conflict it cannot win. And it must begin building a future in which courage is measured not by willingness to die, but by the ability to create a society worth living in.
