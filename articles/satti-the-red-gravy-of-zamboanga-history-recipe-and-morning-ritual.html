Satti: The Red Gravy of Zamboanga

📖 16 min read Food Culture Zamboanga
Published today by Glenn Junsay Pansensoy

You step into Zamboanga City and a few things strike at once. The barter markets hum with bargaining voices. Sardine cans are stacked in neat rows, like small metallic altars. And there is the musical lilt of Chavacano, that Spanish-born creole that has earned the city its old nickname, Asia's Latin City.

But for many who live here, the true introduction comes through the nose. Before the sun has properly cleared the water, a smoky, spicy scent drifts down the street from a satti house, and the day begins to make sense.

It is a simple bowl: small skewers of grilled meat, a few chunks of rice, and a flood of red sauce. And yet it carries the map of centuries in its taste.

The Morning Ritual

The first thing a visitor must learn is that satti is not an evening dish. It is a breakfast, and a serious one. By six in the morning the charcoal is already glowing in the small shops that locals call sattihan, and the first customers are already on the benches. Some are fishermen in from the early tide. Some are office workers in pressed shirts. Some are schoolchildren with their parents, swinging their feet under the plastic tables. Nobody dresses up. Nobody lingers over a menu, because there is hardly any menu to linger over.

The order is almost always the same. You ask for the meat you like, whether beef, chicken, or liver, and you ask for rice. Then you wait, and the waiting is part of the pleasure. Behind the counter, a cook fans the coals with a piece of cardboard or a woven fan, and the skewers sizzle on the grill. The fat spits. Smoke curls up and gets into your hair and your clothes, and you will carry it with you well into the afternoon. Nobody complains about this.

Then the bowl arrives. It is deep rather than wide, and the sauce sits in it almost to the rim. Skewers rest on top, their ends sticking out like oars. Beneath them lie neat, firm blocks of rice, cooked in woven coconut leaves. The sauce is the color of a late tropical sunset, somewhere between orange and red, and it steams.

You eat quickly, because satti is meant to be eaten hot. By the third bite you are sweating. By the fifth you have stopped caring that you are sweating. It is a breakfast meant to wake up the senses and to fuel a long day of work on the water, in the market, or in the classroom. One food writer described the sauce as thick and vivid, sweet and spicy at once, and that is as good a summary as any.

A Dish with Many Names

Visitors who arrive expecting to find "satay" will find that nobody here calls it that. The people of Zamboanga say satti, and the shops that serve it are sattihan. The word is close enough to the Malay satay, or sate, that its family tree is obvious. But the change in spelling and sound is a quiet declaration. This dish belongs to the people who cook it, not to the Malay world it came from, and not to the rest of the Philippines either.

Walk through the city and you will find satti shops with the ease and humility of neighborhood institutions. Some are tiny and carinderia-like, a counter, a few stools, a grill by the door. A travel blogger who ate his way through the city singled out Andy's Special Satti on C. Atilano Street in Barangay Tetuan as one of three famous satti houses, alongside Jimmy's Sati and Johnny's Morning Sun Satti, and noted that all of them were cheap, filling, and humble. They are the kind of places where the owner knows half the customers by name and the other half by what they usually order.

That is part of what makes satti so Zamboangueño. It is not a dish for special occasions. It is eaten on an ordinary Tuesday as casually as water, and it is also worth crossing the city for if someone swears the sauce at a certain stall is particularly good.

The Roads of the Sea

Satti did not appear from nowhere. It is the child of tides and tongues.

Long before modern nation-states drew their lines across the water, boats threaded the Sulu Sea and touched the shores where Zamboanga now stands. Tausug merchants from the Sulu archipelago, Sama seafarers, Malay and Indonesian traders in boats with woven sails, and merchants from farther away, following the monsoon winds and the promise of profit, all passed through. The sea was not a barrier in those days. It was a highway, and Zamboanga sat at one of its crossroads.

What traveled on that highway was never only cargo. Sailors carried words and recipes along with sacks of spice and bolts of cloth. According to common accounts, Arab, Malay, and Indonesian spice merchants brought Islam and their culinary habits to the Tausug, the Sama, and the Zamboangueños who already lived along these coasts. The same routes that moved silk and porcelain also moved, in a way no ledger recorded, the idea of meat threaded on a stick and cooked over fire.

Historians can say with confidence that the satay tradition is Southeast Asian, and that satti is most likely a descendant of it, probably imported from nearby Malaysia and the Malay world through the Sulu trade zone. They cannot say, because no record survives, which cook first adapted it, in which year, or in what kitchen. That anonymity is the way most great dishes come into the world. Nobody invents them. They accumulate.

Fire, Skewers, and Sauce

In the kitchens of the Tausug and their neighbors, skewered meat was grilled over coals and turned slowly while the cooks worked. It is not hard to picture the scene. Somewhere a woman bends over a wooden board, pounding chilies, garlic, and ginger. Somewhere a man splits bamboo into thin sticks and soaks them in water so that they will not burn. Coconut leaves lie in a bundle by the door, waiting to be folded into small pouches for the rice.

The grilling itself is simple, and its simplicity is a matter of pride. The meat, whether beef sirloin, chicken, or liver, is cut into very small cubes. A home recipe might season it with garlic, a little soy sauce, and a pinch of turmeric. Another might use a marinade with calamansi juice, coconut vinegar, garlic, ginger, minced chilies, and a touch of oregano. Each family has its own way, and each swears by it. The meat is threaded onto the skewer, three, four, or five pieces at a time, and laid over hot charcoal. Coconut charcoal, when it can be had, burns clean and gives a faint sweetness to the smoke. The cook turns the skewers every few minutes and brushes them with the marinade, sometimes using the crushed tip of a lemongrass stalk as a brush. After a few minutes on each side, the meat takes on a slight char at the edges and is ready.

Turmeric and tamarind, ginger and lemongrass, the marinades of the Malay world, certainly belong to the general vocabulary of the region's grilling. It is tempting to say that every one of them traveled into Zamboanga on the same boats, and some probably did. But the exact path of each ingredient cannot be traced. What can be said is that these flavors overlap across the whole maritime Southeast Asian world, and that Zamboanga, being a crossroads, absorbed many of them.

Over the centuries, other influences braided in. The Spanish arrived and built Fort Pilar, and the city took on a Hispanic overlay of language, saints' days, and surnames. Chinese traders came, as they did everywhere in the archipelago. Each wave left something in the kitchen. It is no accident that Zamboanga's table today holds paella and callos beside curries and sambals, or that a Tausug platter of beef kulma and chicken pyanggang might be eaten a few streets from a restaurant serving seafood paella. The city is a place where traditions do not so much compete as share the same table.

The Sauce That Defines It

If the skewers are the body of satti, the sauce is its soul.

In Malaysia and Indonesia, satay is usually served on a dry platter, with a modest dish of peanut sauce on the side for dipping. In Zamboanga, the relationship is reversed. The skewers do not sit beside the sauce; they swim in it. That change tells you something about the people who made it. Filipinos in the south, like Filipinos nearly everywhere, love their rice with something to soak into it, and the sauce answers that wish. It transforms the dish from a snack into a meal.

The sauce itself is a quiet marvel of thrift and patience. It begins with a broth, either beef or chicken, to which minced onion, garlic, ginger, and often a stalk of pounded lemongrass are added. Hot bird's-eye chilies go in next, as many as the cook dares. The red color comes from annatto, the small brick-red seeds known locally as atsuete, rubbed in warm water until the liquid turns a deep, glowing crimson. A little brown sugar rounds out the heat. Peanut is present, but only as a whisper. A spoonful of peanut butter, or a handful of ground peanuts, gives the sauce a gentle body and a faint nutty memory of its Malay ancestor. As one food writer put it, the sauce carries only a "very subtle hint" of peanuts.

Then comes the thickening. Cornstarch dissolved in water, or in some kitchens cassava, is stirred in until the sauce turns glossy and clinging. It is no longer a dipping sauce. It is something closer to a soup, or a thin gravy, thick enough to coat a spoon and loose enough to flow down into the crevices of the rice.

This is why, in the telling of Zamboangueños, satti is not the peanut-dressed satay of Java. It is not the sweet soy skewers of Indonesia, glazed with kecap manis. It is its own child: a red-gravied, chili-bright, slightly sweet dish, spicy enough to make your forehead bead, but gentle enough that a child can eat it. Different cooks push the balance in different directions. Some make theirs sweeter, almost orange. Others lean on the chili until the bowl takes on a harder edge. But the family resemblance is unmistakable, and no one who has eaten one bowl will mistake it for another dish.

Puso, the Hanging Rice

No account of satti would be complete without puso.

Puso, which means "heart" in Filipino, is rice boiled inside a pouch of woven coconut leaves. The leaves are folded and braided into small diamond shapes, filled with raw rice, and dropped into boiling water. As the rice cooks, it swells to fill the pouch, and the tight weave presses it into a firm, dense block. When the cooking is done, the pouch is cut open and the rice slides out in a neat, compact shape that holds together on the spoon.

In the market, puso is sold in bunches, tied with a string and hung from poles or hooks, which is why many people call it "hanging rice." On a morning stroll you will see them dangling like green lanterns above the stalls. They are cheap and portable, and they last well in the tropical heat, which is one reason they have been the rice of choice for travelers and fishermen across the southern seas for generations.

In a bowl of satti, the puso is chopped into chunks and settles at the bottom, where it slowly drinks up the sauce. This is the pleasure of the dish. The first bites are all grilled meat and sharp heat. The last bites are all rice, soaked through with sauce and sweetness and smoke. A good bowl ends with the spoon scraping the bottom, and nobody in Zamboanga considers that impolite.

Some places serve ordinary steamed rice instead. Nobody will say no to this. But to many locals, satti without puso is a little like a song missing a verse.

The Market and the Sea

To understand why satti belongs here, it helps to understand the city around it.

Zamboanga sits at the southwestern tip of Mindanao, nearer to Sabah and the Sulu islands than to Manila, in distance and in outlook. Its harbor opens onto the Sulu Sea and the Basilan Strait. Its markets, especially the barter trade stalls, have long been places where goods from the southern islands and from across the border change hands. Sacks of dried fish, bolts of patterned cloth, packaged goods from Sabah, and spices of every kind are sold and haggled over. The bargaining voices form a hum that rises and falls with the tides.

The sea gives the city its living and its character. Zamboanga is often called the sardine capital of the Philippines, and the stacked tins in its shops are a visible sign of that. The waters also yield curacha, a sweet spanner crab the city has made famous, along with imbao clams, prawns, and squid. A traveler could eat well here for a week without touching a skewer.

And yet satti holds its place. It stands where sea trade meets land cooking, Moro meets Chavacano, and Tausug meets Zamboangueño. It is eaten by Muslims and Christians alike, and it has been shared, quietly and daily, for longer than anyone can remember.

Language of the Table

The Chavacano language gives a clue to how the city thinks about its food. Chavacano is a creole, born when Spanish met the speech of the peoples already living here. It borrowed Spanish vocabulary and wrapped it in local grammar, producing something new and entirely its own. The city's table follows the same pattern. There is ensalada de chamba, a salad of eggplant and tomato topped with salted egg. There is beef kulma, simmered in curry paste, and tyulah itum, the dark Tausug beef soup made with burnt coconut. Some dishes carry Spanish names and some carry Tausug names, and no one at the table stops to ask which is which. Satti sits comfortably in this company, with a Malay ancestor, a Tausug upbringing, and a Zamboangueño home.

The Making of a Memory

Ask a Zamboangueño about satti, and the answer often begins not with a recipe but with a memory.

Imagine the Sunday mornings at the same stall, the rainy day of fogged-up glasses and sauce ladled over rice while the street outside turned to rivers, the morning after a night of too little sleep when only a hot bowl of red gravy could pull a person back to life.

These memories are what keep the dish alive. A recipe can be written down and copied, but the shared experience of eating it cannot. For people who have left the city to work in Manila, or overseas, satti is one of the first things they ask for when they return. Even a Manila restaurant, Satti, has been opened to serve Mindanao cuisine under the dish's name.

There is something else about satti that is easy to miss. It is a democratic food. A bowl costs very little, and the same bowl is served to the driver and the doctor. The benches are shared. The conversation across them is often loud, drifting between Chavacano, Tagalog, Tausug, and English, sometimes within a single sentence. In a city that has known conflict and division, the morning satti house is one of the places where ordinary people, regardless of religion or background, simply sit down together and eat. It would be an exaggeration to say a bowl of satti keeps the peace. But it would not be an exaggeration to say it reminds people, every morning, of how much they share.

Fire and Heat

Heat is the thread that runs through everything about satti. There is the heat of the charcoal, the heat of the chili, and the heat of the morning. There is the heat that rises from the bowl and fogs your face. There is the heat of arguments and laughter around the table.

The people of Zamboanga do not shy away from heat. They seem to treat it as a form of welcome, and the red sauce of satti is a kind of edible hospitality. Yet there is subtlety in it as well. The sweetness balances the chili, the faint nuttiness softens the bite, and the smoke of the grill lingers underneath. The best bowls do not shout; they hum.

A Dish in Motion

Like all living foods, satti keeps changing. Younger cooks experiment with new cuts of meat and new kinds of heat. Some shops offer a milder bowl for visitors and a fiercer one for regulars. Food bloggers photograph it, and the pictures travel far beyond the city. Some residents worry that this attention will flatten the dish into something too polished and cautious. Others welcome it, seeing recognition for a city often described in the news for the wrong reasons. Both views are probably right. Food traditions survive by being loved, and they change by being loved too. Still, it is hard to imagine a Zamboanga without the morning smoke, the hanging rice, and the red bowl set down in front of a hungry customer who has not yet fully woken up.

What the Bowl Remembers

Come back, then, to the bowl on the table.

It is only a few dozen grams of grilled meat, a few cubes of rice, and a ladle of sauce. It costs less than a cup of coffee in a Manila mall. And yet inside it you can taste the long, patient history of a sea crossing: the monsoon winds that carried the first sails, the traders who bargained at the shore, the cooks who adjusted the recipe one pinch at a time. You can taste the Spanish centuries and the Malay ones, the Tausug hearth and the Chavacano street. You can taste the chili of today's cook and the memory of yesterday's grandmother.

Satti does not announce its history. It simply carries it, the way the sea carries a boat. And the next time someone asks what Zamboanga tastes like, the answer is not hard to find. It tastes like smoke at six in the morning, like heat on the tongue and sweetness at the edges. It tastes like red gravy, glossy and bright, pooling around a block of rice cooked in a leaf.

It tastes, in the end, like home to those who grew up here, and like an invitation to everyone else.
