Why Life Feels So Difficult in the Philippines Right Now

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · Economy · November 25, 2026

Many Filipinos feel that getting ahead has become harder than ever. Prices continue to rise, job opportunities remain limited, and many people work long hours without seeing meaningful improvement in their lives. This article explores ten major reasons behind the country’s economic struggles—and why feeling exhausted does not mean you are lazy.

1. The Cost of Living Keeps Rising

Basic goods have become more expensive, while wages have not grown at the same pace. Items such as rice, eggs, transportation, and electricity cost significantly more than they did years ago.

For many workers, the problem is not simply that prices are high. It is that their income has stayed almost the same while daily expenses continue to increase. When a large portion of one’s salary goes toward food, rent, utilities, and transportation, saving money or building a better future becomes extremely difficult.

2. Businesses Are Struggling to Survive

Small enterprises are especially vulnerable to rising operating costs, weak consumer spending, high rent, and complicated regulations. When businesses close, workers lose jobs, suppliers lose clients, and communities lose sources of income.

The effects go beyond business owners. A closed shop can mean fewer opportunities for employees, delivery riders, suppliers, and young people looking for their first job. When businesses cannot survive, employment becomes even harder to find.

3. There Are More Job Seekers Than Available Jobs

Every year, thousands of Filipinos finish school and enter the labor market. However, the number of quality jobs does not always grow at the same rate.

This creates intense competition. When many qualified applicants compete for a small number of positions, employers gain more bargaining power. Wages may remain low because workers feel they have limited alternatives, even when they are skilled and hardworking.

4. Starting a Business Can Be Difficult

Entrepreneurship is often presented as the path to financial freedom, but starting and maintaining a business in the Philippines can be challenging. Business owners may face registration requirements, taxes, permits, rent, competition, and other expenses before they become profitable.

For small entrepreneurs, one mistake or slow month can lead to serious financial trouble. Larger companies often have more capital, connections, and resources to survive difficult periods, while smaller businesses may be forced to shut down.

5. Corruption Undermines Public Services

When public funds are misused or stolen, ordinary citizens pay the price. Money intended for roads, hospitals, schools, and community programs may fail to reach the people who need it most.

Poor infrastructure, underfunded hospitals, and overcrowded classrooms are not just inconveniences. They affect health, safety, education, and long-term economic opportunity. Corruption also weakens trust in government and makes people feel that hard work alone may not be enough to improve their situation.

6. National Debt Affects Everyone

Government debt does not remain an abstract number. It can influence taxes, public services, inflation, and the cost of essential goods and services.

When a country carries a heavy debt burden, future generations may inherit the consequences. Citizens may end up paying more through taxes and higher prices, while receiving fewer improvements in public services. This makes financial pressure feel like something passed down from one generation to the next.

7. Dependence on Imports Weakens Local Producers

The Philippines imports many products, including food items that could potentially be produced locally. While imports can help meet demand, heavy dependence on them can hurt local farmers, manufacturers, and small producers.

If imported goods are cheaper or more readily available, local producers may struggle to compete. This can keep farming and local manufacturing less profitable, discouraging investment and limiting job creation in rural communities.

8. Gambling and Scams Have Become More Visible

Online gambling, betting platforms, investment scams, and questionable “easy money” schemes have become increasingly visible on social media. Some are promoted by influencers, making them appear normal or even glamorous.

The danger is that people may risk money they need for essentials, hoping for a quick win. Instead of improving their financial situation, they can fall deeper into debt or lose savings. Scams also exploit people’s desire to escape financial hardship, making caution more important than ever.

9. Education Does Not Always Lead to Stable Careers

Many Filipinos invest years and significant money into college education, hoping it will lead to a better life. However, graduation does not always guarantee a job related to one’s course or a salary that reflects the cost of education.

Some graduates end up working in jobs unrelated to their studies, such as retail, delivery services, or customer support. A diploma remains valuable, but it is no longer enough on its own. Practical skills, experience, digital literacy, and adaptability have become increasingly important.

10. Crab Mentality Holds People Back

In some communities, success can attract criticism instead of support. Rather than celebrating someone’s progress, people may gossip, compare, or try to pull them down.

This mindset can discourage ambition. When people fear judgment or resentment for trying to improve their lives, they may avoid opportunities or hide their success. A healthier culture would encourage mentorship, cooperation, and celebrating one another’s achievements.

The Bigger Picture

The difficulty many Filipinos feel is real. It is shaped by inflation, limited employment, weak local production, corruption, debt, and social pressures. These problems cannot be solved by simply telling people to “work harder.”

At the same time, individuals can still take steps to protect themselves: learn marketable skills, avoid scams and gambling, manage debt carefully, build emergency savings when possible, and seek opportunities beyond traditional employment. Personal effort matters, but it should not replace the need for fair wages, honest governance, stronger local industries, and better public services.

The message is simple: if you are struggling, you are not alone—and you are not lazy. The system around you may be difficult, but understanding it is the first step toward finding ways to move forward.
