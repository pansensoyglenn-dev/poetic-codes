Israel’s Election Ends in Deadlock, Leaving Netanyahu’s Future and the Country’s Direction Unresolved

By Glenn · October 31, 2026

TEL AVIV — Israel’s first national election since the October 7, 2023, Hamas-led attack has ended not with a decisive verdict but with a political stalemate. Final results released early Friday gave Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s governing bloc 53 seats in the 120-member Knesset—eight short of a majority—while the opposition bloc led by former military chief of staff Gadi Eisenkot won 48. Arab-led parties, holding a historic 19 seats, now hold the balance of power.

The outcome leaves Israel facing weeks, and possibly months, of coalition negotiations. Neither Netanyahu nor Eisenkot has an obvious route to the 61 seats required to form a government, and both men have publicly insisted that they will be the one to lead the next administration.

For Netanyahu, the result is a sharp rebuke even in defeat. His Likud party remains Israel’s largest, with 27 seats, but the coalition that supported him in the outgoing Knesset fell from 68 seats to 53. For Eisenkot, the election confirmed substantial public appetite for change—but also exposed the central obstacle confronting Israel’s anti-Netanyahu camp: its refusal to rely on Arab political representation.

A High-Turnout Rejection of the Status Quo

Israelis went to the polls on Tuesday in unusually large numbers. Final turnout reached 77.9 percent of eligible voters, with 5,716,665 ballots cast—the highest figure in decades, according to the Central Elections Committee.

The scale of participation underscored the stakes. This was the first nationwide vote since Hamas-led attackers killed about 1,200 people in Israel and took roughly 250 hostages on October 7, 2023, triggering the war in Gaza and a wider regional confrontation. The election was widely viewed as a referendum on Netanyahu’s leadership, his government’s security record, and the country’s political direction after more than three years of crisis.

Yet voters did not deliver a clear mandate to either camp. Netanyahu’s bloc retained the largest number of seats, but its losses were substantial. Likud dropped from 32 seats to 27, while Shas, one of its ultra-Orthodox allies, fell from 11 to 6.

The opposition gained ground but not enough to govern alone. Eisenkot’s Yashar party won 21 seats, while B’Yachad took 12, The Democrats 8, and Yisrael Beytenu 7. Together, the anti-Netanyahu bloc holds 48 seats.

The most striking shift came among Arab-led parties. The Joint List and Ra’am together won 19 seats, nearly doubling their previous representation and setting a record for Arab political strength in the Knesset. Palestinian citizens of Israel make up about 21 percent of the country’s population, and their parties’ surge has transformed them from peripheral players into potential kingmakers.

The Arithmetic of Power

Israel’s proportional electoral system makes coalition-building the decisive phase of every election. Voters choose party lists, and the resulting Knesset must produce a government capable of winning confidence votes from at least 61 of its 120 members.

The official results create a starkly divided parliament:

Bloc Seats Position
Netanyahu bloc 53 Largest bloc, but short of majority
Eisenkot bloc 48 Largest opposition force, but short of majority
Arab-led parties 19 Potential kingmakers

Netanyahu would need to attract roughly eight additional lawmakers to reach a majority. Eisenkot, if he continues to exclude the Arab parties, would need about 13 defectors—an exceptionally difficult task.

The Arab parties could, on paper, push an Eisenkot-led government comfortably past the majority threshold. A partnership between his bloc and the Arab factions would command more than 67 seats. But that path collides with campaign promises. Eisenkot and several allies ruled out depending on the Joint List or Ra’am, citing disagreements over Hamas and the war in Gaza.

That refusal has frustrated many liberal and Arab voters who hoped the election would end Netanyahu’s rule. Outside Eisenkot’s residence in Herzliya, left-wing activists demonstrated this week for a government that would include Arab parties.

How Government Formation Works

Under Israeli law, the Central Elections Committee will present the final results to President Isaac Herzog on Wednesday, after completing any outstanding inquiries into complaints or errors. Herzog will then consult every party leader, each of whom may recommend a candidate for the first attempt at forming a government.

The president is not required to select the leader of the largest party. Instead, he must choose the candidate he judges most likely to assemble a viable coalition. That person receives 28 days to secure support from at least 61 lawmakers, with a possible 14-day extension. If that effort fails, Herzog may give another candidate the mandate.

Based on the seat distribution, Eisenkot appears more likely than Netanyahu to receive the largest number of recommendations from newly elected lawmakers. But recommendations are not seats. They signal parliamentary support for a candidate’s attempt to negotiate; they do not guarantee a government.

The Knesset is scheduled to be sworn in on November 10 regardless of whether a coalition has been formed. Officials and analysts expect the negotiations to be protracted, potentially lasting more than two months.

President Herzog has urged the parties to avoid another election. “We must do everything we can, certainly after yesterday, to avoid another round of elections,” he said, adding that he expected party leaders to arrive at consultations “with an open mind.”

Netanyahu’s Narrow Paths

Netanyahu’s most straightforward route to survival would be to rebuild a majority from his existing bloc. But with 53 seats, he needs at least eight more lawmakers, and no obvious partner is available.

One possibility is a grand coalition with Eisenkot’s camp. Such a government could bring together Netanyahu’s right-wing bloc and moderate elements of the center and right, potentially stabilizing Israeli politics and easing cooperation with Washington on regional issues, including Gaza reconstruction and possible normalization with Saudi Arabia.

But the idea faces formidable political barriers. Eisenkot has repeatedly pledged not to serve in a government led by Netanyahu. The two men would also need to resolve who would serve as prime minister and whether the post would rotate between them.

Netanyahu may instead hope that time works in his favor. A prolonged stalemate could fracture the opposition, and another election could theoretically improve his position if Arab turnout declines or if voters conclude that the anti-Netanyahu camp cannot govern.

He also retains a powerful tool: the possibility of renewed military escalation. In his election-night speech, Netanyahu said Israel still needed to complete its mission against Iran and permanently remove what he described as an existential threat.

Analysts note that a renewed confrontation with Iran could reshape the coalition talks. A national emergency could increase pressure on Eisenkot to join a unity government, much as he did after October 7, when he entered Netanyahu’s emergency government and war cabinet before resigning eight months later.

“Netanyahu definitely has an incentive to go to war,” Danny Citrinowicz, an Israeli analyst on Iran, told NPR. “When you have an emergency in Israel, the pressure on Eisenkot to enter some sort of unity government would obviously mount.”

Eisenkot’s Dilemma

Eisenkot enters the negotiations with momentum but constrained options. A relative newcomer to electoral politics, the former military chief built his campaign around competence, national renewal, and an end to Netanyahu’s leadership.

His bloc’s 48 seats make him the natural focal point for the opposition. But his refusal to govern with Arab parties leaves him short of a majority.

He could attempt to form a minority government supported from outside by the Arab parties, an arrangement that would not require them to take cabinet posts. Even that, however, would contradict commitments made during the campaign.

Eisenkot’s alternative is to use the parliamentary interregnum to weaken Netanyahu. His bloc is considering an effort to replace the Knesset speaker, a powerful role that shapes the legislature’s agenda. It also hopes to advance legislation creating a state commission of inquiry into the political and security failures preceding the October 7 attack.

Such moves would not form a government, but they could allow the opposition to demonstrate governing capacity while negotiations continue. Eisenkot’s allies may also seek to reverse legislation passed under Netanyahu that critics say weakened judicial independence and democratic institutions.

Fraud Claims Rejected

The results were announced amid online claims of electoral manipulation, including reports of a possible Qatari influence campaign. The Central Elections Committee rejected allegations of widespread fraud, with acting director Dean Livne calling the reports “entirely unfounded.”

Livne said the investigation would not affect the final results submitted to the president and did not concern any particular sector of the population.

The statement appeared intended to head off efforts to delegitimize the outcome before coalition talks begin. Israel’s electoral system has repeatedly produced close results and contentious negotiations, but the committee’s rapid rejection of the fraud claims signaled that the official count will stand.

A Country at a Crossroads

The election has revealed an Israel deeply divided over security, governance, the war, and the role of Arab citizens in political life. It has also demonstrated the limits of personalization in a proportional system: Netanyahu remains the country’s most consequential political figure, yet his coalition has lost its parliamentary majority.

The next government will inherit a daunting agenda: the aftermath of war, the fate of hostages and displaced communities, Israel’s relationship with the United States, tensions with Iran, and mounting questions about democratic institutions and social cohesion.

For now, however, no leader can claim a mandate to address them. Netanyahu’s bloc has the largest number of seats; Eisenkot’s camp has the broader anti-Netanyahu coalition; and the Arab parties have unprecedented leverage.

Israel has been here before. Between 2019 and 2021, the country held five elections in roughly three years as repeated attempts to form a stable government failed. The current deadlock raises the possibility that the cycle could begin again.

The difference this time is the magnitude of the crisis surrounding it. After October 7, after wars across the region, and after an election that produced the highest turnout in decades, Israeli voters have delivered a message that is difficult to ignore: they want change—but they have not agreed on what that change should look like.

The Bottom Line

Israel’s election has produced a deadlocked Knesset: Netanyahu’s bloc holds 53 seats, Eisenkot’s opposition 48, and Arab-led parties a historic 19. With no obvious path to 61 seats for either camp, the country faces months of coalition negotiations—or the possibility of yet another election.

The deadlock reflects a deeper national divide over Netanyahu’s leadership, the war’s aftermath, and the political role of Arab citizens. Until one camp can assemble a majority, Israel’s direction will remain unresolved.
