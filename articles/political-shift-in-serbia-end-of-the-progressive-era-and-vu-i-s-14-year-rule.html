Political Shift in Serbia: End of the Progressive Era and Vučić's 14-Year Rule

A historic political earthquake ends nearly 14 years of dominance as a student-led movement converts grief and public anger into electoral power.

Belgrade

In a historic political earthquake that reshaped the Balkan nation’s political landscape, Aleksandar Vučić’s nearly 14-year reign over Serbia came to an end following the October 2026 snap parliamentary election. The student-led opposition movement known as the Student List, headed by cardiologist and university professor Ilija Srdanović, secured an outright parliamentary majority, defeating the Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) and its “United Serbia” coalition in an election that had seemed unthinkable only two years earlier.

The result was not merely a change of government. It marked the collapse of a political system built around one dominant party, one dominant leader, and a network of institutions that critics said had gradually been subordinated to executive power. For Serbia, a country at the crossroads of Central and Southeastern Europe, the election represented a rare moment in which a decentralized civic movement converted sustained public anger into electoral power.

Vučić, who had served as prime minister from 2014 to 2017 and as president from 2017 until his resignation in September 2026, conceded defeat on election night. “It is clear that we have lost these elections,” he told supporters at SNS headquarters in Belgrade, acknowledging that projections showed the Student List would win between 135 and 140 seats in the 250-member National Assembly, while his own coalition would receive about 105.

The Student List ultimately captured just over 52 percent of the vote, while Vučić’s coalition received approximately 39 percent. With an exceptionally high turnout approaching 69 percent, the opposition won enough mandates to govern alone, without needing to negotiate a coalition with smaller parties.

A Tragedy That Became a Catalyst

The origins of this political shift began on November 1, 2024, when a concrete canopy collapsed at the main railway station in Novi Sad, Serbia’s second-largest city. Sixteen people were killed. The station had recently undergone extensive renovation as part of a broader infrastructure modernization program, and the disaster immediately raised questions about construction quality, public procurement, regulatory oversight, and the transparency of contracts involving foreign state-linked companies.

In the days after the collapse, Serbians gathered in silent vigils for the victims. The mood was initially one of mourning rather than open confrontation. But grief soon gave way to anger as citizens demanded the release of all documentation connected to the station’s reconstruction, accountability for officials and contractors, and an independent investigation into how a newly renovated public structure could fail so catastrophically.

Protesters began using a phrase that would come to define the movement: “corruption kills.” Red handprints appeared on government buildings, a symbolic accusation that authorities had “blood on their hands.” Demonstrators blocked courthouses, roads, and university buildings, demanding that judicial institutions perform their duties without political interference.

The Novi Sad canopy collapse became more than a tragedy; it became a metaphor. To many Serbians, it embodied what they saw as the hazards of a state in which large public projects, political loyalty, opaque financing, and weak oversight had become intertwined. The fact that the station renovation was connected to major infrastructure initiatives involving Chinese state companies intensified scrutiny of the government’s development model.

From Mourning to Mobilization

What began as grief and calls for justice grew into a sustained civil movement. University students across Serbia organized blockades, rallies, strikes, and commemorative marches. They demanded judicial accountability, publication of documents, resignation or prosecution of those responsible, and broader institutional reform.

The students’ tactics were notable for their discipline and persistence. Rather than relying on a single charismatic politician, they built a horizontal movement rooted in faculties, local communities, and repeated public actions. Students marched from towns and villages across Serbia to Novi Sad to honor the victims, turning the anniversary of the disaster into a national act of remembrance and protest.

Over nearly two years, the student movement expanded into a broad coalition joined by academics, teachers, farmers, actors, cultural figures, and civic groups. This breadth mattered. Earlier opposition efforts had often been dismissed as urban, partisan, or elite-driven. The anti-corruption movement, by contrast, drew support from people who had not previously identified with formal opposition politics.

The movement’s central demand was not simply the removal of Vučić. It was the restoration of institutions capable of checking power: independent courts, transparent public administration, free media, credible elections, and accountability for abuse of public office. That message resonated in a country where many citizens had grown accustomed to viewing state institutions as extensions of the ruling party.

The Progressive Era

To understand the scale of the 2026 result, one must understand the political order that preceded it. Vučić and the SNS rose to power in the aftermath of Serbia’s turbulent post-Milošević transition, presenting themselves as a force of stability, state capacity, and pragmatic modernization. After becoming prime minister in 2014, Vučić consolidated control over the state apparatus and later moved to the presidency, retaining decisive influence over government policy even from that formally different office.

Under SNS rule, Serbia pursued large infrastructure projects, expanded road and rail networks, attracted foreign investment, and cultivated relationships with the European Union, Russia, China, and the United States. Supporters credited the government with improving infrastructure and maintaining stability in a volatile region. Critics, however, argued that modernization had come at the cost of democratic accountability.

Over time, international observers and domestic opposition figures described Serbia as drifting toward competitive authoritarianism: elections continued, opposition parties existed, and media outlets operated, but the ruling party enjoyed overwhelming advantages in state resources, media visibility, public employment, and institutional influence. The Student List’s victory challenged the assumption that such a system could not be defeated at the ballot box.

Vučić’s political style combined populist communication, tight party discipline, and a capacity to frame every political conflict as a struggle between Serbia and its external enemies. After his defeat, he blamed “shadowy foreign forces,” a familiar rhetorical pattern that sought to reinterpret a domestic civic revolt as foreign interference.

The Road to the Ballot Box

Facing civil unrest and mounting pressure, Vučić dissolved parliament in September 2026 and called early elections. In a decisive political gamble, he resigned the presidency on September 27, eight months before the end of his term, in order to lead the SNS campaign for prime minister. Under Serbia’s Constitution, the premiership carries more direct executive authority than the presidency, and Vučić sought to return to the office where his national political career had begun.

The decision reflected both confidence and necessity. Vučić had long been Serbia’s most formidable campaigner, capable of mobilizing state resources, dominating television coverage, and presenting himself as the indispensable guarantor of national stability. Yet the student movement had altered the political environment. It was not a conventional opposition party with a leader vulnerable to personal attacks; it was a decentralized civic list whose legitimacy came from nearly two years of protest.

The election was held on October 25, 2026. Pre-election polls had suggested a close race, but the result exceeded even many opposition expectations. The Student List presented a ticket of 250 candidates, including professors, actors, farmers, doctors, entrepreneurs, and local activists. Crucially, the list did not include the student organizers themselves; instead, it selected public figures nominated through the protest movement’s networks.

Ilija Srdanović, a cardiologist and professor at the Faculty of Medicine of the University of Novi Sad, was the first name on the list. He described himself as “first among equals,” reflecting the movement’s deliberate rejection of hierarchical party politics. The Student List did not initially name a candidate for prime minister, underscoring its collective and improvisational character.

The campaign centered on anti-corruption reform, judicial independence, institutional transparency, and the restoration of democratic norms. The Student List’s appeal was less a detailed ideological program than a promise to return public institutions to public control. Its candidates were largely unknown outside their communities, which paradoxically became an asset: voters could see them as representatives of civic renewal rather than as members of an entrenched political class.

Election Night

When polls closed, the country waited for projections with unusual tension. Less than two hours after voting ended, Vučić appeared before supporters and conceded. Citing stratified sampling data, he said the Student List would secure between 135 and 140 seats, while the SNS-led coalition would win approximately 105.

The concession was extraordinary in its speed and clarity. For years, critics had accused Serbian electoral institutions of favoring the ruling party, and opposition supporters had feared that a close result could be manipulated or prolonged through recounts, legal challenges, and administrative pressure. Instead, Vučić acknowledged the outcome before final official results were announced.

Partial results from the Republic Electoral Commission showed the Student List with 52.54 percent of the vote based on more than 8,000 processed polling stations. Later figures placed the movement at approximately 52.65 percent, with the SNS-led United Serbia coalition at 39.2 percent. Turnout reached 68.8 percent, an exceptionally high figure for a Serbian parliamentary election.

The Student List was projected to win about 140 of 250 seats, comfortably above the 126-seat threshold required for a majority. Vučić’s coalition was projected to receive roughly 103 to 105 seats. The scale of the victory meant that the incoming government would not need to depend on smaller parties or unstable coalition arrangements.

In Belgrade, tens of thousands gathered near parliament to celebrate. Some waved flags; others embraced, cried, or chanted slogans that had been heard at protests for nearly two years. For many young Serbians, the night represented not only a partisan victory but a personal vindication—the sense that sustained civic action could still change the direction of the country.

“Tonight Serbia Has Won”

Addressing supporters in Belgrade, Srdanović declared that “tonight Serbia has won.” He said the country was moving toward “a more civilized and better Serbia,” framing the result as a victory for citizens who had refused to accept corruption, impunity, and political stagnation.

His words captured the emotional core of the movement. The Student List had not presented itself as a conventional party seeking power for its own sake. Its leaders repeatedly emphasized that the election was a means to restore dignity to public life and accountability to state institutions. Srdanović said he was proud of “the people who stood up to tyranny,” language that reflected the movement’s belief that Serbia had been governed through fear, patronage, and control rather than genuine democratic consent.

The victory also carried a generational significance. The students who initiated the protests were often too young to remember the wars of the 1990s or the early post-Milošević transition. Their politics were shaped less by nostalgia or ethnic nationalism than by everyday encounters with corruption, precarious public services, restricted opportunity, and the sense that institutions served the powerful rather than ordinary citizens.

For Serbia’s older opposition voters, the Student List offered something different from the fragmented party landscape of previous years. It offered unity without a traditional party boss, moral clarity without ideological rigidity, and a practical route to power without requiring voters to abandon their local communities or everyday concerns.

What Lies Ahead

As the Student List prepares to form a government, the incoming coalition faces the task of managing economic priorities, implementing governance reforms, and navigating Serbia’s strategic positioning between the European Union, Russia, and China. These challenges are formidable, and the movement’s lack of governing experience may prove as consequential as its electoral appeal.

The first test will be governmental formation. Serbia’s new National Assembly must convene within 30 days of the announcement of final results. Although the Student List has an outright majority, it must still choose a prime minister, allocate ministries, and establish a coherent program of government. Srdanović is widely viewed as the most likely candidate for premier, but the movement has not formally confirmed its leadership structure.

The second test will be justice. Public prosecutors have reportedly reopened the investigation into the Novi Sad railway station collapse following Vučić’s defeat. Any new government will face pressure to ensure that investigations are independent, thorough, and free from political direction. Failure to deliver credible accountability could quickly disillusion the movement’s supporters.

The third test will be institutional reform. The Student List campaigned on rebuilding civil institutions and strengthening anti-corruption safeguards. That will require more than symbolic personnel changes. It will involve reforming prosecutorial and judicial governance, protecting independent media, depoliticizing public administration, increasing procurement transparency, and ensuring that regulatory bodies can act without fear of retaliation.

The fourth test will be economic governance. Serbia’s development model has relied heavily on infrastructure investment, foreign capital, and close ties with Chinese state enterprises. A new government cannot simply abandon these projects without economic consequences, but it must reconcile development with transparency, labor standards, environmental safeguards, and genuine public oversight. The Novi Sad disaster demonstrated the political cost of failing to do so.

Foreign Policy Crossroads

Serbia’s geopolitical position adds another layer of complexity. For years, Vučić pursued a multidirectional foreign policy: formally committed to European Union membership while maintaining close ties with Russia and expanding economic cooperation with China. This balancing act allowed Serbia to extract benefits from multiple partners, but it also left the country exposed to competing pressures.

The Student List’s victory is likely to alter Brussels’s calculations. Vučić had been the EU’s principal interlocutor in Serbia for more than a decade, and his ouster exposes the limitations of a policy that prioritized stability and cooperation with a single leader over democratic institutional development. European officials will now need to engage with a more pluralistic, less predictable, but potentially more democratically legitimate Serbian government.

At the same time, the new government will face pressure to clarify its stance toward Russia and China. The student movement arose in part from anger over opaque infrastructure projects involving Chinese state companies, but it has not necessarily campaigned on a wholesale reversal of Serbia’s foreign partnerships. Its immediate priority is domestic reform; its long-term foreign policy will depend on whether it can build a governing program that unites its diverse supporters.

The question of Kosovo remains another unavoidable issue. Any Serbian government must manage relations with Kosovo, negotiations mediated by the EU, and domestic nationalist sensitivities. The Student List’s civic, reform-oriented identity may offer a different tone from Vučić’s confrontational nationalism, but it does not eliminate the underlying difficulty of the dispute.

The End of an Era

Vučić’s defeat ends one of the most durable political dominations in modern Balkan history. For nearly 14 years, he defined Serbia’s political agenda, controlled its governing coalition, and embodied its state power. His departure from executive authority does not erase the SNS’s institutional footprint, but it removes the central figure around whom that system was organized.

The election also demonstrates the vulnerability of nationalist populists when their promise of stability collides with visible governance failure. The Novi Sad canopy collapse did not merely expose a construction defect; it exposed a broader crisis of trust. Citizens asked why public money had been spent, who had approved the work, who had inspected it, and why accountability seemed so difficult to obtain. Those questions became the foundation of a political revolt.

The Student List’s success offers a case study in how civil society can challenge entrenched power. It began without a party, without a conventional leader, and without access to the full machinery of the state. It relied on students, public vigils, blockades, local organization, and a moral demand for accountability. Over time, that moral energy became an electoral coalition.

Yet the movement now enters a more difficult phase. Protest movements often unite around what they oppose; governing requires them to decide what they will build. The Student List must transform a decentralized civic uprising into a functioning administration while preserving the credibility that made it victorious. Its supporters will judge it not only by speeches about freedom and dignity, but by whether institutions change in practice.

A New Serbian Political Language

The most striking feature of the 2026 election may be the language in which it was fought. The Student List did not campaign primarily on ethnicity, religion, or historical grievance. It campaigned on corruption, transparency, accountability, and the rule of law. In a region where political competition has often been framed through nationalist narratives, this represented a significant shift.

The movement’s slogan-like insistence that “corruption kills” connected abstract institutional failures to human consequences. It turned a railway station canopy into evidence of a wider political pathology. It made governance—not identity—the central question of national politics.

That shift may outlast the election itself. Even if the Student List struggles in office, it has demonstrated that Serbian voters can mobilize around democratic accountability and defeat a dominant party system. The 2026 election may therefore be remembered less as the simple replacement of one government by another than as the moment when Serbia’s political imagination expanded.

For now, Serbia stands at the beginning of a new era. The Progressive Party’s long rule has ended, and a movement born in mourning has taken power through the ballot box. Whether this becomes a durable democratic renewal or another episode of political volatility will depend on the choices made in the coming months. But on the night of October 25, 2026, the direction of Serbian politics changed decisively.

As Srdanović told supporters, “tonight Serbia has won.” The challenge before the Student List is to ensure that the victory of that night becomes more than a moment of catharsis—and becomes the foundation of a more accountable, transparent, and democratic Serbian state.
