China’s Rise and the New Economic Era: The Contest for Global Leadership

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · Global Politics · November 23, 2026

The world is entering a new economic era. It is not simply an era in which one country replaces another as the “number one economy.” It is an era in which the center of global production, trade, infrastructure, technology, and geopolitical influence is shifting away from the Atlantic world and toward Asia—above all toward China.

For more than a century, the United States has stood at the center of the global capitalist order. Its financial markets, corporations, universities, military alliances, and cultural influence shaped the modern world. Yet China’s extraordinary transformation over the past four decades has created a new reality. China is now the largest economy in the world when measured by purchasing-power parity, and it has become the indispensable manufacturing hub of the global economy.

This development has profound implications. It challenges the assumption that Western-style capitalism is the only successful model of development. It forces countries across Asia, Africa, Latin America, and the Middle East to reconsider their economic strategies. And it raises one of the most important questions of our time: should economies be governed primarily by private capital and financial markets, or should the state use markets as instruments to achieve national development and social progress?

China’s answer has been clear. It has built a system in which private enterprise, market competition, foreign investment, and global trade are encouraged—but within a framework ultimately directed by the Communist Party and the Chinese state. This is not capitalism in the classical Western sense. It is also not the rigid, centrally planned socialism of the Soviet Union. It is a hybrid system: socialism with Chinese characteristics, state-led market economy, or state capitalism, depending on one’s perspective.

The result has been one of the most remarkable economic transformations in human history.

The scale of China’s transformation

When China began its reform and opening-up policy in 1978, it was a poor, largely agricultural country. Hundreds of millions of people lived in rural villages with limited electricity, poor transportation, weak healthcare, and few opportunities for social mobility. China’s per-capita income was extremely low, and its industrial base was small compared with that of the United States, Japan, and Western Europe.

Today, China is the world’s largest manufacturer, the largest trading nation, and one of the most important centers of infrastructure, technology, and industrial production. It has built high-speed rail networks, ports, highways, airports, power grids, telecommunications systems, and entire industrial cities on a scale that few countries can match.

This transformation did not happen by accident. It resulted from decades of sustained investment in education, infrastructure, manufacturing, science, and technology. It also resulted from a deliberate strategy: China used its enormous labor force, its large domestic market, foreign investment, export-oriented industry, and state coordination to move from low-cost assembly to advanced manufacturing.

China’s industrialization created jobs on a massive scale. Factories absorbed rural workers, cities expanded, wages rose, and a new middle class emerged. The country became the workshop of the world, producing everything from clothing, toys, and household goods to smartphones, electric vehicles, solar panels, industrial machinery, and advanced electronics.

The most important result was the reduction of poverty. China lifted hundreds of millions of people out of extreme poverty in a relatively short historical period. This is one of the greatest anti-poverty achievements ever recorded. It demonstrates that economic growth, when combined with infrastructure, education, employment, and state capacity, can transform the lives of ordinary people.

Of course, China still faces serious problems. Inequality between coastal and inland regions remains significant. Urban-rural divides persist. Many workers face long hours, insecure employment, and high living costs. Environmental damage from decades of rapid industrialization has been severe. An aging population, a weakening property sector, youth unemployment, and slowing growth also present major challenges.

But these problems do not erase the central achievement. China transformed itself from a poor agrarian society into an industrial superpower within one generation. That achievement has changed the global balance of power.

The meaning of economic power

People often ask whether China has “overtaken” the United States. The answer depends on how economic power is measured.

In nominal GDP—economic output measured in current U.S. dollars—the United States remains larger. Its economy benefits from the global role of the U.S. dollar, powerful financial markets, major technology firms, and high levels of consumption.

But when measured by purchasing-power parity, which accounts for differences in the cost of goods and services between countries, China is already the larger economy. This is because a dollar buys much more in China than it does in the United States. A worker in China may earn less in dollars, but housing, food, transportation, healthcare, and manufactured goods can be substantially cheaper.

Purchasing-power parity is especially important when assessing real productive capacity. It measures what an economy can actually produce, build, and consume in domestic terms. By this measure, China’s economy is not merely catching up with the United States; it has already surpassed it.

This does not mean that the average Chinese citizen is richer than the average American. China has a much larger population, so its total economic output is spread across more people. The United States still has a higher GDP per capita and higher average living standards. It also retains advantages in finance, higher education, advanced research, military power, and global cultural influence.

But the overall direction is clear. China’s economic weight is growing, while the relative share of the United States and other Western economies in global production is declining. The world is no longer organized around a single economic center.

China’s model: markets under state leadership

China’s economic system is often misunderstood. Some people describe it as communist, others as capitalist, and others as authoritarian capitalism. These labels are partly useful but incomplete.

China is not a free-market economy in the Western sense. Private companies do not fully control the direction of the economy. Financial markets do not have the same independence as those in the United States or Europe. Large private firms operate under political constraints, and the state can intervene in major industries when national interests are at stake.

At the same time, China is not a centrally planned economy in the old Soviet style. Most prices are determined by markets. Private businesses employ hundreds of millions of workers. Entrepreneurs, investors, exporters, and consumers play a major role in economic life. Competition is intense in many industries, and Chinese companies have become some of the most innovative and competitive firms in the world.

The distinctive feature of China’s system is that the state remains in command of the overall direction of development. The Communist Party sets long-term goals. The government builds infrastructure, directs credit toward strategic industries, supports research and development, manages foreign investment, and coordinates industrial policy.

In China, the government guides capitalists. In much of the West, capitalists often influence, fund, and shape governments.

This difference is fundamental.

In the United States and many Western countries, large corporations, banks, investment firms, and wealthy individuals have enormous influence over public policy. They finance political campaigns, employ lobbyists, own media platforms, fund think tanks, and shape public debate. Governments often depend on private capital for investment, employment, and growth. As a result, economic policy can become oriented toward the interests of financial markets, shareholders, and corporate executives.

This does not mean that Western governments have no power. They regulate businesses, collect taxes, provide public services, and respond to crises. But the balance of power is different. In many Western economies, capital has gained enormous influence over the state. In China, the state has retained greater control over capital.

China’s model allows private enterprise to flourish, but it does not allow private capital to become the ultimate ruler of society. The state can discipline companies, redirect investment, restrict speculative activity, and prioritize national objectives such as industrial development, technological self-reliance, poverty reduction, and infrastructure.

This is why China can build high-speed rail networks, expand renewable energy, develop semiconductor industries, construct entire industrial zones, and pursue long-term technological goals with remarkable speed. The state can mobilize resources toward objectives that private markets alone might neglect because they are too costly, too risky, or too slow to produce immediate profit.

The strength of state capacity

One of China’s greatest advantages is state capacity. The Chinese government can plan over decades, not merely over election cycles. It can commit resources to projects whose benefits may not appear for years. It can coordinate central government, provincial governments, banks, state-owned enterprises, private firms, universities, and local authorities around national priorities.

This capacity has produced extraordinary results. China built the world’s largest high-speed rail network. It expanded electricity generation, ports, roads, bridges, telecommunications, and urban infrastructure at a pace unmatched by most countries. It invested heavily in manufacturing supply chains, creating clusters of factories, suppliers, engineers, logistics firms, and skilled workers.

State capacity also enabled China to respond quickly to crises. During the global financial crisis of 2008, China launched a massive stimulus program that supported construction, employment, and industrial production. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the state mobilized resources for public health, production, and economic stabilization.

This does not mean that Chinese planning is always efficient or wise. State-led investment can produce waste, corruption, debt, overcapacity, and poorly chosen projects. Local governments sometimes pursue growth targets in ways that create financial risks. The property sector has shown how state-led credit expansion can create dangerous imbalances.

But the broader lesson remains important. A country needs more than markets. It needs a capable state that can build infrastructure, educate its people, protect strategic industries, coordinate investment, and pursue long-term goals.

China’s rise shows that development is not simply the result of letting markets operate freely. It is also the result of building institutions capable of organizing a society toward common national objectives.

The weaknesses of Western capitalism

Western capitalism has produced enormous wealth, technological innovation, and high living standards. It would be wrong to deny these achievements. The United States, Europe, Japan, South Korea, Australia, and Canada have built prosperous societies with strong industries, universities, scientific institutions, and consumer markets.

But Western capitalism also has deep contradictions.

One of the most serious is the growing power of finance. In many Western economies, financial markets have become more important than productive industry. Companies are pressured to maximize short-term profits, cut costs, outsource production, and return cash to shareholders. Factories close. Jobs move overseas. Wages stagnate. Communities that once depended on manufacturing suffer decline.

Another problem is inequality. Wealth has become increasingly concentrated in the hands of a small number of individuals, families, and corporations. The owners of capital benefit from rising asset prices, while workers may struggle with stagnant wages, expensive housing, debt, and insecure employment.

A third problem is political capture. When corporations and wealthy individuals have disproportionate influence over politics, governments may prioritize tax cuts, deregulation, financial interests, and corporate profits over public welfare. This can weaken democracy and make it harder to address problems such as climate change, inequality, healthcare, housing, and industrial decline.

The 2008 global financial crisis exposed these weaknesses. Banks and financial institutions took excessive risks, and when the system collapsed, governments used public money to rescue them. Ordinary workers lost jobs and homes, while many large financial institutions were protected. This created widespread anger and distrust toward political and economic elites.

Since then, many Western countries have struggled to rebuild industrial strength. Globalization brought cheap consumer goods, but it also shifted manufacturing to Asia. The United States and Europe became dependent on Chinese supply chains for electronics, pharmaceuticals, rare-earth materials, batteries, solar panels, and many other essential products.

The West is not collapsing, but it is facing a crisis of direction. It remains wealthy, innovative, and powerful, yet it is increasingly divided, unequal, and uncertain about its future.

China’s alternative

China offers a different answer.

Rather than allowing capital to dominate the state, China seeks to make capital serve the state’s development goals. Private companies are encouraged to grow, innovate, employ workers, and compete globally. But they must operate within national priorities.

This does not mean that Chinese businesses are free from problems. Corruption, monopolies, debt, speculative real-estate development, and unequal opportunity remain serious concerns. The government itself has acknowledged the need to address these issues.

But China’s system gives the state tools that many Western governments lack. It can direct investment toward strategic sectors. It can prevent certain private interests from blocking national projects. It can use public banks, state-owned enterprises, and industrial policy to build capacity in areas considered essential for the country’s future.

For example, China invested heavily in renewable energy, electric vehicles, batteries, solar panels, and high-speed rail before these industries became fully profitable. The state saw them as strategic industries, not merely as short-term business opportunities. As a result, Chinese firms now dominate several global clean-energy markets.

China also invested in digital infrastructure, artificial intelligence, telecommunications, and advanced manufacturing. These investments were guided by a long-term vision: China wanted to reduce dependence on foreign technology and become a leader in the industries of the future.

This approach has risks. State direction can lead to inefficiency if officials choose the wrong priorities. It can also create tension with private entrepreneurs who want greater freedom. But it has allowed China to build industrial strength at extraordinary speed.

The global significance

China’s rise is not only important for China. It changes the options available to the rest of the world.

For decades, developing countries were told that the path to prosperity required privatization, financial liberalization, free trade, and minimal state intervention. This advice was often promoted by Western governments, international financial institutions, and multinational corporations.

Some countries benefited from elements of this approach. But many others experienced instability, debt crises, deindustrialization, and dependence on foreign capital. They opened their markets but did not build strong industries. They attracted investment but remained trapped in low-value production. They followed Western prescriptions but did not achieve Western living standards.

China’s experience challenges that model. China did not simply open its economy and wait for markets to solve every problem. It used state power to protect and develop strategic industries. It controlled capital flows. It invested massively in infrastructure. It built industrial capacity before fully liberalizing its financial system. It used trade and foreign investment strategically, rather than allowing foreign corporations to dominate every sector.

This does not mean every country should copy China exactly. China’s model depends on a huge population, a strong central state, a long history of state administration, and unique historical conditions. Other countries may need different approaches.

But China’s success proves that there is more than one road to development. A country can use markets without surrendering to markets. It can welcome private enterprise without allowing private capital to control the state. It can participate in global trade without becoming economically dependent on foreign powers.

The coming multipolar world

The new economic era will not be defined by one country alone. It will be multipolar.

The United States will remain a major power. It has the world’s largest nominal economy, the most influential financial markets, leading technology firms, powerful universities, and a global network of alliances. It will continue to shape global affairs for decades.

Europe will remain important, especially in science, manufacturing, environmental policy, and international institutions. Japan, South Korea, India, Southeast Asia, the Gulf states, Brazil, and other emerging economies will also play larger roles.

But China will be at the center of this new order. Its manufacturing capacity, domestic market, infrastructure, technological ambitions, and global trade links give it a unique position.

The world is moving away from a system in which the United States alone sets the economic rules. It is moving toward a system in which several centers of power compete, cooperate, and negotiate.

This transition will not be peaceful or simple. The United States and China are competing over technology, trade, military influence, semiconductors, artificial intelligence, energy, and control of global supply chains. Countries in Southeast Asia, Africa, Latin America, and the Middle East will face pressure to choose sides, although many will seek to maintain relations with both powers.

The competition between the United States and China is not merely a rivalry between two governments. It is a contest between two models of economic organization.

One model places private capital and financial markets at the center of economic life. The other places the state at the center, using markets and private enterprise as tools for national development.

The central lesson

China’s rise teaches an important lesson: markets are powerful, but they are not enough.

Markets can allocate resources efficiently when they function well. They can encourage innovation, reward effort, and connect producers with consumers. But markets cannot by themselves build railways, educate an entire population, protect strategic industries, reduce inequality, or plan a nation’s long-term future.

A successful economy needs both markets and a capable state.

China’s achievement came from combining these forces. It allowed private businesses to grow. It welcomed foreign investment. It encouraged competition and entrepreneurship. But it also built infrastructure, supported industry, expanded education, and directed national resources toward strategic goals.

The West, by contrast, has often allowed capital to become too powerful. Corporations influence governments. Financial markets shape policy. Wealth concentrates at the top. Industrial capacity moves abroad. Public priorities are subordinated to private profit.

This is not an argument that every Western institution is failing. The United States and Europe still possess enormous strengths. But it is an argument that the Western model is no longer the unquestioned model of the future.

China has shown that a developing country can rise without adopting Western political and economic institutions in full. It has shown that a state can guide markets rather than be guided by them. And it has shown that economic development can be pursued as a national project, not merely as the accumulation of private wealth.

A new era begins

We are entering a new era because the old assumptions no longer hold.

The assumption that the United States will always dominate the global economy is weakening. The assumption that Western capitalism is the only successful path to development is being questioned. The assumption that markets alone can solve society’s problems is increasingly challenged by inequality, climate change, financial instability, and industrial decline.

China’s rise does not guarantee that it will surpass the United States in every field. It still faces major obstacles: demographic decline, debt, technological restrictions, geopolitical conflict, environmental pressures, and the difficult transition from rapid growth to high-income development.

But China has already changed the world.

It has become the largest economy by purchasing-power parity. It has built the industrial base of global trade. It has lifted hundreds of millions from poverty. It has created a new model of development that combines markets, private enterprise, and state leadership.

The twenty-first century will be shaped by whether countries can learn from this transformation. They must decide whether to allow capital to rule society or whether to build states strong enough to direct capital toward the common good.

China’s rise signals that the age of unquestioned Western economic dominance is ending. A new era has begun—one defined not by the victory of one ideology, but by the struggle to build economies that can create wealth, reduce poverty, develop technology, and serve the long-term interests of ordinary people.
