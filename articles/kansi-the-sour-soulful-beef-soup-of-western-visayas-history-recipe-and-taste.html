Kansi: The Sour, Soulful Beef Soup of Western Visayas — History, Recipe, and Taste

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · Culinary · November 11, 2026

Kansi is Iloilo's sour, soulful beef soup — rich, tangy, and deeply comforting.

Written by Glenn Junsay Pansensoy  ·  Culinary  ·  18 min read

A rich, sour beef soup from Western Visayas — and the layered history behind its orange broth.

Kansi, also spelled cansi, is one of the most recognizable dishes of Western Visayas. It is a rich, sour beef soup associated especially with Iloilo and Bacolod, made with beef shank, bone marrow, the sour batuan fruit, lemongrass, annatto, and often unripe jackfruit. Its deep orange broth, tender beef, and tangy flavor have made it a favorite comfort food across the Philippines.

Although kansi is often described as an Ilonggo dish, its story is more complicated than a simple claim of local invention. Spanish colonialism did not create kansi in the sense that Spanish colonizers designed the recipe or introduced its central ingredients. The dish’s most important flavors come from local Visayan ingredients and cooking traditions, particularly batuan, beef shank, bone marrow, and lemongrass. However, Spanish colonial rule and the Manila–Acapulco galleon trade helped shape the modern form of kansi by introducing annatto, or atsuete, into Philippine cooking.

In other words, Spanish colonialism was not the sole cause of kansi’s creation. It was one important historical influence among many. Kansi is best understood as a local Visayan dish that developed within a colonial society and absorbed ingredients and influences from wider trade networks. Its identity comes from the meeting of indigenous Visayan food traditions, Spanish-era trade, and the creativity of Ilonggo and Negrense cooks.

Understanding Kansi

Kansi is a hearty beef soup known for its combination of richness and sourness. The base is usually made from beef shank, a cut that contains meat, tendon, connective tissue, and bone. When simmered slowly, these parts release collagen and marrow into the broth, creating a soup that is thick, savory, and deeply satisfying.

The sourness of kansi traditionally comes from batuan, also called batwan. This small green fruit is native to parts of Western Visayas, especially Panay and Negros. Its sharp, fruity acidity is difficult to replace. While tamarind, kamias, calamansi, and commercial sinigang mixes can make a soup sour, they do not fully reproduce the distinctive taste that batuan gives to kansi.

Lemongrass, known in Filipino kitchens as tanglad, adds a clean citrus fragrance to the broth. Ginger, onion, and garlic provide a warm aromatic foundation. Chili peppers contribute mild heat and fragrance, while annatto gives the soup its recognizable orange color. Unripe jackfruit, or langka, is often added as well, giving the dish more texture and making it more filling.

Kansi is commonly served with steamed white rice. Many people pour the broth over their rice or eat bites of beef alongside spoonfuls of rice. Fish sauce, calamansi, and chili may be placed on the table so each person can adjust the flavor to their preference.

The dish is especially comforting during cool or rainy weather, but it is enjoyed throughout the year. Because it is rich and filling, kansi is often treated as a complete meal rather than a light appetizer. A single pot can feed a family, particularly when served with plenty of rice.

The Indigenous Foundations of Kansi

The core identity of kansi is rooted in local Visayan food culture. Its most distinctive ingredient, batuan, is not a Spanish import. It is a sour fruit associated with the Visayas, particularly the islands of Panay and Negros. Its use in local cooking reflects the region’s access to native and locally grown ingredients.

Batuan provides the acidity that balances the richness of beef and marrow. Without it, kansi would lose much of what makes it unique. The fruit’s tartness cuts through the fattiness of the broth and prevents the dish from becoming overly heavy. This balance between rich meat and bright sourness is one of the defining characteristics of Visayan cooking.

Beef shank and bone marrow are equally important. Beef shank contains tough muscle fibers, connective tissue, and bone, all of which benefit from long, slow cooking. As the meat simmers, its collagen breaks down and gives the broth body. The marrow adds richness and a savory depth that makes the soup feel more substantial than an ordinary clear broth.

This cooking method is practical and rooted in local food traditions. Tough cuts of meat were often used because they were more affordable and could be transformed into tender, flavorful meals through patient simmering. The bones were not discarded; they became part of the meal, adding flavor to the broth and marrow to be enjoyed directly from the bone.

Lemongrass, ginger, garlic, onion, and chili are also familiar ingredients in Filipino and Southeast Asian cooking. They reflect a broader regional preference for aromatic broths, balanced flavors, and dishes that combine savory richness with acidity and heat. Kansi fits naturally into this culinary tradition.

Unripe jackfruit adds another layer to the dish. When young and green, jackfruit has a firm, fibrous texture that becomes tender as it cooks. It absorbs the flavors of the broth and gives the soup more body. In many households, jackfruit also served a practical purpose: it stretched the dish and made it more filling without requiring more meat.

These elements show that kansi was not simply imported from Spain. Its central ingredients and cooking logic are local. The dish reflects the resources, tastes, and household practices of Western Visayas.

Spanish Colonialism in the Philippines

Spain’s colonial presence in the Philippines began in the sixteenth century and lasted until 1898. During this period, the Philippines became part of a vast Spanish imperial network that connected Europe, the Americas, and Asia. Manila became an important trading hub, linking Spanish territories in the Americas with Asia through the Manila–Acapulco galleon trade.

This trade route brought more than silver, textiles, and religious influence. It also moved plants, foods, animals, and culinary practices across the Pacific. Ingredients from the Americas entered the Philippines, while Philippine products and food traditions were also carried outward through Spanish networks.

Spanish colonialism therefore changed Philippine cuisine in important ways. It introduced new ingredients, encouraged new cooking techniques, and connected local cooks to global trade. However, it did not erase existing food traditions. Instead, Filipino communities often adapted foreign ingredients to local tastes and incorporated them into dishes that remained distinctly Filipino.

Kansi is an example of this process. Spanish influence affected one part of the dish, but it did not determine the dish’s entire character. The soup’s sourness, beefiness, and regional identity remained rooted in Visayan food culture.

Annatto and the Spanish Connection

The clearest Spanish connection to kansi is annatto, known in the Philippines as atsuete or achuete. Annatto comes from the seeds of the achiote tree, Bixa orellana, a plant native to tropical America. The name “achuete” comes from a Nahuatl word, reflecting the ingredient’s Indigenous American origins.

Spanish colonial trade brought annatto to the Philippines during the colonial period. It became known as atsuete in Filipino kitchens and gradually became a common coloring and flavoring ingredient in many regional dishes. By the seventeenth century, annatto was already being used in Philippine cooking.

In kansi, annatto gives the broth its characteristic orange or golden-orange color. This visual quality is one of the first things many people notice about the dish. A bowl of kansi without annatto may still be delicious, but it would look very different from the familiar version served in Ilonggo homes and restaurants.

Annatto also contributes a subtle earthy flavor. It does not dominate the dish, but it adds warmth and depth to the broth. In combination with lemongrass, ginger, garlic, and chili, it helps create the layered flavor profile that makes kansi distinctive.

This makes annatto an important Spanish-era contribution to kansi. However, it is important not to exaggerate its role. Annatto gives the soup color and a mild flavor note, but it is not the ingredient that defines kansi’s taste. Batuan remains the dish’s most important souring agent, and beef shank remains its culinary foundation.

How Spanish Ingredients Were Adapted

The arrival of annatto in the Philippines illustrates how foreign ingredients can become part of local food traditions. Spanish traders and colonial networks introduced the ingredient, but it was Filipino cooks who found ways to use it in local dishes.

Rather than replacing indigenous ingredients, annatto was incorporated into existing cooking practices. In kansi, it was combined with batuan, beef, lemongrass, and jackfruit. The result was not a Spanish soup adapted for Filipino tastes. It was a Visayan soup that used a Spanish-introduced ingredient as one element among many.

This process is often called indigenization. A foreign ingredient enters a local food culture, but local cooks reshape its use according to their own preferences and available resources. Annatto became Filipino not because it was Spanish, but because Filipino cooks made it part of their own culinary vocabulary.

The same pattern can be seen in other Filipino dishes. Annatto is used in Ilonggo adobo sa pula, Ilonggo lauya, Bacolod-style chicken inasal, and many other regional specialties. In each case, the ingredient became part of a local dish rather than simply remaining a foreign addition.

Kansi therefore reflects a broader truth about Filipino cuisine. It is not the product of one culture alone. It developed through centuries of local innovation, migration, trade, colonization, and adaptation. Spanish influence is part of that story, but it is not the whole story.

The Role of Beef and Colonial Food Systems

Beef itself also has a complicated history in the Philippines. Cattle were not originally native to the islands in the same form they are known today. Spanish colonial rule contributed to the expansion of cattle raising and beef consumption in parts of the archipelago. Over time, beef became more widely available in certain regions and social settings.

This does not mean that Filipinos did not eat meat before Spanish colonization. They did. However, Spanish colonial administration, trade, and agricultural systems influenced which animals were raised, how livestock moved through markets, and how certain meats became more accessible in towns and cities.

In Western Visayas, beef became an important ingredient in dishes such as kansi. The use of beef shank and bone marrow reflects both local cooking preferences and the availability of cattle in the region. The dish turns a relatively tough cut of meat into something tender and flavorful through slow cooking.

This does not make kansi a Spanish dish. It means that Spanish colonialism influenced the food environment in which the dish developed. Local cooks used the ingredients available to them, including beef, and transformed them according to Visayan tastes.

The use of bone marrow is especially important. Rather than treating bones as waste, kansi makes them central to the dish. The marrow enriches the broth and becomes part of the eating experience. This practical, resourceful approach to food reflects a broader Filipino tradition of using the whole animal and making the most of available ingredients.

Lemongrass and Local Aromatics

Lemongrass is another essential element of kansi. Known locally as tanglad, it gives the broth a fresh, citrus-like fragrance that balances the richness of the beef. Its presence makes the soup feel lighter than its ingredients might suggest.

Lemongrass is widely used across Southeast Asia, and its role in kansi reflects regional culinary connections. It is not a Spanish ingredient. Its use in kansi points instead to the shared aromatic traditions of Southeast Asian cooking, where herbs, roots, and fragrant plants are used to build flavor in broths and stews.

Ginger, garlic, and onion also play important roles. These ingredients create the savory base of the soup. They do not overpower the batuan or beef, but they give the broth complexity and warmth.

Chili peppers add heat and fragrance. In many versions of kansi, finger chilies are used because they provide flavor without excessive spiciness. The amount of chili can vary from household to household. Some cooks prefer a mild version, while others add more chilies for a stronger kick.

These ingredients show how kansi is built through balance. The beef provides richness, batuan provides sourness, lemongrass provides fragrance, annatto provides color, and chili provides heat. No single ingredient defines the dish on its own. Its appeal comes from the way all these elements work together.

Jackfruit and Household Cooking

Unripe jackfruit is a traditional ingredient in many versions of kansi. When young and green, jackfruit has a firm texture that becomes tender during cooking. It absorbs the sour, savory broth and adds a satisfying bite to the dish.

Jackfruit also has a practical role. It makes the soup more filling and allows cooks to feed more people without relying entirely on meat. This made it valuable in household cooking, where stretching ingredients was often necessary.

Not every version of kansi includes jackfruit. Some cooks prefer a simpler soup with only beef, batuan, lemongrass, and annatto. Others consider jackfruit essential. This variation reflects the reality that kansi is a family dish, not a standardized restaurant product.

Different families and communities developed their own versions based on preference, availability, and tradition. Some versions use kamansi, a smaller fruit related to jackfruit, instead of young jackfruit. Others may use different levels of sourness, spice, or richness.

This flexibility is part of what has allowed kansi to endure. It is not locked into one exact recipe. Its essential identity remains recognizable, but its details can change from kitchen to kitchen.

Iloilo, Bacolod, and Regional Identity

Kansi is closely associated with Iloilo and Bacolod, two major food destinations in Western Visayas. Iloilo, located on Panay Island, has long been known for its distinctive food culture, including dishes such as batchoy, molo, and various seafood preparations. Bacolod, on Negros Island, is equally known for its rich culinary traditions.

The dish is often described as Ilonggo, but its connection to Negros is also important. Some food writers and cooks argue that cansi is originally a Negrense dish, while others emphasize its strong association with Ilonggo cuisine. This debate reflects the close cultural and culinary ties between Panay and Negros.

The two islands share languages, trade routes, family connections, and food traditions. Ingredients such as batuan are used across parts of the region, and cooking practices often overlap. Rather than treating kansi as belonging exclusively to one place, it is more accurate to see it as a Western Visayan dish with strong roots in both Iloilo and Negros.

Over time, kansi moved from home kitchens into restaurants. Establishments in Iloilo and Bacolod helped popularize the dish and introduced it to a wider audience. As Filipino cuisine gained more national attention, kansi began appearing in restaurants outside Western Visayas, including in Metro Manila.

This spread did not erase the dish’s regional identity. Instead, it made kansi a symbol of Ilonggo and Negrense food culture. For many people, a bowl of kansi immediately evokes Western Visayas, even if it is eaten far from Iloilo or Bacolod.

Why Kansi Is Often Compared to Bulalo and Sinigang

Kansi is often compared to bulalo and sinigang because it shares features with both dishes. Like bulalo, it uses beef shank and bone marrow, creating a rich, gelatinous broth. Like sinigang, it is sour and refreshing, with a broth that balances the richness of meat.

However, kansi is not simply a combination of the two. Its flavor is more complex because of batuan, lemongrass, and annatto. It has the heartiness of bulalo, the tang of sinigang, and an aromatic character that sets it apart from both.

Bulalo is usually associated with clear beef broth and bone marrow. Sinigang is a broader category of sour Filipino soups that can use pork, shrimp, fish, or beef. Kansi sits at the intersection of these traditions but remains distinct because of its regional ingredients and preparation.

The comparison is useful because it helps people understand the dish quickly. However, it can also obscure kansi’s unique identity. Kansi is not merely “sinigang na bulalo.” It is a specific Western Visayan dish with its own history, ingredients, and cultural meaning.

The Importance of Batuan

Batuan deserves special attention because it is the ingredient most closely tied to kansi’s identity. Its sourness is not generic. It has a fruity, sharp quality that gives the broth brightness without making it taste overly acidic.

In Western Visayas, batuan is used in several dishes beyond kansi. It appears in KBL, an Ilonggo dish made with pigeon peas, pork, and jackfruit. It is also used in pinalmahan, an Ilonggo-style paksiw. Its presence in kansi connects the soup to a wider tradition of sour, ingredient-driven Visayan cooking.

Because fresh batuan can be difficult to find outside Western Visayas, cooks in other regions often substitute kamias, tamarind, or commercial sinigang mix. These substitutes can produce a delicious soup, but they cannot fully recreate the original flavor.

This is why many Ilonggo and Negrense cooks consider batuan essential. It is not just a souring agent. It is the ingredient that gives kansi its regional character and distinguishes it from similar dishes elsewhere in the Philippines.

How Kansi Is Traditionally Cooked

Traditional kansi begins with beef shank. The meat is cleaned and sometimes seared before being simmered in water. Searing helps develop a deeper flavor, although some home cooks skip this step and simply boil the meat directly.

As the broth begins to simmer, foam and impurities rise to the surface. These are skimmed off to produce a clearer broth. Onion, garlic, and ginger may be added early in the process to build the savory base.

The beef then simmers slowly for an hour or more, sometimes closer to two hours. This long cooking time allows the tough fibers and connective tissue in the shank to soften. It also allows the marrow to melt into the broth, enriching the soup naturally.

Annatto is often prepared separately by steeping the seeds in hot broth or water. The resulting liquid is then added to the pot. This prevents clumping and helps distribute the color evenly throughout the soup.

Batuan is added to provide sourness. Some cooks crush or mash the fruit before adding it, allowing its juices and flavor to release into the broth. Lemongrass, jackfruit, and chilies are usually added later, depending on how long they need to cook.

The final dish is seasoned with salt and pepper. Some cooks add fish sauce or other seasonings, though many prefer to keep the seasoning simple so the natural flavors of the ingredients remain clear.

Serving and Eating Kansi

Kansi is best served hot. It is commonly paired with steamed white rice, which balances the soup’s acidity and richness. The rice absorbs the broth and makes the meal more filling.

Fish sauce is a common condiment. A small dish of patis may be placed on the table, allowing each person to add saltiness according to preference. Calamansi and chili may also be served alongside the soup.

Eating kansi is often a shared experience. The pot is placed in the center of the table, and everyone serves themselves. Diners may pick tender pieces of beef, scoop broth over rice, or enjoy the marrow directly from the bone.

The dish encourages slow eating. Because the beef has been simmered for hours, it is tender enough to pull apart easily. The broth is rich enough to be savored, but the batuan keeps it bright and refreshing.

This makes kansi more than just food. It is a social dish, often associated with family meals, gatherings, and shared tables. Its generosity reflects the values of hospitality and communal eating that are important in Filipino culture.

Modern Adaptations

As kansi spread beyond Western Visayas, cooks began adapting it to different ingredients and settings. Fresh batuan is not always available, especially outside Panay and Negros. Kamias, tamarind, and commercial souring mixes became common substitutes.

Canned green jackfruit is another practical alternative. It is easier to find in many places, particularly among Filipino communities overseas. While fresh jackfruit may offer a better texture, canned jackfruit allows more people to prepare the dish at home.

Restaurants have also experimented with the format. Some serve kansi in traditional clay pots, while others present it in more modern ways. Sizzling kansi, for example, reimagines the dish in a different presentation while retaining its familiar flavors.

These adaptations show that kansi is a living dish. It continues to evolve as it travels beyond its original home. However, its connection to batuan, beef shank, and Western Visayan cooking remains central to its identity.

Why Spanish Colonialism Matters

Spanish colonialism matters to kansi because it helped introduce annatto to the Philippines. Through colonial trade networks, annatto traveled from the Americas to the islands and became part of Filipino cooking. In kansi, it gives the broth its distinctive color and a subtle earthy flavor.

Spanish colonialism also influenced the broader food system in which kansi developed. It affected trade, agriculture, livestock raising, and the movement of ingredients across regions. These changes helped create the conditions in which dishes like kansi could develop and spread.

However, it would be inaccurate to say that Spanish colonialism created kansi. The dish’s central flavors and cooking traditions are local. Batuan, beef shank, bone marrow, lemongrass, and jackfruit reflect Visayan food culture, not Spanish cuisine.

The most accurate explanation is that kansi is a product of interaction. Local Visayan traditions provided the foundation. Spanish colonial trade introduced annatto and influenced the availability of certain ingredients. Ilonggo and Negrense cooks combined these elements into a dish that became distinctly their own.

Kansi as Cultural Heritage

Kansi is more than a recipe. It is a piece of cultural heritage. It reflects the geography of Western Visayas, the availability of local ingredients, and the creativity of the people who cook there.

The dish also reflects the resilience of Filipino food traditions. Even under colonial rule, local communities continued to cook according to their own tastes and resources. They absorbed foreign ingredients when useful, but they did not simply copy foreign dishes. Instead, they transformed those ingredients into something uniquely Filipino.

Kansi’s journey from Ilonggo homes to restaurants across the Philippines shows how regional dishes can gain wider recognition. It began as a local comfort food and became a symbol of Western Visayan identity. Today, it is enjoyed by people who may never have visited Iloilo or Bacolod.

For Filipinos living abroad, cooking kansi can be a way to reconnect with home. The smell of lemongrass, the color of annatto, and the taste of batuan can evoke memories of family meals and regional roots.

A Dish of Many Histories

Kansi is a dish of many histories. It carries the flavors of Western Visayas, the practical wisdom of household cooking, and the influence of global trade. It is local in its core identity but connected to wider historical forces through ingredients such as annatto and beef.

Spanish colonialism is part of that story, but it is not the whole story. Kansi was not born from Spanish kitchens or Spanish recipes. It emerged from Visayan food culture and was shaped over time by local cooks, families, and communities.

Its most important lesson is that Filipino cuisine cannot be reduced to a single influence. It is layered, adaptive, and deeply regional. Kansi shows how a dish can be both rooted in place and open to change.

When someone eats a bowl of kansi, they are not simply tasting beef and sour broth. They are tasting the history of Western Visayas: its local ingredients, its family traditions, its trade connections, and its ability to turn simple ingredients into something memorable.

That is why kansi endures. It is comforting, flavorful, and deeply tied to place. It is a dish that reminds people where they come from, even when they are far from home.
