Europe’s October Riots: How Economic Anger, Islamism and the Far Right Are Colliding

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · Global Politics · October 24, 2026

Europe’s October riots began over schools, rents and wages—but they did not end there.

The Street and the File: Europe’s October Unrest and the Ideology It Refuses to Name

By Poetic Codes  |  19 October 2026  |  Politics & Society

On 17 October 2026, between roughly 550,000 and 800,000 people marched through France. By nightfall, the demonstrations had fractured. In Paris, Lyon, Marseille, Limoges and Bordeaux, masked groups clashed with police, set vehicles on fire and looted shops. Interior Ministry figures put arrests somewhere between about 1,800 and 2,400, depending on the count; hundreds of officers were hurt, with some estimates approaching a thousand.

That same weekend, tear gas filled Milan’s Piazza del Duomo. Belgium saw violence, while Tirana endured days of unrest. Spain and Portugal remained largely within the bounds of housing protests.

None of this amounted to a terrorist campaign. Treating it as one distorts Europe’s security debate—and allows the more difficult argument to be dismissed before it is even made.

Where the anger began

The autumn protests began as a rebellion against the state. In mid-September, teachers and students in poorer districts on Paris’s edge walked out over empty classrooms, leaking buildings and schools that had effectively stopped functioning.

The grievance spread: the cost of living in France and Italy, rents in Madrid and Lisbon, corruption and a disputed coastal development in Albania. Young people were at the center because they have gained least from the post-2008, post-pandemic order.

A secular, class-based politics would have kept the focus there. Parts of the street did not.

Two stories over one fire

Within days, two irreconcilable explanations competed for the same images.

One came from Washington and Europe’s nationalist right. Donald Trump described the unrest as “nothing less than out of control mass migration” and claimed it was “about Islam wanting to take over a once great country.” Marine Le Pen connected the looting to “decades of immigration” and a state that had “lost control.” AfD leader Alice Weidel warned of a “dangerous mix of mass migration and leftwing extremism.” Éric Zemmour called the riots the start of an “ethnic war,” treating a Toulouse image—a Joan of Arc statue dressed in a yellow vest resembling a hijab, with a Palestinian flag placed in her hand—as evidence that France had already been Islamized. Hungarian and Polish leaders used Paris to justify border policies they had long defended.

The other account, offered by French authorities, wire services and an Alliance4Europe data study, was more mundane—and more uncomfortable for that narrative. The placards demanded better schools, budgets and prices. French police did not identify immigrants as the organizers of the violence. In French-language social media, only a small fraction of posts blamed migrants or Islam; that share was several times higher in English. Claims that immigrants had burned Limoges town hall spread first and loudest outside France.

Most demonstrators were not jihadists. Black-bloc militants, anarchist networks and, in Italy, occupied social centers and pro-Palestinian collectives moved to the front of marches whose original organizers sometimes objected.

Both explanations omit something. The nationalist version turns a revolt over wages and schools into a migration conspiracy. That is why it travels so easily abroad, but lands poorly among people who were actually marching. The liberal version correctly identifies the economic trigger, then stops—as though flags, slogans and organized political currents were mere decoration.

They are not decoration. They are how a protest gets captured.

The left’s intellectual retreat

This is where the European left’s intellectual decline becomes relevant.

Marxism, whatever its flaws, was materialist and secular. It saw religion as both the “sigh of the oppressed” and a tool of oppression—not as a community beyond criticism. It presumed a single working class divided by wages and ownership, not a hierarchy of protected identities. It had no theoretical space for a politics whose primary loyalty is the ummah, whose authority is divine law, and whose historical record includes the destruction of secular left movements: the Algerian civil war, the crushing of the Iranian left after 1979, the marginalization of Palestinian Marxists by Islamist movements, and the murder of trade unionists and teachers by jihadists from the Sahel to Mosul.

What replaced that outlook across much of Western Europe was not conversion to Islam. It was substitution. As factories and parties weakened, “the oppressed” became a moral identity rather than a social class. Anti-imperialism, then anti-racism, supplied the narrative: the West is the permanent aggressor, and anyone resisting it becomes, in practice, an ally.

Political Islam entered through that opening. It brought numbers, intensity and a ready-made story of humiliation. In exchange, parts of the left agreed to treat blasphemy as violence, criticism of Muslim Brotherhood-linked networks as colonial nostalgia, and the sexual politics of conservative religious movements as off-limits—while still claiming the inheritance of a tradition that despised clerical power.

French polemicists called this islamo-gauchisme. The term is often wielded as a weapon. But the phenomenon is visible without it.

It is the teacher who freely dissects the Church yet falls silent before religious pressure in the same school. It is the party that explains wages in the morning, then marches in the afternoon behind slogans denying Israel’s right to exist or treating the methods of October 7 as “resistance.” It is the student union whose education strike acquires—without any clear vote—a foreign-policy agenda and a set of flags.

The October protests did not create this habit. They displayed it. In Italy, pro-Palestinian networks and left collectives tried to steer the marches. In France, Palestinian flags appeared beside burning street furniture, and at least one reported placard was openly antisemitic. A researcher writing in a Danish newspaper could look at the same crowds and worry, bluntly, about a future religious voting bloc. A Times columnist called it a red-green alliance.

One need not accept either conclusion in full to recognize the coalition.

The trap of the alliance

An older Marxism would have acknowledged the economic demands as real while naming the religious alliance as a trap.

The trap is not that Muslims themselves are enemies of workers. It is that Islamism is a right-wing political project—hierarchical, anti-feminist, anti-secular and anti-communist—dressed as the newest liberation movement. A left that embraces it does not gain a constituency. It loses the language through which it could ever again discuss class without first seeking a cleric’s approval.

Two emergencies, one blindness

The danger this autumn is not that a Tunisian warehouse worker in Lyon will become a terrorist because a bus stop burned.

The danger is that Europe now faces two emergencies on separate tracks, while its politics can only perceive one at a time.

The first is visible on the placards: schools that do not teach, cities people cannot afford, and a generation that experiences the republic as a locked door. Unless that is answered materially—through staffing, housing, wages and a state that still functions in the banlieues and provincial lycées—the street will remain available to whoever is organized.

In October, those organized fragments were black blocs and, in some places, Islamist-adjacent and pro-Palestinian networks. Next time, the mixture may change. The far right is already measuring the ashes for the 2027 French presidential election and for electoral gains in Spain and Italy. That is not a reason to pretend the ashes contain no politics. It is a reason not to let the far right monopolize the act of noticing them.

The second emergency is found in the police file: a jihadist movement that has killed hundreds across Europe, still carries out attacks every year, still recruits in Maghrebi communities and among people with no migration background at all, and now has a base in the Sahel within reach of Spanish and Italian shores.

War in the Arab east and state collapse on Europe’s southern flank did not “send terrorists” in the simplistic sense. But they expanded the supply of weapons, veterans, grievances and propaganda—all in the name of a theology that divides humanity into believers and targets.

Europe imported labor for decades and convinced itself that integration would happen automatically. Workers integrated, unevenly. An anti-liberal religious politics did not, because that was never the same task.

What a serious left would say

A left with any remaining Marxist instinct would hold both emergencies open at once.

It would say that the boy in the leaking classroom is not a suspect—and that the network moving money from Tangier to a cell in Spain is not merely a victim of bad housing policy. It would defend the secular school against the minister starving it and against the activist seeking to subordinate it to a religious cause.

It would reject Trump’s sentence—that the riot is Islam. But it would also reject the reply that there is nothing to see but budgets.

October showed a republic arguing with itself in the streets, while an older ideological war waited at the edge of the crowd—and found friends.
