Babaylan: Spiritual Leaders of the Pre-Colonial Philippines

Journey into the rich spiritual world of the Philippines before colonial rule — where healers, priestesses, and ritual specialists shaped society, culture, and identity.

As we step back through time, join me as I guide you into the rich world of the Philippines before colonial rule. Historical documents, oral traditions, archaeology, and the memories preserved by communities help us understand who we are today. We cannot separate ourselves from the past, because it has shaped our society, culture, beliefs, languages, and identity. In this episode, I will share factual information about the babaylan, who were important spiritual leaders in many pre-colonial Philippine communities.

But who were the babaylan? What roles did they perform in their communities? Where did their traditions come from? And why were they often consulted before a village went to war or launched a raiding expedition?

Before Christianity and Islam became major influences in the Philippine Islands, communities followed their own religious traditions. At the center of many of these traditions were spiritual specialists known as the babaylan. They were not merely “witches,” fortune-tellers, or mysterious figures from old stories. They were respected leaders whose work touched religion, healing, education, memory, agriculture, diplomacy, and even warfare. To understand the babaylan is to understand a Philippines that already possessed complex systems of belief, governance, medicine, art, and social organization long before colonization.

A Philippines Before Colonization

Long before Spanish ships arrived in the sixteenth century, the Philippine archipelago was home to many independent communities. These communities were often organized around kinship, geography, trade, and shared customs. In many areas, especially in the Visayas and parts of Luzon, a local political leader known as the datu guided the community. The datu was not always an absolute ruler in the modern sense. Authority often depended on wealth, bravery, family lineage, generosity, alliances, and the ability to protect and provide for followers.

Alongside the datu stood another powerful figure: the babaylan. In the Tagalog region, this ritual specialist was often called the catalonan or katalonan. In the Visayas, the term babaylan or baylan became especially well known. Other communities used related titles such as balian. Although the names differed, the basic role was similar: the babaylan served as a mediator between the human community and the spiritual world.

This does not mean that all pre-colonial Filipino societies were exactly the same. The Philippines was never a single, unified kingdom before colonization. Different islands, language groups, and communities had different customs, gods, spirits, rituals, and political structures. However, many communities shared important ideas: respect for ancestors, belief in nature spirits, the importance of rituals, and the need for spiritual specialists who could guide people through life’s most difficult moments.

Who Were the Babaylan?

The word babaylan is commonly associated with indigenous shamans or priestesses in the Philippines. However, the name and exact duties varied among different ethnic groups. In the Visayas, the term babaylan became especially well known. Other communities used different titles, including balian and catalonan. These individuals were believed to communicate with anito—ancestral spirits—and with other supernatural beings connected to nature, illness, fertility, weather, and the community’s welfare.

The babaylan were not simply fortune-tellers. Their work involved religion, medicine, education, tradition, and community leadership. They served as religious leaders during important ceremonies. They could lead rituals for planting, harvesting, healing, marriage, birth, death, and protection. They also preserved important knowledge through prayers, chants, songs, stories, and oral traditions.

Many babaylan were women, and their authority was closely connected to ideas of fertility, life, healing, and the ability to sustain the community. However, not all babaylan were women. Some historical sources also describe men who took on the role and sometimes adopted feminine dress or behavior. These individuals were known in some contexts as asog or bayog. This reminds us that gender in pre-colonial Philippine societies could be more flexible than the strict categories later imposed during colonial rule.

The babaylan’s authority did not usually come from owning land, commanding soldiers, or holding a formal political office. Instead, it came from spiritual prestige, knowledge, experience, and the community’s trust. A babaylan was believed to have the ability to speak with spirits, interpret signs, heal sickness, and maintain balance between the living and the unseen world. This made the babaylan one of the most respected figures in the community.

The Spiritual World of the Anito

To understand the babaylan, we must first understand the spiritual world in which they worked. Many pre-colonial Filipinos believed in anito or diwata. These terms could refer to ancestral spirits, nature spirits, guardian beings, or powerful deities. They were not always distant gods living in a faraway heaven. Often, they were understood as present in the world around people: in rivers, mountains, large trees, rocks, fields, houses, and sacred places.

Because communities depended heavily on agriculture, fishing, forests, and rivers, spiritual rituals were closely connected with the natural environment. A successful harvest, a safe journey, recovery from illness, or victory in conflict could all be understood as events requiring spiritual guidance and proper ritual action. People offered food, rice wine, betel nut, animals, and other gifts to honor spirits, ask for protection, or restore good relations with the unseen world.

The babaylan performed rituals and sacrifices to appease, honor, and converse with these spirits. These traditions were believed to help ensure a bountiful harvest, a positive outcome in war, and wise decisions in trade, travel, and marriage. They also assisted in rituals for safe childbirth and sacred preparations for death. In this sense, the babaylan’s duties covered nearly every major moment of community life.

This worldview did not separate nature, religion, and daily life in the way many modern societies do. The forest was not only a source of wood and food; it could also be the home of spirits. The river was not only used for fishing and transportation; it could be sacred. Illness was not only a physical problem; it could be connected to an offended spirit, soul loss, sorcery, or a broken relationship with ancestors. The babaylan helped the community understand and respond to these connections.

Healers of Body and Spirit

One of the babaylan’s most important duties was healing. They used knowledge of plants, roots, leaves, massage, chants, prayers, and ritual offerings. Some healing practices were practical and medicinal, while others addressed the spiritual causes that people believed were behind an illness. It is important to understand that pre-colonial Filipino beliefs did not always separate medicine, religion, nature, and community life in the same way modern societies often do.

For the babaylan, caring for a sick person could involve treating the body, restoring spiritual balance, and asking ancestors or spirits for help. In some Visayan beliefs, a person was thought to have more than one aspect of the soul. One was connected to breath and physical life, while another could travel during dreams or become lost through spiritual danger. If a person became severely ill, confused, or emotionally troubled, the babaylan might perform a ritual to recover the lost soul and restore well-being.

The babaylan was skilled in herb lore. They could prepare remedies, antidotes, and other treatments from roots, leaves, seeds, and other natural materials. They also used incantations, dances, offerings, and trance-like rituals. To modern readers, some of these practices may seem supernatural. But we should be careful not to dismiss them. Traditional healers often possessed real knowledge of local plants, wounds, childbirth, fevers, and the environment. Their work combined what we might now call medicine, psychology, community care, and religious practice.

Babaylan also helped ensure safe pregnancy and childbirth. Birth was a dangerous and sacred event, especially in communities without modern hospitals. The babaylan could lead prayers, perform protective rituals, and guide the family through the process. At the other end of life, they helped prepare the dead for burial and guided mourning rituals. Death was not simply the end of the body; it was a passage that required proper respect for the deceased and their spirit.

Keepers of Memory and Tradition

The babaylan also served as keepers of memory. In an oral society, knowledge survived through people who could remember and transmit it. There were no printed books in most communities, and knowledge was stored in human memory, song, chant, story, and ritual. The babaylan helped preserve stories about ancestors, customs, sacred places, rituals, and the origins of the community.

They memorized and retold songs, old poems, stories, and beliefs that bound the people together. Through epic chants and ritual performances, they passed on tribal knowledge to the next generation. This made the babaylan a kind of teacher, historian, poet, and cultural guardian. They did not only explain the past; they kept the community’s identity alive.

This role was especially important because pre-colonial communities valued lineage and ancestry. Knowing the names and deeds of ancestors could establish a family’s status, explain alliances, and justify leadership. A babaylan who could chant genealogies was not merely entertaining the community. They were preserving the social map that told people who they were, where they came from, and how they related to one another.

Their knowledge also included cosmology, natural signs, weather, agriculture, and ritual practice. Some historians describe the babaylan as a proto-scientist because they observed nature, studied plants, understood seasonal cycles, and developed practical knowledge long before modern scientific institutions existed. Of course, we should not force modern categories onto the past. But it is fair to say that the babaylan were intellectual leaders of their communities.

Advisers to the Datu

The babaylan were also advisers to political leaders, including the datu. Their influence did not always come from political office. Instead, it came from spiritual authority, knowledge, experience, and their relationship with the community. In many communities, the datu and babaylan formed a powerful partnership: the datu handled leadership, defense, and external relations, while the babaylan guided ritual life, healing, and spiritual decision-making.

This relationship was not always equal, and it was not always peaceful. A strong datu could have great influence, but a respected babaylan could also challenge or balance that power. Some accounts suggest that the babaylan’s spiritual authority served as a counterweight to the datu’s political and military authority. The datu could command warriors, but the babaylan could read the omens that might determine whether a raid should proceed at all.

In some communities, the babaylan was so respected that they could act as an interim leader if the datu was absent. This shows that spiritual authority could become political authority. The babaylan was not outside society; they were one of its central pillars.

Their advice was sought for many decisions: when to plant, when to travel, when to marry, how to respond to illness, and how to deal with conflict. Because the babaylan was believed to understand the will of the spirits, their guidance carried great weight. A datu who ignored important spiritual signs might risk losing the trust of the community.

Rituals for Planting, Harvest, and Daily Life

Pre-colonial communities depended on the land and sea. Rice, root crops, fruits, fish, forest products, and livestock were essential for survival. Because of this, rituals connected to planting and harvesting were among the most important ceremonies led by the babaylan.

Before planting, the community might ask for permission from spirits associated with the land. Offerings could be placed near fields, trees, houses, or granaries. The babaylan might chant prayers, dance, and invoke ancestors to bless the seeds and protect the crops. During harvest, rituals expressed gratitude for abundance and asked for continued protection from pests, storms, and disease.

Fishing communities also relied on spiritual guidance. The sea could provide food, but it could also bring storms, accidents, and danger. Before long voyages or important fishing expeditions, the babaylan might perform rituals for safe travel. These ceremonies reflected a practical truth: people’s survival depended on forces they could not fully control. Ritual gave them a way to face uncertainty with courage, discipline, and unity.

Marriage, birth, and death were also marked by ritual. A wedding was not only an agreement between two individuals; it could unite families, create alliances, and transfer property or status. The babaylan could bless the union and ask the ancestors to guide the new household. At birth, rituals protected the mother and child. At death, rituals honored the departed and helped the community mourn.

Warfare, Raiding, and Spiritual Preparation

Babaylan could also have an important role before warfare or raiding expeditions. A raiding campaign was not only a military decision. It could affect the entire community, including its families, resources, trade relations, and spiritual security. Before warriors departed, the babaylan could conduct rituals, interpret signs, consult spirits, and seek protection for the expedition.

They might advise the datu and warriors about whether the timing was favorable. Omens, dreams, unusual events, and ritual signs could be interpreted as warnings or encouragement. A strange bird call, an unusual dream, a bad sign during a ritual, or an unexpected event could be understood as a message from the spirit world. The babaylan’s job was to interpret these signs and help leaders decide what to do.

The babaylan could also perform ceremonies intended to strengthen courage, protect the warriors, and ask for the support of ancestors or spirits. These rituals were not empty performances. They helped create unity, discipline, and confidence. Warriors preparing for danger needed more than weapons; they needed a sense that their cause was righteous, their ancestors were with them, and their community stood behind them.

It is important to remember that raiding and warfare in the pre-colonial Philippines were part of a wider world of alliances, revenge, trade, status, and survival. Communities sometimes competed over resources, trade routes, people, and political influence. The babaylan did not simply encourage violence. Their role was to help the community approach such dangerous events with spiritual care and to reduce the risk of disaster.

Clothing, Objects, and Ritual Performance

The clothing and accessories of the babaylan varied by region and community, but their appearance often reflected their special status. Ritual leaders might wear layered textiles, ornaments, headdresses, or objects believed to hold spiritual power. Gold ornaments, beads, bells, staffs, and ritual containers could all be part of ceremonial dress. These objects were not merely decorative. They symbolized authority, protected the wearer, and helped create the sacred atmosphere needed for ritual.

During ceremonies, the babaylan might chant, dance, drum, burn offerings, or enter a trance. Spanish accounts described babaylan entering altered states of consciousness to communicate with the spirit world. To the communities themselves, this was not “performance” in a shallow sense. It was a serious religious act in which the babaylan became a bridge between the living and the spirits.

Music, dance, and poetry were central to these rituals. Chants carried prayers, histories, and sacred knowledge. Dance helped express devotion and invite spiritual presence. Through these performances, the babaylan transformed an ordinary gathering into a sacred event. This shows that pre-colonial Philippine culture already had rich artistic traditions connected to belief and community life.

Women, Power, and Social Status

The babaylan also reveal something important about gender and power in pre-colonial Philippine society. Many babaylan were women, and they held authority that could rival or complement that of male political leaders. Their power came from their role as spiritual mediators, healers, and guardians of communal knowledge.

Some historians argue that women in many pre-colonial Philippine communities enjoyed greater social respect than women did under later colonial systems. The babaylan is one example of this. She was not defined only as a wife, mother, or daughter. She could be a leader, healer, teacher, ritual specialist, and adviser.

The existence of male babaylan who adopted feminine roles also challenges the idea that gender roles were always rigid. Early Spanish sources recorded the asog or bayog, men who took on the duties and sometimes the dress of female ritual specialists. Their presence shows that spiritual authority was not always tied to biological sex in the way colonial society later demanded.

This history matters today because it reminds us that Filipino culture was not always shaped by foreign ideas about gender, religion, and authority. Pre-colonial communities had their own ways of recognizing leadership, honoring women, and understanding identity. The babaylan stands as evidence of a society with a more complex view of power than is often assumed.

The Arrival of Colonization

The world of the babaylan changed dramatically with Spanish colonization in the sixteenth century. Spanish missionaries saw indigenous rituals, spirit beliefs, and babaylan authority as obstacles to Christian conversion. They targeted the babaylan because their spiritual influence competed with that of Catholic priests. Ritual objects, images, and sacred places were destroyed or dishonored in some areas.

The babaylan were often portrayed by colonizers as witches, deceivers, or servants of evil. This was not simply a religious disagreement. It was also a struggle over power. As long as the babaylan retained the people’s trust, they represented an authority outside the control of the Church and colonial state.

Many babaylan resisted. Some continued their practices in secret. Others adapted by blending Catholic images and rituals with older beliefs. In later centuries, babaylan-like figures appeared in resistance movements against Spanish rule. Their continued influence shows that indigenous spirituality did not disappear overnight. It survived, changed, and adapted under pressure.

The suppression of the babaylan also affected Filipino ideas about women, gender, healing, and leadership. When colonial authorities dismissed female spiritual leaders as immoral or dangerous, they weakened a long tradition of women’s public authority. Understanding the babaylan therefore helps us recover a more complete picture of Philippine history.

Why the Babaylan Still Matter

Why should we study the babaylan today? First, they show us that pre-colonial Filipinos had sophisticated belief systems. They were not a people without history, religion, or knowledge. They had healers, leaders, poets, ritual specialists, and guardians of tradition.

Second, the babaylan remind us that Filipino identity is layered. Christianity and Islam are major parts of Philippine life today, but they arrived in a land that already had deep spiritual traditions. Recognizing the babaylan does not mean rejecting modern faith. It means acknowledging the older roots from which Filipino culture grew.

Third, the babaylan challenge us to respect indigenous knowledge. Their healing traditions, environmental awareness, oral literature, and community-centered leadership remain relevant. In a time of environmental crisis, cultural loss, and social division, the babaylan’s connection between people, nature, ancestors, and community offers an important lesson.

Finally, the babaylan remind us that history is not only about kings, wars, and conquest. It is also about healers, singers, mothers, teachers, and spiritual guides. The babaylan were spiritual leaders of many pre-colonial Philippine communities. They healed the sick, led ceremonies, preserved oral traditions, advised leaders, interpreted omens, and helped prepare communities for major events, including warfare and raiding expeditions.

Their clothing and accessories varied, but their role was consistently connected with spiritual authority, community welfare, and ancestral knowledge. To study the babaylan is to look at a Philippines that already had complex religious systems, social institutions, medicine, art, and leadership long before colonization.

The babaylan were not relics of a forgotten past. They are part of the story of who we are. When we remember them, we remember that Filipino identity was formed not only by colonization, but also by the wisdom, resilience, and creativity of the communities that lived in these islands long before foreign powers arrived.
