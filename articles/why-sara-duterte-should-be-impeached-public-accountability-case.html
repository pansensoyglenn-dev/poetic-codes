Why Sara Duterte Should Be Impeached: Public Accountability Case

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · Local Politics · October 9, 2026

Vice President Sara Duterte should face impeachment not because she belongs to a powerful political family, but because the Philippines cannot afford leaders who treat public office as a private inheritance. The allegations now before the Senate—unexplained wealth, incomplete asset declarations, and continued business interests while in office—go to the heart of public trust. If proven, they would show that a national leader may have placed personal and family interests above the constitutional duty to serve the Filipino people.

This article argues for impeachment as a constitutional remedy, not as a slogan, a vendetta, or an attack on any family name. Impeachment is the people’s tool for holding the highest officials accountable when evidence shows betrayal of public trust, culpable violation of the Constitution, graft, or corruption. The Filipino masses must understand this process because political dynasties survive when citizens mistake loyalty to a surname for loyalty to the nation.

The central principle

Public office is a public trust. This is not merely a phrase printed in civics textbooks; it is the foundation of democratic government. A vice president does not own the Office of the Vice President, its budget, its staff, or the authority attached to it. These belong to the Filipino people.

When citizens elect a leader, they do not surrender their right to ask questions. They do not surrender their right to demand financial transparency. They do not surrender their right to insist that a leader’s private wealth, business dealings, and family transactions be explained when those matters intersect with public duty.

A political dynasty may be legal under Philippine law, but legality is not the same as legitimacy. A family can win elections through name recognition, machinery, wealth, media influence, and political alliances. Yet no election victory erases the people’s right to demand honesty from those who govern them.

The issue is not whether the Duterte family is popular in Davao or elsewhere. The issue is whether Sara Duterte, as Vice President, has met the standards required of someone entrusted with immense public power. The Senate impeachment court must decide that question based on evidence, but citizens must not be afraid to demand that the question be answered.

What impeachment means

Impeachment is often misunderstood. It is not a criminal conviction, and it does not automatically mean jail. Under Article XI of the 1987 Constitution, impeachable officials may be removed from office upon conviction for culpable violation of the Constitution, treason, bribery, graft and corruption, other high crimes, or betrayal of public trust.

A conviction in an impeachment trial can lead to removal from office and disqualification from holding public office under the Republic. However, the convicted official may still face criminal prosecution, trial, and punishment under ordinary law.

This distinction matters. Impeachment is a political and constitutional process, but it is not a substitute for criminal investigation. If financial records show unlawful conduct, the appropriate agencies must investigate and prosecute under the law. Impeachment addresses whether an official can remain in office; criminal prosecution addresses whether that official violated criminal statutes.

The Filipino people should therefore reject two false ideas. The first is that impeachment is “harassment” whenever it targets a powerful politician. The second is that impeachment is “justice” simply because a person is unpopular. Impeachment is neither. It is a serious constitutional process that must rest on evidence, due process, and the constitutional grounds enumerated in the Charter.

The remaining impeachment case

The current impeachment case against Sara Duterte is narrower than the original complaint. The House prosecution withdrew three of the four articles of impeachment in October 2026, leaving Article II as the sole remaining charge.

Article II alleges that Duterte amassed unexplained wealth disproportionate to her lawful income, failed to fully and truthfully disclose assets in her Statements of Assets, Liabilities and Net Worth (SALNs), and continued business interests during her tenure as Vice President.

These allegations are serious because they concern the basic duty of a public official to be transparent about personal wealth. A SALN is not a meaningless form. It is one of the few tools citizens have to detect conflicts of interest, illicit enrichment, and abuse of power.

If a public official’s declared assets do not match their known income, financial transactions, corporate interests, or property holdings, the public deserves an explanation. The burden of explaining suspicious discrepancies should not fall on ordinary citizens who have no access to bank records, corporate filings, or government databases.

The allegation of unexplained wealth

The prosecution’s forensic financial analyst, Alexander Cabrera, testified that Duterte and her husband, lawyer Manases Carpio, allegedly had P817.87 million in unexplained wealth from 2022 to 2025. The prosecution’s analysis was based on bank and tax records, corporate records, SALNs, and other financial documents.

According to reporting on the testimony, the alleged amount included undeclared cash, undeclared personal and real properties, and undeclared income. The prosecution also cited alleged undeclared income connected to Cale 88 Foods Corp., Metro Chow King Food Corp., and GenCorp Industries Inc.

These figures are allegations, not established facts. Duterte’s defense team disputes them and has presented its own witnesses and evidence. The Senate must weigh the competing evidence before reaching a verdict.

But the scale of the allegation explains why the public should pay attention. When the possible gap between declared wealth and financial activity reaches hundreds of millions of pesos, this is not a minor clerical issue. It concerns whether a national official has accurately disclosed the economic reality of her household and business interests.

Ordinary Filipinos are required to explain their income, pay taxes, and account for their assets. A vice president should be held to an even higher standard, not a lower one. Public officials enjoy public trust, public resources, and public power; therefore, they must accept public scrutiny.

SALNs and the duty to disclose

The SALN is one of the most important anti-corruption instruments in Philippine public life. It requires public officials to declare their assets, liabilities, net worth, and financial interests. Its purpose is simple: to allow the public and oversight institutions to detect unexplained enrichment and conflicts of interest.

The prosecution alleges that Duterte’s SALNs from 2022 to 2025 were untruthful because substantial assets belonging to her and her husband were not reported. The defense contests this interpretation and argues that the assets and business interests were properly declared or explained.

An Ombudsman official testified that Duterte declared several business interests in her SALNs without indicating corresponding shares of stock or acquisition costs. The same testimony noted that her declared net worth rose from P7.25 million in 2007, when she became Davao City vice mayor, to P98.66 million in her 2025 SALN.

Again, a rise in net worth is not automatically proof of corruption. People may inherit property, receive lawful income, make investments, or benefit from business growth. But when a public official’s wealth rises dramatically while they hold high office, the public has every right to ask for a credible explanation.

The question is not whether Sara Duterte is wealthy. Wealth is not a crime. The question is whether her wealth can be lawfully explained and whether it was honestly declared. Those are different questions, and both matter in a democracy.

Business interests in public office

Another major issue is Duterte’s alleged continued participation in business while serving as Vice President. The prosecution argues that she remained a shareholder and board member of Metro City Chow Foods Corp. from 2018 to 2025, and that SEC records show a 20 percent ownership stake based on subscription fees paid from 2022 to 2024.

The prosecution says this violates Article VII, Section 13 of the Constitution, which prohibits the President, Vice President, Cabinet members, and their relatives from participating in certain business activities or holding financial interests that may create conflicts of interest.

Duterte’s defense disputes the prosecution’s interpretation and evidence. The Senate must determine whether the constitutional prohibition applies to the facts proven at trial.

Still, the principle is clear. A vice president should not be able to use public office while maintaining private business interests that could benefit from government decisions, public contracts, regulatory discretion, or political influence. This is not about punishing success in business. It is about preventing public power from becoming a tool for private enrichment.

The Filipino people should ask a simple question: if a leader can profit from private business while holding national office, how can citizens be sure that public decisions are made for the public good rather than for private advantage?

Why dynasties must be scrutinized

Political dynasties are not automatically corrupt. Some members of political families may be competent, honest, and effective public servants. But dynasties create special risks because power, wealth, name recognition, and political networks can be passed from one generation to the next.

When the same families dominate provinces, cities, congressional districts, and national offices for decades, ordinary citizens may find it difficult to compete fairly in elections. Political dynasties can control local machinery, business relationships, media narratives, and access to government resources. This can turn democracy into a family enterprise.

This is why dynasties must be scrutinized more closely, not less. Power that is concentrated in a family must be matched by stronger transparency, stronger oversight, and stronger citizen vigilance.

The Duterte family has been a dominant force in Davao politics for decades. Sara Duterte herself served as Davao City mayor before becoming Vice President. Her father, former President Rodrigo Duterte, remains one of the most influential political figures in modern Philippine history. This political history does not prove wrongdoing, but it does explain why the public has a legitimate interest in ensuring that family power does not become family immunity.

The danger of political loyalty

One of the greatest obstacles to accountability in the Philippines is political loyalty. Supporters often defend a leader not because they have examined the evidence, but because the leader belongs to their political tribe, region, religion, class, or family network.

This is dangerous. When citizens defend corruption because the corrupt official belongs to their preferred political family, they become participants in the problem. They allow public office to become a shield for misconduct.

Loyalty to a political dynasty should never outweigh loyalty to the Constitution. A true patriot does not ask, “Is this person from my side?” A true patriot asks, “Is this person honest? Is this person accountable? Does this person serve the people or serve themselves?”

The Filipino masses must stop treating elections as family feuds. Elections are not contests between clans. They are decisions about who will control public money, public institutions, public safety, public education, public health, and the future of the country.

The earlier confidential-funds issue

The original impeachment complaint also included allegations involving confidential funds. The House alleged misuse of P612.5 million in confidential funds from the Office of the Vice President and the Department of Education.

However, the prosecution withdrew the article concerning those allegations in October 2026. Therefore, the current Senate trial does not rest on the confidential-funds issue.

This does not mean the public should forget the issue. Confidential funds deserve special scrutiny precisely because they are, by design, difficult to audit. Secrecy may sometimes be necessary for national security or intelligence work, but secrecy must never become a cover for waste, abuse, or corruption.

Citizens should demand clear rules on how confidential funds are requested, spent, documented, audited, and reported. Public money does not become private money simply because it is labeled “confidential.” The public has a right to know that every peso of taxpayer money is used for a lawful public purpose.

The Supreme Court’s earlier ruling

The impeachment process has already faced major legal obstacles. In 2025, the Supreme Court declared the Articles of Impeachment lodged by the House unconstitutional, citing procedural and due-process concerns.

Importantly, the Supreme Court stated that its ruling did not absolve Duterte of the charges. The Court addressed the constitutional validity of the impeachment process, not the truth or falsity of the allegations themselves.

This distinction is crucial. A procedural ruling does not erase the public’s right to demand accountability. It also does not prove guilt. It means the case must proceed through a constitutionally valid process.

The Supreme Court later laid down standards for a valid impeachment complaint. The current Senate trial is active, and the defense began presenting evidence in November 2026.

Why the public must follow the trial

Many Filipinos may feel disconnected from impeachment proceedings. The language is legalistic. The process is slow. The news is filled with technical debates about evidence, jurisdiction, procedure, and constitutional interpretation.

But citizens should not tune out. Impeachment is one of the few moments when the highest officials of the land can be directly questioned about their conduct in office. It is a public lesson in how power is supposed to be controlled.

When citizens ignore impeachment, they allow political elites to define the narrative. When citizens follow the evidence, ask questions, and demand transparency, they remind politicians that public office belongs to the people.

The Senate impeachment court has admitted much of the prosecution’s documentary evidence, including SALNs, bank records, tax records, corporate filings, property records, Anti-Money Laundering Council reports, and a financial forensic report. The court clarified that admitting evidence does not mean it proves guilt; the senator-judges must still determine its weight and sufficiency.

That is exactly how a serious democratic institution should work. Evidence must be tested. Accusations must be challenged. Defense must be heard. But the public must not be asked to accept secrecy as normal or opacity as protection.

What the Filipino masses must learn

The Filipino masses must learn that political dynasties do not automatically serve the people. Some do. Many do not. The test is not the family name, the campaign posters, the religious slogans, or the emotional speeches. The test is the record.

Citizens should ask:

  • Does the leader disclose their wealth honestly and completely?
  • Does the leader separate public duty from private business?
  • Does the leader explain sudden increases in wealth?
  • Does the leader use public office to benefit family members or allies?
  • Does the leader respect oversight institutions, auditors, journalists, and critics?
  • Does the leader treat public funds as sacred trust or as personal reward?
  • Does the leader accept accountability when evidence raises serious questions?

These questions should be asked of every politician, regardless of party, region, religion, or family name.

The cost of impunity

Impunity is expensive. It is paid for by ordinary Filipinos who struggle with low wages, high prices, poor public services, weak hospitals, overcrowded schools, and limited opportunities.

When public officials escape accountability, the message is clear: the rules apply to ordinary people, but not to the powerful. This corrodes faith in government. It teaches young people that connections matter more than competence, and that family names matter more than integrity.

The Philippines cannot build a fair society if its citizens believe that the powerful are above the law. A country becomes stronger not when its leaders are worshiped, but when its institutions are respected.

Impeachment is one way to reinforce that principle. It tells every public official that power is temporary, but accountability is permanent.

Fairness still matters

Calling for impeachment does not mean declaring Sara Duterte guilty before trial. Due process requires that she be allowed to answer the allegations, present evidence, cross-examine witnesses, and challenge the prosecution’s case. Her defense team is doing exactly that.

But due process also requires that the public take the allegations seriously. It requires that evidence be examined openly. It requires that political influence not be allowed to bury inconvenient facts.

The proper position is neither blind condemnation nor blind loyalty. It is informed vigilance. Citizens should support a fair trial, demand full disclosure, and insist that the Senate decide based on evidence rather than political convenience.

A country worth defending

The Philippines does not lack talented people, hardworking citizens, or democratic ideals. What it often lacks is the political courage to confront entrenched power.

If Sara Duterte is found guilty of betraying public trust or violating the Constitution, she should be removed from office. If the evidence does not support conviction, she should be acquitted under the rule of law. Either outcome must be based on facts, not fear, favoritism, or family loyalty.

The Filipino people must remember that no leader is bigger than the nation. No dynasty is bigger than the Constitution. No political name is bigger than the public interest.

The fight against corruption is not merely about one vice president. It is about whether Filipinos will continue to accept a system in which power is inherited, wealth is concealed, and accountability is optional. It is about whether the masses will remain spectators in their own democracy—or become the force that demands a government worthy of their sacrifice.

Public office must serve the Filipino people. Not dynasties. Not families. Not political allies. Not personal fortunes. The people.

— A case for public accountability, based on constitutional principles and the ongoing impeachment proceedings.
