Philippine Supernatural Belief: Aswang, Anito & Diwata History

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · Culture · November 1, 2026

From anito to aswang: how Filipino spirits survived colonialism and faith.

Written by Glenn Junsay Pansensoy  ·  Culture  ·  9 min read

The supernatural beliefs of the Philippines form one of Southeast Asia’s most enduring cultural traditions. Long before Spanish colonization, Filipino communities understood the world as inhabited by unseen powers: ancestral spirits, nature guardians, deities, and dangerous beings that moved between the human and spirit realms. These beliefs did not disappear with the arrival of Christianity; instead, they adapted, merged with Catholic practice, and continued to shape local ideas about illness, death, morality, and the natural environment.

The Pre-Colonial Spiritual World

Before the sixteenth century, most Philippine societies practiced forms of animism. They believed that trees, rivers, mountains, stones, animals, storms, and even ordinary household spaces could possess spiritual power. The dead, too, remained present in the lives of the living, requiring remembrance, offerings, and proper ritual treatment.

The term anito referred broadly to spirits, ancestors, and sacred presences. In many communities, anito were not automatically benevolent or malevolent. They could protect families, bless harvests, and heal sickness, but they could also become angry when neglected or offended. This worldview emphasized balance rather than a simple division between good and evil.

Ritual specialists served as intermediaries between the community and the spirit world. In the Visayas, they were often called babaylan or baylan; in parts of Luzon, katalonan. These figures healed the sick, interpreted omens, led agricultural rites, conducted funerals, and advised political leaders. Their authority came from spiritual knowledge, ritual skill, and their ability to communicate with unseen forces.

Diwata and Anito: Guardians of Nature and Ancestors

Diwata are commonly imagined today as fairies or beautiful nature spirits, but their historical meaning was broader and more complex. They could be deities, ancestral spirits, guardians of mountains and forests, or powerful beings connected to fertility, healing, and the land. The word itself entered Philippine languages through Sanskrit devatā, reflecting centuries of contact with Indianized kingdoms and trading networks in Maritime Southeast Asia.

Famous figures such as Maria Makiling, the guardian of Mount Makiling, represent the later folkloric image of the diwata. Yet in older traditions, diwata were not merely gentle spirits. They could be territorial and dangerous, especially when humans violated sacred spaces, hunted excessively, cut trees without permission, or failed to show respect.

Anito, by contrast, was more closely associated with ancestral and local spirits. Offerings of food, drink, cloth, animals, or other valued goods were made to secure protection, fertility, good fortune, and successful harvests. These practices expressed a reciprocal relationship between people and the unseen world: humans gave respect and tribute; spirits, in return, maintained order and well-being.

The Aswang: Fear, Ambiguity, and Social Boundaries

The aswang is perhaps the most widely recognized supernatural figure in Philippine folklore, especially in the Visayas, Bicol, and parts of Southern Luzon. It is not a single monster but an umbrella term for a range of nocturnal predatory beings. Depending on the region, an aswang may appear as a blood-drinking vampire, a corpse-eating ghoul, an animal shape-shifter, or a viscera-sucking creature.

The manananggal is one of the most distinctive forms. Often described as a woman by day, she supposedly separates her upper body from her lower half at night, grows wings, and flies in search of victims. Her long, tube-like tongue is associated with attacks on pregnant women and unborn children. This image reflects deep anxieties surrounding pregnancy, childbirth, disease, and maternal survival.

Other aswang traditions include the mandurugo, a vampire-like figure that drains blood from sleeping victims, and the bal-bal or busaw, corpse-eating beings associated with graves and funeral practices. Some aswang are said to transform into dogs, pigs, cats, or birds, allowing them to move unnoticed through villages after dark.

The aswang’s enduring power lies in its ambiguity. It may look like an ordinary neighbor during the day, making it a symbol of hidden danger within the community. Stories about aswang often expressed fears of unexplained deaths, strange illnesses, miscarriages, social outsiders, and violations of communal norms.

Other Beings of the Philippine Landscape

Philippine folklore includes many beings associated with specific places and human experiences. The kapre is a large, often tobacco-smoking tree spirit. Though sometimes frightening, it is more often a trickster than a murderer, known for confusing travelers or causing them to lose their way.

The duwende and nuno sa punso are small earth spirits believed to live in mounds, roots, trees, or underground spaces. Disturbing their homes without asking permission is said to cause illness, swelling, or misfortune. The customary phrase “tabi-tabi po,” meaning “excuse me, may I pass,” remains a living expression of this belief.

The tikbalang, a tall creature with a horse’s head and a humanoid body, inhabits forests, mountains, and remote paths. It is famous for leading travelers astray, turning familiar roads into endless journeys. Like the kapre, it represents the unpredictable dangers of the wilderness.

In Visayan mythology, the Bakunawa is a giant serpent or dragon associated with eclipses. Traditional stories describe it as attempting to swallow the moon, prompting communities to make noise, pray, or perform rituals to drive it away. The Bakunawa shows how Philippine mythology connected celestial events with sacred narrative and communal action.

Austronesian Roots

Philippine supernatural belief is part of a much older Austronesian cultural world. Austronesian-speaking peoples migrated across Island Southeast Asia and the Pacific over thousands of years, carrying with them ideas about ancestral spirits, sacred places, shamans, and the spiritual power of nature.

Similar concepts appear across the region. The Philippine anito has linguistic and thematic relatives in words such as aniti in Micronesia, antu in parts of Indonesia and Malaysia, and atua or aitu in Polynesia. These parallels point to shared Austronesian ideas about spirits of the dead and sacred non-human beings.

Predatory spirit traditions also have regional parallels. The Philippine manananggal resembles the Malay penanggalan, the Thai krasue, and the Balinese leyak: beings associated with detached body parts, night flight, and attacks on pregnant women. These similarities do not mean the creatures are identical, but they suggest that Philippine folklore developed within a wider Southeast Asian world of shared motifs and cultural exchange.

Indian Influence and Maritime Trade

Before Spanish colonization, Philippine societies were connected to extensive maritime trade networks linking China, mainland Southeast Asia, Indonesia, India, and the Middle East. Through contact with Indianized kingdoms such as Srivijaya and Majapahit, Sanskrit religious and political vocabulary entered many Southeast Asian languages.

The word diwata is one clear example. Derived from Sanskrit devatā, it merged with older indigenous ideas about powerful spirits and guardians. This did not replace native belief; rather, it enriched it. Local communities absorbed new terms and concepts while preserving their own rituals, ancestors, and sacred landscapes.

The origin of the word aswang is more debated. Some writers connect it to Sanskrit asura, a term for powerful demonic or anti-divine beings in Indian religion. While this connection is plausible, it should be treated cautiously, since Philippine aswang traditions also have strong local roots and regional parallels.

Spanish Colonization and Religious Change

Spanish missionaries arrived in the Philippines in the sixteenth century and sought to replace indigenous religion with Catholicism. Sacred objects were destroyed, rituals were suppressed, and native spiritual leaders were condemned as idolaters, witches, or servants of the devil.

The babaylan were especially targeted. As healers, ritual leaders, and community authorities, they competed with Spanish priests for religious influence. Many were accused of witchcraft, stripped of authority, persecuted, or forced to abandon their practices. Over time, colonial narratives transformed respected spiritual specialists into feared figures associated with kulam, or harmful magic.

This colonial transformation also affected beings such as the aswang. Indigenous beliefs about dangerous spirits already existed before Spanish rule, particularly in lowland Visayan and Bicol communities. However, Spanish missionaries reinterpreted these beings through a Catholic framework of sin, demonic possession, and witchcraft. The result was not the invention of Philippine folklore, but its reshaping under colonial pressure.

Survival and Adaptation

Philippine supernatural belief endured because it remained useful. It explained sickness, death, misfortune, and strange events. It taught respect for sacred places, warned against greed and disrespect, and preserved memories of ancestors and local landscapes.

Even after centuries of Christianization, many Filipinos continued to blend Catholic devotion with older practices. Prayers, charms, offerings, healing rituals, and respect formulas such as “tabi-tabi po” coexist with church attendance and Catholic sacraments. This blending is not a contradiction but a historical survival strategy: older spiritual ideas adapted to new religious and social conditions.

A Living Heritage

Philippine mythology is not a single, uniform system. It is a collection of regional traditions shaped by Austronesian migration, Indianized trade, local ecology, colonial history, and modern storytelling. The diwata reflects the sacredness of nature; the anito preserves the presence of ancestors; the aswang expresses fears surrounding illness, death, and hidden evil; and the babaylan recalls a pre-colonial spiritual authority that colonial rule tried to erase.

Together, these beliefs reveal a worldview in which humans, ancestors, animals, land, and spirits are deeply interconnected. Philippine supernatural folklore is therefore not simply a collection of scary stories. It is a historical record of how Filipino communities understood the world, defended social values, and maintained a relationship with the unseen forces surrounding them.
