The Obsolescence of Rigid Maoism in the Philippines: Why the Left Must Evolve

Glenn Junsay Pansensoy · Local Politics · November 24, 2026

The Philippine left cannot build a credible future by defending a revolutionary doctrine designed for conditions that have largely disappeared. A rigid Maoism—centered on a semi-feudal diagnosis of society, rural guerrilla warfare, and a centralized vanguard party—has become increasingly disconnected from the realities of contemporary Filipino life. The left does not need to abandon its concern for workers, peasants, the urban poor, indigenous communities, women, and victims of state abuse. It needs to abandon the assumption that one ideological formula, preserved unchanged for decades, can still deliver liberation.

The central question is no longer whether inequality, landlessness, corruption, foreign dependence, and political violence exist in the Philippines. They do. The question is whether a movement can respond to them with a strategy suited to the present—or whether it will continue asking new generations to sacrifice themselves for a war whose premises, methods, and promised outcome no longer match the country they live in.

A doctrine built for another era

The Communist Party of the Philippines was founded in 1968 under Jose Maria Sison. It adopted Marxism-Leninism-Maoism and developed a national-democratic framework that portrayed Philippine society as semi-colonial and semi-feudal. In this analysis, foreign imperialism, comprador capitalism, and landlordism were the principal enemies; the peasantry was the main revolutionary force; and armed struggle through the New People’s Army was the principal path to political power.

This framework emerged in a very different historical moment. The Cold War was intense, authoritarian rule was a live threat, rural poverty was widespread, and many Filipinos had little faith in electoral institutions. For a generation confronting dictatorship, land hunger, and political repression, revolutionary politics offered a powerful language of dignity, resistance, and hope.

But history did not remain frozen in 1968. The Philippines has changed economically, socially, demographically, and politically. Its population is increasingly urban. Its economy is tied to global supply chains, remittances, business process outsourcing, tourism, construction, informal labor, and digital platforms. Millions of Filipinos work in service sectors, factories, call centers, transport, retail, construction, and overseas employment rather than in the classic peasant economy imagined by older revolutionary theory.

A political movement that cannot accurately describe the society it seeks to change cannot credibly claim to lead it.

The problem with “protracted people’s war”

The defining feature of the CPP’s strategy has been the idea of a “protracted people’s war”: building rural base areas, expanding guerrilla zones, weakening the state through armed struggle, and gradually encircling the cities before seizing national power. This strategy places the countryside, the peasantry, and the NPA at the center of political transformation.

The strategy has endured for more than five decades. Yet it has not produced a revolutionary government, a durable alternative state, or a broad national consensus behind armed struggle. Instead, the conflict has continued across successive administrations, with repeated cycles of recruitment, combat, arrests, displacement, civilian harm, and failed peace processes. The insurgency remains active, but its strategic breakthrough remains as distant as ever.

This is not an argument that the Philippine state is just, humane, or beyond criticism. State violence, militarization, red-tagging, extrajudicial abuses, and the failure to address rural poverty all deserve condemnation. But condemning state abuses does not require treating armed struggle as the only legitimate form of politics.

A strategy can be morally serious only if it can answer a basic question: what has it actually achieved for the people it claims to represent? If the answer is another generation of funerals, destroyed communities, displaced families, imprisoned activists, and a leadership that remains unable to govern, then the ideology—not only the state—must be questioned.

The left’s real enemies are material, not doctrinal

The Philippine left often speaks as though the main obstacle to change is a lack of revolutionary consciousness. In reality, the obstacles are more concrete: low wages, insecure work, contractualization, unemployment, underemployment, inflation, debt, housing shortages, inadequate health care, weak public education, corruption, political dynasties, disaster vulnerability, and climate change.

These are not abstractions. They are the daily conditions through which millions of Filipinos experience inequality.

A renewed left should therefore be judged not by its ability to repeat revolutionary language, but by its ability to improve material life. Can it organize workers to win better contracts? Can it help communities secure clean water, housing, and disaster relief? Can it defend journalists, activists, and ordinary citizens from political persecution? Can it build institutions that survive elections, leadership disputes, and changing political conditions?

If not, then it is not a movement for liberation. It is a movement for perpetual opposition.

What Maoism gets wrong about the Philippines

The countryside is not the whole country

Classic Maoist strategy assumes that rural areas are the decisive revolutionary terrain. This made sense in societies where the majority of people were peasants and state power was weak outside major cities. But the Philippines is no longer simply a rural society.

Urban poor communities, workers, informal settlers, transport workers, call-center employees, teachers, nurses, students, migrants, and small entrepreneurs are all part of the political landscape. Ignoring them—or treating them merely as auxiliary supporters of a rural war—narrows the left’s imagination.

The peasantry is not the only revolutionary subject

Peasants and rural workers remain central to any serious program of land reform and rural justice. But they are not the only agents of change. Workers, women, urban poor communities, indigenous peoples, youth, professionals, and migrants all have distinct interests and capacities.

A left that speaks only in the language of peasant revolution risks becoming irrelevant to the very people it claims to represent.

Armed struggle does not automatically create socialism

The most dangerous assumption in rigid Maoism is that military struggle naturally leads to emancipation. History suggests otherwise. Armed movements can overthrow governments, but they do not automatically create democracy, prosperity, gender equality, accountable institutions, or a just economy.

Power without democratic institutions can reproduce the same domination it claims to oppose. A revolution that cannot tolerate debate, pluralism, or accountability is not a liberation movement; it is merely a new form of rule.

China and Vietnam: lessons without imitation

Supporters of rigid Maoism often point to China and Vietnam as proof that revolutionary socialism remains viable. But both countries have fundamentally revised the economic and strategic models associated with Maoist orthodoxy.

China’s reform and opening-up program moved the country away from a rigid planned economy toward what it officially calls a socialist market economy. Vietnam similarly developed a “socialist-oriented market economy,” combining market mechanisms with state intervention and continued one-party rule.

This does not mean the Philippine left should imitate China or Vietnam. Both countries remain authoritarian in significant ways, and their models raise serious concerns about political freedom, labor rights, censorship, and minority protections.

But the lesson is still important: even states that call themselves socialist recognized that rigid dogma could not solve modern economic problems. They adapted. They reformed. They experimented. They changed their methods while retaining certain political commitments.

The Philippine left should learn the same lesson without copying the same political system.

What the left should embrace instead

The alternative is not neoliberalism, passivity, or surrender to elite politics. It is a democratic, pluralist, and practical socialism grounded in the actual conditions of Filipino life.

A politics of social rights

The left should fight for universal health care, decent public education, affordable housing, food security, social protection, and a real social safety net. These are not “reformist” distractions. They are the basic conditions through which people can live with dignity and participate in politics.

A person who cannot afford medicine, rent, transportation, or food does not experience freedom in any meaningful sense. Social rights are not charity; they are the foundation of democratic citizenship.

A politics of labor

The left should organize workers in factories, farms, hospitals, schools, call centers, transport, construction, retail, and the informal economy. It should fight for living wages, secure employment, safe workplaces, collective bargaining, and protection against contractualization and abuse.

This means moving beyond symbolic protest and building durable institutions: unions, cooperatives, mutual-aid networks, worker education programs, and community organizations that can survive beyond a single rally or election cycle.

A politics of land and climate justice

Land reform remains necessary, but it must be connected to a broader rural program: irrigation, credit, storage, transportation, cooperatives, disaster preparedness, climate-resilient agriculture, and protection for farmers and fisherfolk.

The Philippines is one of the most disaster-prone countries in the world. Any left that ignores climate change, flooding, typhoons, drought, and environmental degradation is disconnected from the immediate survival concerns of millions of Filipinos.

A politics of democracy

The left should defend civil liberties, press freedom, academic freedom, electoral integrity, local autonomy, and the right to organize. It should oppose red-tagging, political persecution, and state violence—but it should also apply the same standards to itself.

A movement that demands democracy from the state while suppressing internal dissent is not democratic. A movement that claims to represent the people while treating criticism as betrayal is not liberationist.

Palestine is not a substitute for analysis

Many young Filipino activists feel genuine solidarity with Palestinians, particularly in the context of civilian suffering, displacement, and occupation. That solidarity can be morally serious. But it becomes politically shallow when it replaces analysis with symbolism.

Waving a Palestinian flag at a rally does not automatically make a movement internationally literate, strategically coherent, or genuinely leftist. It can become a substitute for thinking: a way of borrowing the emotional intensity of another conflict without developing an independent understanding of Philippine society.

The deeper danger is when activists adopt the language, symbols, and narratives of one struggle without applying consistent democratic principles. A serious left must be able to distinguish between Palestinian civilian rights and self-determination, on one hand, and uncritical identification with Hamas, Islamist politics, or any armed faction, on the other.

Marxism, at its best, begins from class, material conditions, democratic emancipation, and secular politics. Islamism is a religious-political project. It cannot simply be merged with socialism by adding a Palestinian flag to a Philippine protest.

The true test of solidarity is not which flag is most visible. It is whether activists can defend civilian life, oppose authoritarianism and theocracy, and apply the same democratic principles at home and abroad.

Historical literacy is part of Marxism

A serious left must also be historically literate. This is especially important in discussions of Israel and Palestine, where slogans often replace evidence.

Jewish resistance to Roman rule culminated in three major revolts between 66 and 135 CE: the Great Revolt, the diaspora revolts, and the Bar Kokhba Revolt. Rome destroyed the Second Temple in 70 CE, and after defeating the Bar Kokhba revolt in 132–135 CE it devastated Judaea, killed, enslaved, or displaced much of the Jewish population, renamed the province Syria Palaestina, and barred Jews from Jerusalem.

The claim that Rome violently suppressed Jewish resistance and contributed enormously to Jewish displacement is historically sound. The more precise formulation, however, is that Rome did not conduct one single, total “expulsion of all Jews from Israel.” Jewish communities had already existed across the Mediterranean and Middle East before these revolts, and Jewish presence in the land did not entirely end afterward. But the destruction of the Temple, the Bar Kokhba catastrophe, and Roman repression were decisive events in the long history of Jewish dispersion.

Historical literacy means recognizing that peoples can have deep roots in a place while also having been displaced, ruled, colonized, or denied political rights. It means understanding causation without pretending that one historical fact cancels every other.

Applying historical method to solidarity

A Marxist approach should examine history materially and contextually, not reduce it to a single moral label. Historical materialism asks how economic structures, class relations, state power, empire, and material conditions shape political conflict over time.

A historically literate left should therefore reject both forms of distortion:

  • Denying Jewish historical connection to the Land of Israel, or treating Roman-era persecution as irrelevant.
  • Using ancient Jewish history to deny Palestinians’ contemporary existence, rights, or national aspirations.

The historical record supports several facts at once: Jews had deep historical, religious, and political roots in the Land of Israel long before the Roman era; Roman conquest and repression devastated Jewish political life in Judaea and accelerated Jewish dispersal; modern Palestinians and modern Israelis both developed national identities in the modern era; and neither ancient history nor modern suffering erases the other people’s claims to dignity, security, and self-determination.

The left’s failure of method

The Philippine left often criticizes historical revisionism when it concerns Philippine history, martial law, or U.S. imperialism. It should apply the same standard to the Middle East.

A left that cannot distinguish between:

  • Judaism and the Israeli state;
  • Palestinians and Hamas;
  • criticism of Israeli government policy and antisemitism;
  • solidarity with civilians and endorsement of armed factions;

is not practicing Marxism. It is practicing propaganda.

Marxism, properly understood, begins with evidence, class analysis, and historical context. It does not begin with whichever flag produces the loudest applause at a rally.

The cost of stagnation

The most disturbing aspect of ideological stagnation is not that it produces bad slogans. It is that it can send young people into danger for a strategy that has not demonstrably advanced their liberation.

A movement that asks recruits to die for a “protracted war” must be able to answer a hard question: what exactly has decades of armed struggle won for Filipino workers, peasants, women, indigenous peoples, and urban poor?

If the answer is another generation of funerals, arrests, displacement, and shattered communities, then the ideology—not only the state—must be questioned. A revolution that cannot win power, improve lives, or build durable democratic institutions risks becoming not a revolution at all, but a self-perpetuating conflict.

A better left standard

The Philippine left should adopt a simple rule: no solidarity without historical literacy.

That means learning the history of Jewish dispersion, Roman imperial violence, Zionism, the Nakba, Palestinian displacement, the creation of Israel, subsequent wars, occupation, terrorism, and civilian suffering. It means refusing simplistic narratives that portray one side as purely innocent and the other as purely evil.

It also means recognizing that the Philippine left’s first responsibility is to Filipinos: to workers, peasants, urban poor communities, indigenous peoples, women, and youth. Solidarity with Palestinians should not become an excuse to neglect the concrete struggles happening in Philippine communities.

The left should be capable of saying difficult truths: Jewish history is real; Roman violence against Jews was real; Palestinian suffering is real; Hamas’s ideology and methods are real; Israeli state violence and occupation are real; and none of these facts can be erased by a slogan.

The choice ahead

The Philippine left does not need to disappear. It needs to mature.

It should retain its moral core—equality, dignity, national sovereignty, labor rights, and solidarity with the oppressed—while discarding the rigid Maoist assumption that history can be forced through a single armed formula. It should learn from China’s and Vietnam’s willingness to reform, from democratic socialist traditions, from labor organizing, and from the practical realities of Philippine democracy.

It should also replace slogan-based internationalism with historical literacy, secular democratic principles, and a commitment to civilian protection.

Otherwise, the left will remain trapped between two failures: an old revolution it cannot win, and a present it cannot meaningfully change.

The alternative is not surrender. It is renewal.
