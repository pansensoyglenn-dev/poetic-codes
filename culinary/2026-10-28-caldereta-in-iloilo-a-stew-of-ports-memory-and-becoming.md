  <h1>Caldereta in Iloilo: A Stew of Ports, Memory, and Becoming</h1>
  <p>Where old fires whisper and heirloom recipes drift like seeds through time, shaping the flavors that root us and the stories that feed our souls.</p>
</header>

<main>

  <p class="opening">Here is another stanza in our culinary voyage, where old fires whisper and heirloom recipes drift like seeds through time, shaping the flavors that root us and the stories that feed our souls.</p>

  <p>The galleons did not merely unload spices, cattle, and the products of the New World upon the port of Iloilo. Along with them came recipes—carried in memory, in habit, in the hands of cooks and colonizers, in the names of dishes spoken first in a foreign tongue and later made familiar at Filipino tables. Among these was the caldereta.</p>

  <p>Here, where river meets sea, a pot simmers over fire. Garlic sizzles. Onions soften. Tomatoes melt into oil. Meat browns, and steam rises in a telltale red fog. This is the caldereta.</p>

  <p>But not just any caldereta.</p>

  <p>This is the Ilonggo way: often goat or lamb, thickened with liver, cooked in pots large enough to feed families, parishes, and whole barrios. In Iloilo, a stew is never merely food. It is a signal—feast today, remember yesterday. It is an invitation: pull another chair closer, call a neighbor, make room at the table.</p>

  <p>What we now call Ilonggo caldereta did not begin in a Visayan kitchen. Its roots stretch back to the age of Spanish colonization, when new ingredients, new techniques, and new names began to appear in local markets and home cooking. Yet to say only that it is “Spanish” would be too simple. Caldereta is not a borrowed dish preserved unchanged in a colonial display case. It is a living record of encounter, scarcity, adaptation, and invention. It belongs to the long history of how Filipinos received foreign things and made them unmistakably their own.</p>

  <h2>The Name Remembers the Pot</h2>
  <p>The very word <em>caldereta</em> is a clue.</p>

  <p>It comes from the Spanish <em>caldereta</em>, a diminutive form connected to <em>caldero</em>, meaning a large cauldron or cooking pot. In turn, <em>caldero</em> is linked to the Spanish <em>cálido</em>, meaning warm or hot. The name, therefore, does not merely identify an ingredient. It remembers the vessel: a warm place where food is cooked, a pot placed over fire, a container of transformation.</p>

  <p>In Spain, caldereta referred to slow-cooked meat stews, often made with goat or lamb, sometimes with fish, simmered for hours until tender and deeply flavorful. These were hearty, rustic dishes meant to feed families and gatherings, cooked over open fires in heavy iron or clay pots. In Castile and León, where lamb was abundant, caldereta became especially associated with mutton or lamb simmered in aromatic broth, sometimes with the animal’s own liver.</p>

  <p>The dish was practical before it was celebratory. It made use of tougher cuts. It stretched meat across many mouths. It allowed a cook to leave a pot over low heat while tending to other work. It rewarded patience: the longer the stew simmered, the more the flavors settled into one another.</p>

  <p>That logic—slow heat, shared pot, abundance made from modest means—traveled well.</p>

  <p>When Spain established colonial rule in the Philippines, it brought more than soldiers, priests, and ships. It brought a whole food culture: ingredients, cooking methods, religious calendars, feast days, and the social habit of marking occasions with elaborate meals. Tomatoes, once rare in many local kitchens, became more common. Sautéing garlic, onions, and tomatoes became a standard beginning for many dishes. Stews that once bubbled in Iberian villages began to simmer in Filipino homes.</p>

  <p>Caldereta joined a family of Spanish-influenced dishes—mechado, afritada, menudo—that are now considered everyday Filipino food. Yet their familiarity should not obscure their layered origins. They are Filipino dishes, but they carry the fingerprints of empire, trade, migration, and local creativity.</p>

  <h2>Iloilo: Where History Comes Ashore</h2>
  <p>To understand why caldereta found such a resonant home in Iloilo, one must first understand Iloilo as a place of arrival.</p>

  <p>Long before Spanish colonization, merchants from China and India had traded with Ilonggos, and the region’s rivers, coasts, and fertile plains made it a natural crossroads of commerce. Iloilo’s harbor, sheltered by Guimaras Island, offered calm waters and safe navigation. Its location made it a center of trade long before it became formally known as an international port.</p>

  <p>The Spanish recognized this advantage early. In the sixteenth century, Arevalo—now a district of Iloilo City—already had shipyards where galleys and frigates were built. The port’s deep, navigable river and protected harbor made it valuable not only for defense but also for the movement of people, goods, and ideas.</p>

  <p>By the nineteenth century, Iloilo’s importance had grown enormously. On September 29, 1855, by royal order of Queen Isabella II, the port was opened to international trade. It became one of the most significant ports in the Visayas, drawing ships from across the archipelago and connecting Panay to global markets. The rise of the sugar industry further transformed the region, linking Iloilo to Negros and making the city a major commercial power second only to Manila in its heyday.</p>

  <p>This was not simply an economy of sugar, rice, textiles, and shipping. It was an economy of contact.</p>

  <p>Ships brought cargo, but they also brought tastes. Sailors, merchants, friars, officials, laborers, and migrants carried recipes in their heads and preferences in their palates. A port city is a kitchen as much as it is a marketplace. Ingredients arrive. Names are translated. Techniques are observed, copied, resisted, and remade.</p>

  <p>In such a place, caldereta could become more than a foreign stew. It could become an Ilonggo inheritance.</p>

  <h2>From Spain to the Filipino Table</h2>
  <p>When caldereta arrived in the Philippines, it encountered a different landscape of ingredients, animals, climate, and cooking traditions.</p>

  <p>Lamb, central to many Spanish versions, was scarce and expensive in much of the archipelago. Goat, however, was more accessible in many communities and had long been part of local foodways. It became the default meat for caldereta in many places. Beef followed as cities grew, slaughterhouses expanded, and meat became more widely available.</p>

  <p>This substitution was not a failure to reproduce the original. It was the beginning of a Filipino dish.</p>

  <p>Goat has a distinct character: gamy, earthy, robust. It demands a sauce that can stand beside it, something rich enough to soften its intensity and deep enough to complement it. The tomato base helped, but the decisive Filipino innovation was liver.</p>

  <p>In Spain, liver might appear as a separate dish or an occasional addition. In the Philippines, cooks began to mash or blend cooked liver—and later use ready-made liver spread—to thicken and enrich the sauce. This turned caldereta into something distinct: a tomato-based stew with a deep red color, a velvety texture, and a savory depth that Spanish caldereta did not have.</p>

  <p>The liver did more than thicken. It gave the sauce body. It added umami, a savory richness that made the stew feel complete. It turned a relatively simple meat stew into something almost luxurious. A spoonful of liver spread dissolved into the sauce could make an ordinary pot taste like a feast.</p>

  <p>This is one of the great patterns of Filipino cooking: taking a foreign frame and filling it with local intelligence. The Spanish gave the name and perhaps the broad idea of the stew. Filipino cooks gave it its soul.</p>

  <h2>The Anatomy of an Ilonggo Caldereta</h2>
  <p>An Ilonggo caldereta begins before the pot is even placed on the fire.</p>

  <p>There is the meat: goat, lamb, beef, or sometimes pork, cut into pieces large enough to survive a long simmer. Goat is traditional and celebratory, its flavor assertive and memorable. Beef has become common in many households, offering tenderness and richness. Lamb carries the closest connection to the Spanish original, though it remains less ordinary in many Filipino kitchens.</p>

  <p>The meat may be marinated or seasoned beforehand. Some cooks parboil it first, a practice related to <em>sangkutsa</em>, from the Spanish <em>salcochar</em>, meaning to boil in water and seasonings. This helps remove impurities, soften the meat, and prepare it for the longer cooking ahead.</p>

  <p>Then comes the aromatic foundation.</p>

  <p>Garlic enters hot oil first, releasing its fragrance. Onions follow, softening into sweetness. Tomatoes break down, becoming sauce. This sequence—garlic, onion, tomato—has become so familiar in Filipino cooking that it can feel timeless, yet it also reflects centuries of culinary exchange. It is the opening chord of countless Filipino dishes, from caldereta to afritada to mechado.</p>

  <p>The meat is browned, not merely boiled. Browning matters. It creates depth. It gives the stew a darker, more complex flavor. Then liquid is added, and time takes over.</p>

  <p>Potatoes and carrots may join the pot, absorbing the sauce and adding sweetness and heartiness. Bell peppers bring color and a gentle bitterness. Green olives, in some versions, add brine and a Mediterranean echo. Chili peppers bring heat, sometimes subtle, sometimes fierce. Bay leaves lend a quiet herbal background.</p>

  <p>And then, near the end, comes the liver.</p>

  <p>Whether freshly cooked and mashed or stirred in as liver spread, it darkens the sauce and gives it that unmistakable caldereta richness. The stew becomes glossy. It clings to the meat. It asks for rice—not as an afterthought, but as an essential partner.</p>

  <p>A proper caldereta is not dry. It is not thin. It is somewhere in between: saucy enough to spoon over rice, thick enough to coat every piece of meat. Its color is red-brown, almost brick-like, warmed by tomato and deepened by liver. Its aroma is immediate: savory, slightly sweet, faintly gamey, gently spicy.</p>

  <p>It smells like a gathering.</p>

  <h2>A Dish of Celebration</h2>
  <p>In Iloilo, as in much of the Philippines, caldereta is often a dish of occasion.</p>

  <p>It appears at fiestas, birthdays, reunions, baptisms, weddings, town celebrations, and Sunday lunches when relatives arrive from far away. It is not always an everyday meal, particularly when made with goat, which carries a special-occasion weight. Goat caldereta, in particular, signals that something matters: someone has returned, someone is being honored, a family has reason to gather.</p>

  <p>This social role is as important as its flavor.</p>

  <p>A large pot of caldereta is inherently communal. It is difficult to make a small, lonely serving. The dish assumes abundance. It assumes that people will come. It assumes that rice will be served in generous mounds and that conversation will continue long after the first plates are emptied.</p>

  <p>In this sense, caldereta participates in a deeply Filipino idea of food: that cooking is an act of care, and eating together is an act of belonging. A stew is not only nourishment. It is hospitality made visible.</p>

  <p>The Ilonggo table, like tables throughout the Philippines, is shaped by this ethic. Food is offered before guests can ask. Portions are enlarged for visitors. Leftovers are packed for those departing. To refuse food can feel almost like refusing affection.</p>

  <p>Caldereta fits this culture perfectly. It is rich, generous, and meant to be shared. Its very name recalls a cauldron, and a cauldron implies a crowd.</p>

  <h2>Colonization, Adaptation, and Ownership</h2>
  <p>It would be dishonest to tell the story of caldereta without acknowledging colonization.</p>

  <p>Spanish rule reshaped Philippine society in profound and often painful ways. It brought religion, political structures, forced labor, new hierarchies, and violence. It also brought ingredients, techniques, and dishes that became woven into local life. Food history cannot be separated from this larger history. Every tomato-based stew on a Filipino table exists within a legacy of empire.</p>

  <p>But colonization did not make Filipinos passive receivers of culture.</p>

  <p>Filipino cooks adapted. They substituted. They improvised. They used what was available, affordable, and meaningful to their communities. They changed lamb to goat, altered spices, added liver, adjusted textures, and made the dish answer to local appetites. Over generations, caldereta ceased to be simply a Spanish dish in the Philippines. It became Filipino.</p>

  <p>This is the difference between inheritance and imitation.</p>

  <p>To inherit a dish is to receive it, question it, reshape it, and pass it on with one’s own marks upon it. Filipino caldereta is not a museum replica of Spanish cooking. It is a child of encounter—formed by colonial history, but owned by the people who cooked it, fed their families with it, and made it part of their celebrations.</p>

  <p>The liver spread is perhaps the clearest symbol of this transformation. It is a modern convenience, but it also represents culinary pragmatism. Filipino cooks found a way to achieve depth and richness without depending on ingredients that were difficult to obtain. The result became so distinctive that many Filipinos now consider liver spread essential to caldereta’s identity.</p>

  <p>In that small tin of liver spread lies a larger truth: Filipino cuisine is not defined by purity. It is defined by creativity.</p>

  <h2>The Port as Palate</h2>
  <p>Iloilo’s role in this story is not incidental.</p>

  <p>A port is a place where boundaries blur. It is where foreign goods become local commodities, where languages mix, where people from different islands and countries meet. Iloilo’s harbor connected the region to Manila, to neighboring provinces, and eventually to global markets. Its openness to international trade in 1855 accelerated its transformation into a major commercial and cultural center.</p>

  <p>The port brought sugar, rice, textiles, and manufactured goods. But it also brought the conditions under which food cultures could circulate.</p>

  <p>A dish like caldereta could travel through such networks in many ways: through Spanish households and colonial institutions, through church gatherings and fiestas, through cooks employed in wealthy homes, through markets where new ingredients became available, through families who encountered the dish elsewhere and recreated it at home.</p>

  <p>We may never be able to trace one exact route from a Spanish kitchen to an Ilonggo pot. Food history rarely offers such clean lines. Recipes move like rumors, like songs, like seeds carried by wind and water. They change as they travel.</p>

  <p>What we can say is that Iloilo was precisely the kind of place where such movement could take root: a port, a trading center, a meeting point of land and sea, a community accustomed to receiving the world and making sense of it.</p>

  <p>The city’s food culture reflects this openness. Iloilo is known for La Paz Batchoy, Pancit Molo, chicken inasal, fresh seafood, and a broader culinary tradition shaped by both local abundance and outside influence. Its identity as the “food basket and rice granary” of the region is rooted in fertile land and productive seas, but its cuisine is also a product of exchange.</p>

  <p>Caldereta belongs in that larger Ilonggo story: not as a foreign guest, but as a naturalized member of the household.</p>

  <h2>The Pot as Archive</h2>
  <p>There is a reason food history feels so intimate.</p>

  <p>Official histories record laws, battles, treaties, and administrations. But family histories are often stored in kitchens. They live in the way a grandmother seasons adobo, in the particular order in which a mother adds vegetables, in the amount of chili a household considers normal, in the pot that has been used for decades and carries the faint memory of every meal it has held.</p>

  <p>A caldereta pot is an archive.</p>

  <p>It records scarcity and celebration. It records the movement of ingredients across oceans. It records the practical decisions of cooks who had to feed many people with what they had. It records the preferences of families, the traditions of towns, and the quiet persistence of recipes passed from one generation to the next.</p>

  <p>When someone today stirs caldereta for a gathering, they are not merely following a recipe. They are participating in a chain of hands: Spanish cooks who made caldereta in Iberia, colonial-era cooks who encountered and adapted it in the Philippines, Ilonggo families who made it their own, and present-day cooks who continue to serve it at tables where memory and appetite meet.</p>

  <p>This does not mean every pot of caldereta tastes the same. It should not.</p>

  <p>One household may prefer goat, fiery and rustic. Another may use beef, tender and mild. Some add olives; others do not. Some use fresh liver; others swear by liver spread. Some make it mildly sweet; others want the chili to announce itself immediately. These variations are not errors. They are dialects of the same culinary language.</p>

  <p>Food becomes heritage not because it remains identical, but because it remains meaningful.</p>

  <h2>The Taste of Continuity</h2>
  <p>To eat caldereta is to taste continuity.</p>

  <p>The tomato recalls global exchange. The garlic and onion recall the everyday grammar of Filipino sautéing. The goat or beef recalls local adaptation and economic reality. The liver spread recalls Filipino ingenuity. The chili recalls heat, celebration, and the Filipino love of flavor that refuses blandness. The rice beside it recalls the landscape that made the meal complete.</p>

  <p>Nothing on the plate is innocent of history. Yet nothing on the plate is merely history either.</p>

  <p>Caldereta is also dinner. It is comfort after a long day. It is the smell that fills a house when relatives are expected. It is the dish someone requests on a birthday. It is the food brought to a wake, a reunion, a fiesta, a simple Sunday gathering.</p>

  <p>Its power lies in this double life: it is both historical and ordinary, both colonial inheritance and family possession, both a story of empire and a story of love.</p>

  <p>May this film remind us that our food carries memory.</p>

  <p>Every time we stir a caldero of caldereta for a gathering, we are continuing a centuries-old story of colonization, adaptation, and pride. We are acknowledging that our cuisine was shaped by forces beyond our control, while also affirming that Filipinos transformed those forces into something deeply our own.</p>

  <p>And in that story, Iloilo has a central role: as a port, a powerhouse, and a home where history simmers, ladle by ladle, into the present.</p>

  <p>The galleons are gone. The old fires have changed. The pots are different now—steel instead of clay, gas instead of wood, kitchen counters instead of open hearths. But the stew remains.</p>

  <p>Garlic still sizzles.</p>

  <p>Onions still soften.</p>

  <p>Tomatoes still melt into oil.</p>

  <p>Meat still browns.</p>

  <p>Steam still rises in that telltale red fog.</p>

  <p>And somewhere in Iloilo, a pot is being stirred—not only to feed the people at the table, but to keep alive the long, complicated, delicious story of who we are.</p>
